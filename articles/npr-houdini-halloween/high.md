# 100 Years Later, Harry Houdini's Death Still Haunts Detroit

This Halloween marks 100 years since the death of the great escape man Harry Houdini.

He earned his name by freeing himself from traps that seemed impossible for a normal man to survive. But Houdini couldn't escape the end waiting for him in Detroit on Oct. 31, 1926 — and his spirit still haunts the Motor City.

Houdini performed in Detroit 19 times between the early days of his career in 1894 and the final show of his life in 1926. His path took him from the bottom of the show list to worldwide fame.

He escaped a life of no one knowing his name by declaring no set of handcuffs or jail could hold him. He hung head-down from office buildings in a straitjacket, freely pulling it off in full view of the crowds below. Sometimes the buildings housed a local newspaper, ensuring free ads for Houdini's next stage shows.

In 1906, Houdini jumped about seven metres from Detroit's Belle Isle Bridge into the cold river below, bound with two sets of police handcuffs. He came out safe, but the legend grew: the escape man had jumped through a hole in the ice on the river, the story went, finding air to breathe before finally coming up.

Reports from the time show the river was not really frozen. But movies decades later showed it that way anyway.

As his fame grew, and other magicians began copying his escapes, Houdini raised the stakes, piling one ever more death-defying feat on top of another. By 1914 he had unveiled his signature stunt, the Water Torture Cell — a coffin-sized box filled with water.

Houdini was lowered into it head first, his ankles and the top of the box locked. A curtain was raised to hide his method of escape from the audience.

Even Dorothy Young, who travelled with Houdini as a stage helper at 17, had goosebumps every time. "Mrs. [Houdini] always stood in the wings because they had a secret code in case he couldn't get out," Young said in a 2004 radio interview. "I always watched because it was very exciting!" She was the last surviving person to have worked with Houdini before she, too, died in 2011.

A few years before she died, Young remembered how many movies had made the death story bigger than it was. He did not, as at least one movie depicts, dramatically struggle to escape the torture cell.

Houdini's death likely came from something that happened just before he reached Detroit. Houdini often said, proud and loud, that he could survive any hit to the stomach. In his dressing room at a show in Montreal, a college wrestler took the challenge and hit him several times before Houdini could make his stomach hard.

"One time he showed me that he had a way of doing it," Young said. "And to think those boys didn't give him a chance to get himself protected."

Houdini was in terrible pain, and doctors told him to rest. But he would not cancel his shows in Detroit, already planned. He was under financial pressure to fulfill his contract, according to Jeremy Dimick of the Detroit Historical Museum.

Dimick said Houdini only made it through one performance. "He doesn't have the strength to put his makeup on before the show, and he's almost falling down during the breaks," Dimick said. "And then he has to be carried from the Garrick Theater back to his hotel."

Houdini was taken to a hospital, where doctors found his appendix had burst. Several days and two surgeries later, on Halloween, Houdini breathed his last.

Now the theater, the hotel, even the hospital are gone. The Detroit funeral home that embalmed him became a girls' art school, which also eventually shut its doors. But two decades ago, the school's head, Juanita Hammond, would take visitors up creaky stairs to a locked room with a sloped floor, once used to drain fluids from the dead.

"Houdini was laid out here by the Hamilton Funeral Home, in this room here," Hammond said on a 2004 tour. Some students worried the great man's spirit never left the school. "Some say, 'I'm not going back to the kitchen because I might see Houdini,'" she said.

The escape artist's wife, Bess Houdini, spent years holding séance after séance, searching for her husband's ghost — though Houdini himself had spent his final years showing everyone that psychics he called fakes were preying on sad families. At a séance on Halloween ten years after his death, Bess finally said good night to Harry — and played the scene again in a movie years later.

"And you remember what he always said about these things? That it was impossible for the dead to return," she said. "This proves he was right. For if it would have been possible, I would have had some sign from him in the past 10 years."

That has not stopped others from trying. At the American Museum of Magic in Marshall, Michigan, about 100 miles west of Detroit, John Sherwood knocked on an old metal can from Houdini's own workshop — a can that has sat at the center of several séances. "That is the Houdini milk can," Sherwood said. "Houdini would jump into this while it was filled with warm water, and he would be locked inside with six Mastodon locks."

In reality, Houdini would have escaped long before, reading a newspaper backstage while the tension grew. Finally, with the crowd's worry at its highest, his helpers would throw water on him — he had dried off by then — and the master would step out, breathing hard like an actor.

Sherwood said some séances tried to call the great man back "by speaking to Houdini's Jewish roots — placing a lox and bagel sandwich into the milk can and inviting his spirit to take a bite." He is holding another séance this Halloween. Past attempts failed, but the 100th anniversary of Houdini's death might be the perfect time for him to appear — if only to honor his love of a good show.

"I've always said that the greatest publicity trick Harry Houdini ever pulled off was dying on Halloween, because after that the stories about him grew by leaps and bounds," he said.

It has also raised darker questions: Was Houdini's death paper changed to make it look as if he died on Halloween? Was the wrestler who hit him a hired killer, sent by angry mind readers? In some ways Houdini's death mirrors the tricks he performed in life — wrapped in mystery.
