# Scammers Used AI to Clone Her Daughter's Voice and Fake a Kidnapping

Scammers can lift voice samples from videos people post on platforms like Instagram and TikTok. They then use the copies to convince family members that a loved one is in danger, and talk them out of thousands of dollars in the process.

## The Call

Deborah Del Mastro, a mother in Martinez, California, received a call from an unknown number one morning in May. The man on the line said a Mexican crime group had taken her 37-year-old daughter. Then he played what sounded like her daughter crying for help.

"It was my daughter's voice in a full panic, scared, telling me 'I love you, mom, I'm so sorry, I'm so scared' — and then they just cut it off," Del Mastro told ABC7 News.

"The guy is barking orders, 'don't speak,' 'is there someone there with you?' 'Don't speak,' 'go and get dressed and get out,' 'do this now, it has to happen now,'" she said.

Believing her daughter's life was on the line, Del Mastro followed the caller's instructions for five hours. She sent $5,400 to Mexico from several places and was at last told her daughter would be set free at a food store. When she got there and couldn't find her, she called her daughter directly. Her daughter had been at work the entire time.

Martinez police are investigating, but Del Mastro told ABC7 she doesn't expect to get the money back.

## Why the Brain Falls for It

Kimberly Hyun, a marketing teacher at the University of Cincinnati who studies how a person's voice shapes what people choose to believe, told Fortune these scams work because of how the brain handles sound. Her research finds that when a voice sounds like someone we know, we tend to drop our defenses and become readier to believe what we hear. A family member's voice "can actually trick you through trust," Hyun explains.

It's not just older people falling for these tricks. Gallup found no difference in victim numbers by age, and experienced professionals have been fooled as well. In 2024, a finance worker at an international company in Hong Kong paid out HK$200 million — about $25.6 million — after cheats used deepfakes to play the company's chief financial officer on a video call. The worker first thought it might be a trick email, but set his doubts aside, because everyone on the call looked and sounded like colleagues he knew. All of them were fakes.

## The Tools Are Easy to Find

In March 2025, Consumer Reports published a study of six popular AI voice-copying products. It found that four of them — ElevenLabs, Speechify, PlayHT and Lovo — let researchers easily copy a voice from public audio. Those four companies used no technical check to confirm the speaker had agreed; users only had to tick a box saying they had the legal right. The other two, Descript and Resemble AI, had taken steps to make secret copying harder.

"There are basic steps companies can take to make it harder to copy someone's voice without their knowledge — but some companies aren't taking them," said Grace Gedye, a policy analyst at Consumer Reports. Fortune has reported on voice-copying tricks since 2023, and researchers warn the fakes have now crossed a line: they can no longer be told apart from real voices.

## The Defense: A Code Word

Because a familiar voice is no longer proof of identity, you need a second way to confirm it's really your loved one. Hyun's solution: agree on a code word that only the two of you know. "You really need to create a safe code with your loved ones. Make sure that they're real," Hyun said.

Otherwise, stick to the usual rules: be careful with calls from numbers you don't know, and if someone claims a family member is in trouble, don't stay on the line. "You just need to hang up and just call your family member again," Hyun advises. If it happens to you, report it to the U.S. trade watchdog and the FBI's internet crime office — only 13% of victims in the Gallup survey ever reported the crime.
