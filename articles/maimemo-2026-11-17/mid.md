# The Visitor

The visitor came from overseas, which in this house means one thing: Grandma makes a plan and the plan has a soup in it. He was an old colleague of mine, a man I had worked with for six years through a screen and had met exactly twice, and he had a single free day in the city before his flight home. I offered to show him around. My wife offered lunch. Grandma, hearing the word overseas, took the whole arrangement away from both of us and returned it in writing, with times.

The snow was still on the ground from Sunday, which made the city look like a photograph of itself and made every set of stairs a negotiation. We walked. That was the plan: walking, and soup, and one museum, and no shopping, because a man who has been on a plane for eleven hours does not want to be shown a mall. He was, it turned out, the most patient guest in the world. He stopped in front of the gate of the little park and looked at it for a long time and said, in careful English, that his own city had a gate exactly like it, built in the same year, by the same handbook. Then he took a photograph of it, one photograph, and put the phone away, and did not take another all day.

Bobby was suspicious at first. A stranger in the flat is a serious event when you are fifteen months old, and he handled it the way he handles all serious events: he stood still, held the tiger, and stared. The visitor did the correct thing, which was nothing. He sat on the floor, at the boy's level, and waited. By the second hour Bobby had brought him the elephant. By the third hour he was sitting on the visitor's knee, showing him the page about the bear in the mountain book, and the visitor was making the correct sounds of interest, and my son had decided that the whole business of strangers was, on the whole, worth it.

The soup was the main dish, the winter one, and the visitor ate two bowls of it and asked, with real curiosity, what was in it. Grandma gave him the answer she gives everyone, which is that the recipe is not a recipe, and then, because he was a guest and had asked nicely, she gave him the actual answer, which took eleven minutes and included three ingredients he had never heard of. He wrote them down on a napkin. He put the napkin in his briefcase. I have never seen a napkin enter a briefcase with more ceremony.

At two we went to the museum, where I discovered that my six years of working with this man had taught me nothing about how he looks at a painting. He looks for a very long time and then says nothing. A tour guide passed us twice with a group, saying things about the painter that were, I am fairly sure, wrong, and the visitor listened to both passes with the same attention he gave the paintings. Afterwards I asked him why. "Because he is wrong in an interesting way," he said. "That is a kind of information."

The nightmare started at nine, in the flat, on the laptop, in the way these things always start: a message that says, in effect, everything is fine, and a graph that says otherwise. The console was showing an animation that had been frozen mid-spin for eleven minutes. The scope of the problem was, at first, a single account. Then it was four. Then it was a variable I did not remember writing, in a function whose name I did not recognise, and I had the terrible feeling that comes with recognising your own handwriting on a document you have no memory of signing.

Debugging at nine in the evening is a different sport from debugging at eleven in the morning. The house is quiet, the boy is asleep, and there is nobody to perform competence for. I put on the headphones, opened the old trade-off in my head — speed, or certainty — and chose certainty, which is slower and has never once regretted me. By ten I had found the hole: a boolean that had been true since the spring, quietly, in a place where true meant "give everybody the new version" and where the new version had, since Sunday, been broken for anyone arriving from overseas.

That word again. Overseas. The visitor's own ticket had been bought with that boolean, and it had worked, and it had not. There is a particular kind of embarrassment reserved for the moment when you discover that the bug is not in the code you wrote but in the assumption you made, and the assumption was: everyone is nearby. The fix took four minutes. The explanation, which I wrote into the ticket at ten past eleven, took longer, and ended with a sentence my colleague would have appreciated: we built for one-way trips and then forgot that a one-way trip is still a land somewhere.

I closed the laptop at midnight and did the small tour of the flat that I do after these things. The router's light, steady. The server, humming. The kitchen guard, counting doors, reporting no anomaly, and a battery that has now survived a week of cold and one fever and one nightmare, and has an opinion about none of it. The refrigerator held its four degrees, and I stood in front of it for a minute with the door open, in the way the fridge is always accused of, and looked at nothing, and let the four degrees do their small work on my neck.

Bobby woke at three, not from a nightmare — he does not have those yet, or has them and forgets them within a minute, which is the only advantage of being fifteen months old. He woke because he was thirsty and because he wanted to know, briefly, that the flat was still a flat. I carried him to the window. The snow had turned to a thin crust and the street below was empty and blue and the whole city looked like the inside of a computer that had just been switched off. He put one hand on the glass and said the word he says for everything that is outside and far away, and then he put his head back on my shoulder and went to sleep, and I stood there a while longer, on the wrong side of the glass, for once, and glad of it.

In the morning the visitor's flight was on time, which for a flight in November is a kind of miracle, and I did not go to the airport, because he asked me not to. He had checked in from the platform of the express train with a photograph of his coffee, and the coffee was a brand he did not recognise, and he drank it anyway, which took more courage than the crossing. I sent him a picture of the boy eating his yogurt, and he sent back a picture of a gate ten hours away. We said goodbye at the door of the flat, in the correct way for people who have worked together for six years and met twice: briefly, with a small joke about the boolean, and a promise to be faster next time. He left with his briefcase, and the napkin in it, and the photograph of a gate that is exactly like our gate, in a city ten hours away, built in the same year by the same handbook.

The arrangements held. The soup was finished at lunch. The encyclopedia stayed at its letter. The tutorial I owe nobody got one more paragraph, written in the morning, in the good light, which is when it should have been written all along. Consistency of that kind is not a personality, as I said on Sunday, and it is not a talent either; it is a decision you make again every morning, and some mornings the decision is easy because the light is good and the boy is well and the snow has stopped falling on everyone, nearby and overseas alike.

That night I did the accounts of the visit, the way I do, and this is what they said. The secretary of this family had run the whole day from a piece of paper, and had not once had to raise her voice; she complained only about the price of the museum tickets, and she complained about it to a stranger, which is her highest compliment. The visitor ate no snacks, drank two pots of tea, and left a pair of socks in the bathroom, which I found later, and which I am not going to embarrass him about. The theory he offered about our gate was better than mine. There was one evaluation I did not expect: he said the flat smelled of soup and medicine in equal parts, and that this was, quote, further evidence that he had come to the right house. The purple of the sky at four was the same purple as yesterday, and the encyclopedia stayed on its shelf, and the cream was served at its hour, and the prescription shelf was full, and the constraint of one small scoop held, and the bias of this house toward feeding people held with it. The frustration of the bug had gone by then. The nightmare had been answered with a morning, the way they always are.

There were details I keep instead of conclusions. The soup, which the visitor called delicious twice, had gone sour by Friday, and we ate it anyway on Thursday, and it was worth it. The elevator in his hotel was out for two hours and he used the stairs without mentioning it until dinner, and then only to say that he had not been in such good condition in years. He had no reason to subscribe to anything here, and he said so, and then he asked me to send him the soup recipe, which required a fitting of its own: eleven minutes of Grandma talking and me translating. On his desk at home there is, he told me, a shelf of things people have given him from foreign cities, and the napkin is going on it. He recalled, unprompted, a bug we had fixed together in 2019, and the exact shape of the hole in it, and I had to sit down. And when we were done, at the door, he said the sentence I have been thinking about since: that the hardest part of working overseas is not the distance, it is that you never see the ordinary Tuesday, and the ordinary Tuesday is where the work actually lives.

The flat, on the Tuesday of all this, was still recovering from Sunday, in small ways that I kept noticing. The stove was on before seven, because the bread would not warm itself and the boy would not wait. He got up early, in the way he does when the weather has changed, wearing his pants and one sock, and stood behind me at the library shelf asking for the mountain book with a gesture that has become, by now, almost a word. The river at the end of the street was high with meltwater. There was a concert on the radio at four that we did not listen to, because the visitor was talking, and it did not depress anybody. The snow by then was a crust on the roofs, the kind that makes a city look slept-in. The fever had gone and left behind one habit: he likes to be carried to the bed at night now and read to with the lights low. The gate of the little park was open again. The smell of the flat by evening was soup, and soap, and snow coming in on somebody's boots. Nobody complained about that.

I keep a note of these days the way the family secretary keeps hers, on paper, in the back of the same book. What went into it this week: the visitor's napkin recipe; the boolean that was true since spring; the saucepan that has to be watched or it burns; the boy bringing the elephant to a stranger on purpose; the fridge door left open while I stood there, and the small guilt of that, and the small mercy of a thing that does not hold a grudge. In the margin, in pencil: further work needed on the one-way assumption. In ink, underlined twice: medicine shelf restocked. The prescription for the household, this week, is boring and I am glad of it: sleep early, eat soup, keep the flat warm.

Two things I am still turning over. The first is a theory of Grandma's that I have decided to adopt as my own: that a house is kept by its snacks, not its meals, because a meal is an event and a snack is a habit, and habits are what people remember about a house they visited once. She has a stake in this theory, having supplied both from a bag at nine in the morning, and she is not wrong. The second is the evaluation the visitor left me with at the door: that everything here works, in a way that is not efficient but is, in his word, kind. I have written that down too, because a stranger's evaluation of your refrigerator, your secretary, your soup and your Tuesday is worth more than your own, and I intend to complain about nothing for at least a week, as a test of the theory.

The last thing, and then I will stop. At eleven, before bed, I went around the flat doing what I do at the end of a day like that one. The battery on the balcony sensor had survived the cold snap and reported nothing interesting, which is the report I want. The cream was one scoop, served at four, and the constraint of the house was honoured without discussion, and the consistency of that small ceremony is the only tutorial this family has ever needed. The foreign weather outside had gone quiet. Nothing about the evening frustrated me, and I noticed that, because I usually do not notice. My bias, which I am told I have, is toward believing that a day without incident is a day wasted; the visitor's kind evaluation of our ordinary Tuesday is the counter-argument, and I am keeping it where I keep the napkin recipe. Underwear in the drawer, socks in the wash, the leak in the bathroom ceiling still there and still small, and the encyclopedia at M, and the boy asleep, and the flat warm, and the fridge keeping its four degrees like a promise made to a stranger who turned out to be a friend.
