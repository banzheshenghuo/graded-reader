# Business as a Liberal Art? Some High Schools Say Yes

Sitting in a semicircle around their teacher, Gerri Kimble, students at Hoover High School near Birmingham, Alabama, discuss a classic teenage question: what to do with their part-time job pay. These students say they're buying sweet drinks, putting gas in their cars — and investing.

"I put it in index funds, like S&P and then QQQ and VOO," says junior Darshan Parmar. Kimble asks if his parents do the actual investing. Parmar says he does it himself. One student puts 70 percent in his Roth IRA. Another contributes to a 401(k). A third invests directly in stocks. "Oh, you watch the markets?" Kimble asks. "Love it."

These students are among the first in the country to take a newly created Advanced Placement Business with Personal Finance course. The class is offered for the first time this year at more than 1,500 high schools across the country.

AP courses are college-level classes offered in high school. Enrolled students can take an exam to earn college credit. Typically, these credits are for traditional subjects like English, history and chemistry. Students in Hoover's AP business class, however, get lessons on management and finance, including the outside factors that can make or break a business: political, economic, social, technological, environmental and legal.

"It's a foundational piece of business that we're learning," says senior Bramley Williams.

"Business is always learning to adapt," says David Coleman, CEO of the College Board, the organization that creates AP courses and runs the SAT. It created this business course because students said in AP surveys that they want to turn their ideas into jobs. The class is designed for students drawn to a more practical curriculum than a more theoretical subject such as AP economics, which is still offered at schools like this one.

Yet Coleman considers business a new part of the liberal arts, as important for preparing students for the world as core areas of study like math and history. This kind of teaching that mixes the practical with the theoretical, he says, "could not be more useful today."

The AP course is part of a trend toward more money-and-finance curriculum in U.S. high schools. Thirty-nine states this year require a personal finance class to graduate — four more than two years ago, according to the Council for Economic Education.

Hoover High School, in a rich Birmingham suburb, often feels more like a college: wide green spaces and a student body just under 3,000, the largest high school in Alabama. Many students in the class have college dreams, and the AP course is more challenging — AP classes are often weighted higher, which can lift grade point averages. "This AP course is a way for me to raise my GPA and learn more about a topic that I love," says Damola Kukoyi. Past the AP class, Hoover offers a four-year business academy with classes such as accounting and starting your own company.

Does financial education actually work? A Federal Reserve paper from a few years ago found families in states requiring it made better money decisions: they were more likely to use pandemic stimulus checks to pay down credit card balances and refinance their mortgages while rates were low. "When we teach young people to use money in a smart way, they're really good at it," says Jennifer Steele, a professor of education at American University.

Hoover senior Rachel Deschaine, in the course, says general business knowledge matters because it teaches how people and companies deal with the world in money terms. Her father owned a trucking company; her brother is majoring in economics. She sees the lessons play out at the dinner table. "There's a business in everything," she says. "If you want to understand how it works, it really opens up the opportunities to a person."
