# Business as a Liberal Art? Some High Schools Say Yes

At Hoover High School near Birmingham, Alabama, students sit in a half circle around their teacher and discuss a teenage question: what to do with part-time job pay.

"I put it in index funds," says one. Another student puts 70 percent into a retirement account. A third invests directly in stocks. "Oh, you watch the markets?" the teacher asks. "Love it."

## A New AP Course

These students are among the first in the country to take a new AP Business with Personal Finance course, offered this year at more than 1,500 high schools. AP courses are college-level classes; students can earn college credit through an exam. Usually the subjects are traditional ones — English, history, chemistry.

This new course teaches management and money instead, including the outside forces that can make or break a business: politics, the economy, society, technology and law. "It's a base piece of business that we're learning," says one student in his last year.

"Business is the constant learning of adaptation," says David Coleman, head of the College Board, the organization that runs AP courses and the SAT. It created the course because students said in surveys they want to turn their ideas into jobs — and it's designed for those who prefer something more practical than a theoretical subject like AP economics.

Still, Coleman considers business a new part of a well-rounded education — as important for the world as math or history. Mixing the practical with the theoretical, he says, "could not be more useful today."

## A Growing Wave

The course is part of a trend: this year, 39 states require a class about money to graduate — four more than two years ago, says the Council for Economic Education.

Does it work? A Federal Reserve paper found families in states with money classes made better money choices — during the sick years they were more likely to use stimulus checks to pay down card debt. "When we teach young people to use money in a smart way, they're really good at it," says education professor Jennifer Steele.

Students see the value too. "There's a business in everything," says Rachel Deschaine, whose father ran a truck company. "If you're willing to understand how it works, it really opens up the opportunities to a person."
