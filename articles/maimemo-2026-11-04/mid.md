# The Haircut

Bobby's hair had reached the stage where strangers smiled at him twice: once for the boy, once for the hair. Two hairs, at breakfast, brushed his breakfast. That afternoon, the family secretary put a line in the arrangements book — Wednesday: haircut — and by four o'clock the three of us were walking to the barbershop on the corner, past two foreign restaurants and a phone shop, the one with the striped pole and the barber who has cut my hair since it was long enough to cut. His shop is a local landmark of small talk, two chairs, one mirror with a crack like a river, and a radio that plays songs from a foreign country nobody in the shop has ever visited. The briefcase had no part in this trip and stayed home, honest for once, its one job being to hold the door open for nobody, and the kitchen guard was left in charge, with its battery full and its instructions short: count the doors, hold the cold.

The barber, Mr. Yan, greeted Bobby first, as is correct. "First haircut," he said, more statement than question, because the hair had said it already. He wrapped Bobby in a purple cape, which Bobby liked completely, being the color of his swim cap and, therefore, an old friend. Foreign barbershops, I am told, give candy; Mr. Yan gives dignity, which lasts longer. What followed was a small wonder of patience: scissors going snip around one small head, a head that turned, reached, grabbed, and now and then complained, while Mr. Yan moved like a man doing origami in a wind, and the family secretary watched from a chair by the wall, taking no notes, which from her is the highest form of trust. He has three grandsons. It shows.

I sat in the second chair and watched, and for once my bias against being watched while I work had nothing to feed on. Mr. Yan cut my hair and gave me, free of charge, an evaluation of the modern world: everything is a subscription now, he said, even water, even news, even the purple stripes on his pole, which he repainted himself, every spring, brighter on purpose. His shop has no app, no queue number, no star card; consistency of a kind you can set a watch by. He complained, with great happiness, about this exact thing, the way other men complain about football, and the complaint was so steady that it had become a service.

Halfway through, Bobby found the mirror. He met the other baby in it, the one with the bat ears — wait, the ears were gone, cut off with the hair — and he studied this stranger for a long time, leaning, checking. Then he looked at me, then at the mirror, then at me, working it out. The answer was: still Daddy. The mirror had raised one anomaly and resolved it in under a minute, which is better than most of my troubles at work. He relaxed and let the scissors finish.

When it was done, Mr. Yan turned the chair toward the mirror like a man unveiling a statue, and brushed the last hair off Bobby's neck with a brush softer than anything we own at home. The evaluation was delivered by Bobby himself, in his own language, at volume, to the shop, the street, and the century: one long sentence, concluded by applause for himself, a consistency of taste that runs in the family. A small haircut fee, paid in claps.

The haircut, the barber said, takes about a year to grow back. The hair does not frustrate anybody with its speed; it simply grows, the way the queue of review words grows, the way Wednesday becomes Thursday. My own hair, newly neat, will frustrate me again in three weeks, and the tutorial for that problem, I am told by Mr. Yan every single time, is to come back sooner. I never learn, which frustrates Mr. Yan not at all. Some tutorials are written to be ignored; the barber's consistency in telling me, every single time, is the reliable part.

At home, the before-and-after was examined by the harshest possible jury: the grandmother. She held his face in both hands, turned it left, turned it right, checked the neck, checked the ears, and delivered the verdict with a voice almost too small to hear: "He looks like a boy now. Before, he looked like my baby." Then she complained, briefly, that barbers take too much, and compensated the barber in advance by telling two neighbors about him, and now both of them want the purple cloth treatment, whatever that means. The constraint of her love is that it never computes; it just re-does the math, the old way, and gets a different answer, and keeps both. Her bias is permanent, her evaluation is final, and neither one has ever been wrong, by her accounting.

In the kitchen, the guard had counted our departure and return, two clean marks, no anomaly — the mirror incident had happened outside its jurisdiction — and the refrigerator inside it held four degrees with its flat, professional calm, while the new battery, on the balcony, hummed through the damp evening. The machine did not notice the haircut. The machine does not do hair. It logged a house that left at four and came back at five with a boy inside it, and if it could file an opinion, which it cannot, which is its charm, it would file: same house, smaller head, no action required.

At dinner the new haircut sat at the table and ate its shrimp with focus. He looks taller now, which is impossible, and thinner, which is the hair's opinion, and older, which is the grandmother's fear, and handsomer, which everyone agreed on, including the boy, who clapped. After dinner: one small scoop of cream, the standing constraint, his medicine of four o'clock every day, medicine he has never once refused, served this time with a new spoon because the old spoon had a scratch that only he could see, and he had complained about it, twice, at length, through the medium of pointing.

The encyclopedia has reached J, where the jungles are, and that page was the bedtime story, read twice, once for the facts and once for the sounds. Bobby, newly shorn and well fed, listened in his crib between the tiger and the rabbit, both of whom kept their hair, an unfair advantage nobody had thought to flag. The bias in this crib is against change; the encyclopedia, for its part, is biased toward filing everything; it is held by the smallest citizens, and it loses, every time, to the calendar, and they re-elect it anyway.

The evening audit ran after the bath, small and complete. The purple cloth, folded by Mr. Yan, came home in the bag, a souvenir of an hour. The encyclopedia, back on its shelf at J, gained a new reader's crease. The battery drawer was checked: two full, one empty, one honest. The refrigerator was thanked, out loud, by nobody, which is how it prefers. The arrangements for Thursday were confirmed in thirty seconds, the briefcase by the door was patted twice on the way past, its whole week already planned, and the night's one anomaly, a sock found behind the crib, was processed and closed. Cream: standing by. Medicine: taken. Constraint: in force. The house, in short, was a house, and the briefcase had watched all of it from the door, learning nothing, as is correct for a briefcase.

Tomorrow is Thursday, swim day, and the pool will get the new head first. The tutorial I owe my team is down to its last step, and my evaluation of that fact is cautious joy. The haircut will grow, the way all of it grows: slowly, at its own rate, without paperwork, one Wednesday at a time. And in the arrangements book, under next Wednesday, in pencil, in the secretary's hand, beside a note about cream and a note about the refrigerator's filter, the same word waits, patient as a barber: haircut. Again.
