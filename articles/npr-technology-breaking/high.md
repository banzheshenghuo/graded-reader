We had a routine switchover from an analog copper wire, switching it over to digital fiber. When we did the switchover, what we now know is that it started to create issues in our network, and it created instability. So within a few days, a rapid succession of tech problems disrupted the lives of millions of Americans. It is exposing how fragile our aging digital infrastructure is. But why is this happening all at once?

Let us ask Dmitri Alperovitch. He is the former chief technology officer of the security company CrowdStrike and founder of the Alperovitch Institute for Cybersecurity Studies at Johns Hopkins University.

At this point, is there any evidence that all these incidents are connected in any way or maybe are tied to cybercrime?

No. And I understand that people are very suspicious. Obviously, all of these events happening almost every day is very strange. But these things happen. And, you know, one of Murphy's laws, of course, is that — never attribute anything to malice that is adequately explained by incompetence. I think a lot of these things are happening because people are actually in the midst of a lot of updating right now. There is, of course, the AI threat. A lot of weak spots are being found by these AI models. And as a result, a lot of companies, a lot of organizations across the world are trying to fix these weak spots as quickly as possible. So we are going through this patch apocalypse right now. And sometimes when you are updating systems, we all know things can go wrong, and I think that is partly what is happening here.

So are we having this collision of our extreme reliance on technology, overtaxed, often aging systems that, as you said, periodically need updating? We know that cyberattacks are common. We know that rogue AI is a fear. Are we seeing the fragility of the system all coming together?

Well, these are all different systems. So there is no question that we rely on technology much more now, so when things go down, it causes much more inconvenience for us. You know, in the old days, it was easier to get into, perhaps, your bank and physically and withdraw money, and now we are relying much more on online banking and those sorts of things. So the impact to us is much greater, but these things have always happened. But I do think there is something to the idea that right now it is happening more because everyone is trying to update their systems before they can get hacked by these AI models.

I was going to say updates are a fact of life, but you are saying particular updates happening now because of the risks of AI that are becoming apparent — people trying to get ahead of that.

That is right. More weak spots have been found this year than the last 30 years combined. So that is as a result of AI looking at these software packages, identifying new weak spots. So enterprises, government agencies are right now completely flooded with reports that you need to update your systems because there is another serious weak spot that has just been found.

Could that mean that we are going through a rough patch right now, but it will be temporary? Or is this going to be an ongoing symptom of our modern age?

I think the next 12 to 18 months are going to be very rough not just because of the update cycles, but also because some people will not update in time. And of course, there will be more hacks as a result. But I think we will come out on the other end in a much safer way because a lot of the software will all get much more secure.

It sounds like a cat-and-mouse game in terms of technology and updates and bad actors looking for weak spots. So does that suggest that this is just going to be the ongoing game of cat-and-mouse that lasts forever?

Well, this has been the life in security for the last 40 years, and I do not think that is going away. As long as there are people out there with malicious intentions, whether it is nation-states or criminals or hacktivists, we are going to have problems. And, of course, the biggest vulnerability remains the user, not your computer system, because people fall for social engineering attacks or phishing scams. And that, I expect, will continue to be the case.

And people have weak passwords, and they might not use two-factor authentication. What, to give some practical advice for people, are we all just helpless victims of this? Or what basic things should people be doing to try to protect themselves?

Well, in terms of your own systems, yes, update it automatically. Make sure that you are fully fixed whenever a weak spot comes out. Make sure, as you said, you have two-factor authentication and strong passwords. But when it comes to this infrastructure going down, you know, we are all observers here and unfortunately sometimes victims because if your bank goes down, there is not a lot you can do.

When you looked at the FAA national ground stop, which we now know appears to have been related to an update happening in Arkansas where something did not go well and suddenly the whole system goes down, is there, in general, not a way to prevent small mistakes happening in isolated areas from crashing an entire system?

Well, usually there is. And this was a surprise — that there is sort of this one weakness in the system that could take the whole thing down. Usually, there is redundancy. Now, the problem is that a lot of these systems have gotten so complex with so many layers to them over the years that it is very, very difficult to understand where those points of problem might be. And hopefully this is one area where AI might actually help us to identify these problems ahead of time.

Many people are so focused on the negatives of AI and the fear that it will go rogue and cause chaos, but you are saying here is a reminder that it could help us in this situation.

Absolutely. I think it is going to do some amazing things for us in lots of different areas. But there is no question, like every technology, it comes with downsides.

It sounds to me like your industry has work for infinity, basically.

There is a lot of job security in cybersecurity.

And, in terms of an overall parting thought, is there any way that we as individuals can be trying to minimize the impact of this on our lives, whether it is reducing our dependency on tech, increasing our protections? What is your advice for those who want to throw their hands up in the air?

Everyone should focus on resiliency in their daily life. Always have some cash lying around just in case you cannot get your money withdrawn from an ATM for a period of time. You know, stockpile some water. Stockpile some groceries. I think those are prudent things to do in any scenario. Of course, there could be other catastrophes — weather-related, terrorism, etc. — that you want to be prepared for. So that is always a good thing to do.
