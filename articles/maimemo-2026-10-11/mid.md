# The Sunday Report

At seven on Sunday morning, my phone made one small sound. The kitchen guard had sent its first night report, and I read it in bed: milk, four degrees; the pills from her new prescription, six; all night, every hour, from the sensor inside the refrigerator and the warmer one on the balcony. One line did not fit. At 2:14 in the morning, the temperature inside Grandma's refrigerator jumped to eleven degrees, stayed there for ninety seconds, and fell back to six.

Anomaly. The word sat in my head before my eyes were open. A broken door? A dying sensor? A power cut? I lay still and listened to the house. The refrigerator in our own kitchen hummed its usual note, and outside a bird was already at work.

I called Grandma at eight. She answered on the second ring, awake and dressed, because Sunday has its rules.

"Did you open your refrigerator at two in the morning?"

There was a pause with a little guilt in it. "I woke up and could not sleep," she said. "So I got up, checked that the pills from my prescription were really there, and had a small yogurt. Then I went back to bed." Another pause. "Does your machine report that too?"

"It reports everything," I said. "Unfortunately, it cannot yet tell the difference between a broken door and a grandmother with a midnight itch." The jump had frustrated me for a full hour that morning, and the whole answer was a yogurt.

After breakfast I sat down and made the report better, the way a secretary would build a file: one page, clean lines, nothing extra. The night numbers now arrive as a single sheet. Time, temperature, and a red mark for anything strange. Integers only, no long decimals, because Grandma reads numbers the way she once typed letters, exactly. Consistency of format, she calls it. Her vision for the report was one page and no colors, and her rule stands. My approach was to give her less, not more.

The weekly evaluation looked healthy: good throughput, one report a minute, no lost messages, the two sensors still agreeing well within the margin, which is consistency enough for a kitchen. The program runs on the same framework as my day job, and it has one specific job. The schema had not changed. But the small program that renders the page got a new feature: it merges the quiet night hours into one flat line, so a jump like the 2:14 one now stands up like a raised hand, three pixels high. One line of input, one page of output. The little agent that reads the sensors needed one small bundle of fixes and a cup of my patience, then it worked. I changed a variable, upgraded the program from version one to version two, and pushed it all to the repository through her home router. The upgrade took an hour.

Two more changes. The program now keeps its credentials in one file instead of three, each sensor keeps its tag, and the data load stays light: one night is a short vector of sixty numbers, and a whole month consists of sixty each and still fits next to one photo. Protocol one was fine but quiet, and its proxy still says no to strangers; protocol two sends her phone a short message when something is wrong. The only bottleneck left is her printer, which works when it wants to. The page now awaits night number two, straightforward and patient, like a dog by the door.

Bobby, meanwhile, had been awake since six. He is working on four teeth at once, and the newest one has made him busy and a little angry, a small bear in the mornings. The tooth frustrates him more than it hurts. Teeth are a pain; the spoon is his answer. He chewed it, waved it, and hit me with it, which is his way of saying good morning. The kitchen smelled of rice porridge, and he smelled it too and shouted something that was almost a word. While the porridge cooked, he gave a concert on his toy instruments. Three instruments is still the whole band, and the new tune was mostly drum, no tune at all, just joy. I washed his spoon at the sink and gave it back. In this family, a spoon means stay.

In the late morning we walked to the local market. Grandma has her brands and will not change them, so we went to the honey man first, who is famous on that street. His bees live on the hill by the park, and he named their source without thinking: the flowers behind the library. Bobby watched the honey jars the way I watch code. At the cheese counter the line was long, and it became the bottleneck of the morning. I searched two shelves for the small blue box of the tea she likes, and found it right behind the rice. When Bobby found the sample table a minute later, his happy shout gave him away, and the honey man laughed and let him try the sweetest piece. He wore a shirt with long sleeves; by noon, one sleeve was wet to the shoulder. At the freezer he stopped and pointed, and it took real courage to tell him no. The freezer is the opposite of an oven, and both are magic to him. At home I rinsed the fruit while he watched, then we dressed for the park.

In the afternoon the weather was kind, so we walked to the park, the toy rabbit riding in the bag with its ears out and Bobby in his sun hat. Bobby jumped in my arms at the gate. I put him in the baby swing and pushed, and his shoes pointed every way but forward. Each push of the swing bought me one more second of his complete attention. When the rabbit came out, he chased it across the grass until they both needed a rest. He lost one sock under the swing, as always; we looked it over once and let it go. He bumped his forehead against mine on purpose, twice, because once is never enough for a joke.

At six we walked to the restaurant, an old corner landmark famous for its fish. Grandma's arrangements held: her reservation was for six, and we were all there before her. The lobby was warm and full of the smell of soup. The man who took our coats had a foreign accent, soft and musical, and Bobby turned his whole head at every vowel. Later Bobby tried the sounds himself, quietly, and gave himself away laughing.

"Please, this way," said the man, and we followed him in.

Grandma read the menu like a report. "The prices went up again," she said. "Inflation. Every year, the same soup, one more yuan." She said she did not want to complain, and then complained, happily, for a minute. It was her kind of song.

My brother the nurse came late and hungry, with no delay at the hospital for once. He asked about the kitchen guard, and I told them the story of 2:14. He laughed first. "So it learns nothing," he said. "It just watches."

"It is not neural," I said. "There is no unsupervised learning in it, no magic. Watching a refrigerator is boring work; that is why a machine should have it. My approach is the honest one. It counts."

"The best part," said Grandma, "is that it works unsupervised, like a good secretary. Moreover, it never sleeps and never eats my yogurt."

"What an accent that man has," she added, nodding toward the door. "He could read a phone book out loud, and I would pay to listen."

The explanation of the anomaly, when I gave it, was straightforward: a grandmother, an itch, and a yogurt. In the context of a family dinner, that is not a problem, that is a Sunday.

The food came. The shrimp arrived with their heads on, which my brother enjoys and I do not; unfortunately for the fish, he was hungry. Grandma declared the fish delicious, twice, to be sure. Bobby had his first ice cream, one small spoon of it. He made a sour face, held it, thought about his short life, swallowed, and asked for more. He pointed at it and shouted; I translated: yes. His fork did heavy work, and he bumped his cup with both hands, which we all agreed was a toast. Later came a dessert made of cream, and a bowl of yogurt for him, which he ate like a man paying off a debt.

We passed the phone around the table with the photos from the trip: the hotel, the elevator mirror where Bobby met his double, the sightseeing boat in the foreign city, my nose still pink with sunburn in every third picture. My brother posted the one of Bobby and the ice cream, and it got more likes in an hour than my last two years of code.

The word "departure" is too big for a family dinner, but my brother used it anyway, because he had a night shift. He excused himself first, and Bobby waved and said "Daddy" to every coat at the door, which embarrassed nobody and fixed nothing. No taxi, no fare tonight; we walked home through the wet street. Bobby was carried away, sleepy and gone, before his shoes were off. He did not wake when we came in, too tired even for the door.

The house was quiet. Inside our own refrigerator, the light clicked on for a moment and went out, with no sensor to tell anyone. The old kitchen sink ran once and stopped, the same water music it has played for years. I kept the boy on my shoulder and stood in the dark a minute longer than I needed to.

Tomorrow the report comes again, one page, sixty numbers. Grandma will read it with her coffee and make her small marks. If the temperature jumps at 2:14, I will check the kitchen first and the data second, because by now I know which of them will be standing by the open door, eating yogurt.
