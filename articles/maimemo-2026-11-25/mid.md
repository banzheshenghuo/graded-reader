# Room 14

Rosa had worked the night desk at the Bellview for eight years, and she could tell what a guest needed before the guest reached the counter. Eight years had done that to her without asking, and she never wrote it on any form.

The Bellview had nine rooms, one lift that worked, and a small refrigerator in the back office that held the milk for the morning tray. At eleven Rosa did the round she had done since her first week. Front door, back door, the refrigerator in the back office, then the small battery in the smoke alarm over the stairs, which she tested on the first of every month whether it complained or not. She kept the old ones in a drawer, which was not required and which she could not explain. A smoke alarm with a dead battery was the quiet nightmare of the job, and she had thought about it at three in the morning more than once, and the nightmare never quite went away, though the alarm itself would frustrate her less than the owners' forms did.

The hotel belonged to a family who had left the city, and its paperwork came through a secretary called Mrs. Albers, who ran three other hotels from an office two streets away, and who kept every guest's name in one notebook the way a good secretary keeps a diary for a boss who has forgotten how. The secretary had never once seen the Bellview from inside. She complained about the lifts first, then the photographs, and the weather came last, always in that order. She had a theory that a hotel was kept by its night staff and not its sign, and she had been testing that theory on Rosa for eight years. When a man from the owners arrived with a briefcase and a plan, Mrs. Albers looked at the briefcase and said, to Rosa, that the last man with a briefcase had lasted four months.

At midnight the last train came in and brought the ones who could not afford a taxi. A foreign student from the language school asked for a room in very careful English, and behind her a foreign salesman from the same train wanted a receipt and then, when that ran out, wrote her name in her own letters on the card. That side of foreign never once left Rosa frustrated, though the forms did, and the forms always did, and a form that refused a name could frustrate anybody, because the card machine wanted a postcode the student did not have. Rosa found the old reservation encyclopedia behind the desk, which was older than the lift and wrong about two of its pages, and she trusted that encyclopedia anyway for the plain words, the way she trusted the encyclopedia of medicine in the drawer. She took the student's bag, gave her room 6, and put a snack on the tray. The salesman took a snack too, and a second snack for the morning.

At two the owners' man came down with his evaluation form, which he called an evaluation of service standards in his report. His bias was toward numbers: how many rooms filled, how long each guest waited at the counter, how often the lift failed. The form had a bias built into it, and the bias favoured the hotel that looked like the other forty. Rosa had a bias of her own, toward the guest who came back the following year, and no box on the form asked for that. Neither of them had numbers to settle it. They drank tea and agreed to disagree, and then he asked for further changes anyway. There was one further thing, which was the sign above the door, and further down the page there was a line for her signature, so she signed it.

The first change was a tutorial. Every new night clerk would watch a short video about the desk and then repeat the steps back to her, and the owners would keep a record of it. Rosa had trained five clerks in eight years without a video, and all five had learned the desk in an evening. She said so. The owners' man said the hotels required consistency across forty buildings, and that consistency was the whole point of the tutorial, and the consistency of the tutorial mattered more to him than the hotel did. There was a constraint he did not say out loud, which was money, and she heard it anyway and let it pass. The constraint was not cruel. It came from the shape of the company, and it reached as far as the breakfast tray, and she had lived inside that constraint for eight years.

At three she had her break and made tea with honey and a little cream, and she put a second spoon of cream in it, because the air behind the desk dries a throat out by the fourth hour, and her doctor had written her a prescription for a spray she hardly used, though she kept the prescription in the drawer in case the winter was bad, and the prescription was the only paper in that drawer she had never had to sign. She read the label the way she read the encyclopedia at the desk, for the plain words, and put it back in the drawer next to the spare batteries. The medicine had changed its name twice since she started and the spray had not, and she had used the same medicine through both names, and the medicine in the drawer was older than the tutorial video, and the shelf label had outlived three of the boxes.

Room 14 was the room she kept for the guests who arrived with nothing. The key card for 14 had a strip of purple tape across it, which was not a standard colour for a key card, and the purple was the one thing in the room she had chosen herself. There was a second purple on the drawer of the back office, a third on the folder where she kept the guest notes she did not want anybody else to file. The purple meant nothing official. It was her own label, for the things she did not want to forget.

At five she made the closing arrangements, and she wrote her own evaluation of the night in the book. She wrote them on paper, the same way the guest book was written, and she left the arrangements for the morning clerk on the desk, and the arrangements were the same as yesterday's and she liked that. The smoke alarm got its two lines in the book, the battery in the back office got a date, and the refrigerator got a wipe because the milk and the cream had been in there since Tuesday. She put the day's forms and the tutorial notes into her briefcase, which was older than the owners' man's and held more, and whose broken clasp she had never fixed.

On the walk to the bus she thought about Mrs. Albers' theory and the student who wrote her name in her own letters and the video she had not watched yet. A hotel at night is a set of small arrangements that have to be made again every night. The consistency of it is not a personality. It is a decision she makes at eleven o'clock whether or not the week has been kind. She would watch the video before she complained about it, and she would keep the purple tape on the card for 14 either way. The nightmare of the dead alarm would keep her awake on the bus, and she did not mind that.
