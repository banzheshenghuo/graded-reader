# An Unexpected Activist Emerged to Help Block a $5 Billion Data Center in Maryland

Tall grass covers a huge empty area in Landover, Maryland. It used to be a shopping mall, closed in 2002, and the land has stood empty since.

In 2024, the company got a green light for a $5 billion data center, and word spread through the mostly Black area next door.

"I was like, 'Oh, hell no,'" said Taylor Frazier McCollum, describing the moment she learned about the project. She lived less than a mile away. "Why would you want to bring water-polluting, diesel-generating data centers right down the street from where people live, work, play? It just didn't make sense to me."

Frazier McCollum is one of many Americans who became active in public life because of data centers. Across the country, people have gone to town meetings to speak against them — winning, by various counts, about 500 holds.

Unlike many activists protecting their own homes, she does not own a home. In 2024, she and her young daughter were forced out of their apartment. She was working at a salon that cut pay and could no longer pay rent.

"It was difficult," she recalled. "Because I created the ideal life paying for us to live in this apartment and have our own space... and then to have it taken away... It felt like something that wasn't ever going to end."

They moved around and were living in her grandfather's home when she learned about the project less than a mile away.

She started a list of names that got over 25,000 signatures, leading to big protests and several local holds on data centers. It blocked the $5 billion project, which was set to break ground between 2026 and 2027.

The fight pushed Frazier McCollum to the front of local politics. She went to local meetings, spoke to state leaders and backed candidates. She found herself in a group of left-wingers, green activists, people angry about power bills and neighbors new to campaigning.

Local media interviewed her, and people in data center circles called her "the Landover girl."

## The County Needs Revenue

Staci Hartwell, a green activist for years, says she tried for years to get attention on this before Frazier McCollum came along. "This is something that was happening in her neighborhood, and it was really her awakening," Hartwell said.

But with data centers on hold, some worry about falling tax money. Last year, a local Six Flags closed. The NFL's Washington Commanders announced plans to return to neighboring Washington, D.C. Thousands of federal workers have lost their jobs. The county faces a budget deficit.

In September, Frazier McCollum went to a local council meeting to ask for money to fix the roof on her grandfather's home. Council member Wanika Fisher, who supports moving some data center projects forward, reminded her that the county needs development. "Our area has a money future we have to deal with," Fisher said. "I don't see where the money will come from. I'm not saying a data center is the answer, but we have to talk about it."

## Broadening the Fight

With the Landover project on hold, Frazier McCollum now wants a full ban on that and other data center projects. With money Hartwell helped find, she has taken her message to Black communities fighting data center growth in Atlanta, Memphis, and even Zambia.

"I feel like so many people are depending on this fight to be successful," she said. "Not just for myself, but even across the country, to set a precedent for other people fighting against hyperscale data centers."

She has lost work to attend meetings, and depended on family for rides and help with her child. Her father, Lacy McCollum Jr., sees the fight as rooted in civil rights. "We're talking about the right to water, the right to clean air, the right to a place to live," he said. "People like Taylor, they jump out here so the people behind them get to make progress."

Some push her to run for office. She says she wants to clear her debts and find a home first. For now, she is suggesting the land become a sports site, cheap homes, or a history-tourism spot. But none of those, she admits, would bring in as much money as a data center.
