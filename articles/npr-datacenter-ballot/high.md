# An Unexpected Activist Emerged to Help Block a $5 Billion Data Center in Maryland

The overgrown grass and weeds seem to stretch into the horizon on nearly 90 acres of empty land in Landover, Md. It used to be Landover Mall, which closed in 2002, and the site has been mostly vacant since.

In 2024, the developer that built the mall won approval from county planners for a $5 billion data center campus, and word began spreading through the largely Black community nearby.

"I said, 'No way,'" said Taylor Frazier McCollum, describing the moment she learned about the project. She lived less than a mile away. "Why would you want to put dirty, noisy data centers right next to where people live, work, play? It made no sense to me."

Frazier McCollum is one of a number of Americans who became politically active because of data center development. Across the country, community members have taken to local council hearings to voice their opposition. They've prompted — though estimates vary — about 500 holds on data centers.

Unlike many land-use activists trying to protect their homes, Frazier McCollum does not own a home. In 2024, she and her young daughter were evicted from their apartment. She was working at a salon that cut pay, and she could no longer pay rent.

"It was difficult," Frazier McCollum recalled. "Because I created the ideal life paying for us to live in this apartment and have our own space... and then to have it taken away... It felt like something that wasn't ever going to end."

They've moved around and were living in her grandfather's home when she learned about the proposed project less than a mile away.

She started a petition that got over 25,000 signatures. That led to widely attended protests and several local moratoriums across the county. It blocked the $5 billion project, set to break ground between 2026 and 2027.

The fight pushed her to the forefront of local politics. She went to county meetings, testified before state legislators and endorsed political candidates. She found herself among left-wingers, green activists, people angry about power bills and neighbours new to campaigning.

Local media interviewed her, and people in data center circles called her "the Landover girl."

## But the Area Needs Money

Staci Hartwell, who has fought for the environment for years, says she tried for years to get attention on the issue before Frazier McCollum came along. "It was happening right where she lives, and it really woke her up," Hartwell said.

But with data centers paused, some worry about declining tax revenue. A local theme park closed last year. The Washington football team plans to move back to Washington, D.C. Thousands of government workers have lost their jobs. The area is short of money.

In September, Frazier McCollum appeared at a county council meeting to advocate for roof repairs. Council member Wanika Fisher, who wants some projects to go ahead, reminded her the area needs growth.

"Our area needs to make money," Fisher said. "I don't see where it will come from. I'm not saying a data center is the answer, but we have to talk about it."

## Taking the Fight Further

With the Landover project on hold, she now wants a full ban on data centers. With money Hartwell helped find, she has taken her message to Black communities fighting data center growth in Atlanta, Memphis and even Zambia.

"So many people are depending on this fight to be successful," she said. "Not just for me, but across the country, to set precedent for others fighting hyperscale data centers."

She has lost work to attend meetings or knock on doors, and depends on family for rides and help with her child.

Her father, Lacy McCollum Jr., sees the fight as a matter of civil rights. "We're talking about the right to water, the right to clean air, the right to a place to live. That's everybody's fight," he said. "While I'm afraid for her, we need people like that. We needed the Medgar Everses. We needed the Martin Luther Kings. People like Taylor jump out here so the people behind them can make progress."

Some urge her to run for office. She says she wants to pay off her debts and find a home first. "I didn't want to focus on housing and a campaign at once," she said. "Now is not the moment, but I do want to run for something."

For now, she is pitching plans to turn the land into a sports complex, affordable housing, or a heritage-tourism site. But none of those, she admits, would bring in as much money as a data center.
