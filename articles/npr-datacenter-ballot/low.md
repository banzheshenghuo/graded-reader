# One Woman Helped Block a $5 Billion Data Center in Maryland

Tall grass covers a big empty lot in Landover, Maryland. It used to be a shopping mall, which closed in 2002. For more than 20 years, the land has stood mostly empty.

Then in 2024, the company got a green light for a $5 billion data center. Word spread fast in the mostly Black area next door.

"I said, 'No way,'" said Taylor Frazier McCollum. She lived very close by. "Why would you want to put dirty, noisy data centers right next to where people live?"

## A Political Awakening

She is one of many Americans whose public life started because of data centers. Across the country, people have gone to town meetings to speak against them. They have won about 500 holds on data centers.

Unlike many people who fight to protect their own homes, she does not own a home. In 2024, she and her young daughter were forced out of their flat after she lost her job and could not pay rent.

"It was hard," she said. "I built the perfect life for us in that apartment... and then to have it taken away." They moved around and were staying in her grandfather's home when she learned about the project less than a mile away.

## The Petition

She started a paper with names that got over 25,000 names. That led to big protests and several local holds on data centers. It blocked the $5 billion project.

The fight pushed her into local politics. She went to local meetings, spoke to state leaders, and backed candidates. She found herself among left-wingers, green campaigners, people angry about power bills, and people new to campaigning.

Local media interviewed her. People in data center circles called her "the Landover girl."

## But the County Needs Money

Staci Hartwell, who has fought for the environment for years, says she tried for years to get people to notice before Frazier McCollum came along. "This was happening in her neighborhood, and it was really her awakening," Hartwell said.

But with data centers on hold, some people worry about falling tax money. A local Six Flags closed last year. The Washington Commanders football team plans to move back to Washington, D.C. Thousands of government workers have lost their jobs. The area is short of money.

In September, she asked the local council for money to fix her grandfather's roof. Council member Wanika Fisher, who wants some projects to go ahead, reminded her that the area needs growth. "We need to sort out our money future," Fisher said. "I'm not saying a data center is the answer, but it's worth a hard look."

## Taking the Fight Further

Now Frazier McCollum wants a total ban on data centers. With money from Hartwell and others, she has taken her message to Black communities fighting data center growth in Atlanta, Memphis, and even Zambia.

"I feel like so many people are depending on this fight to be successful," she said. "Not just for myself, but to set an example for other people fighting these huge data centers."

She has lost work to attend meetings and knock on doors, and has depended on family for rides and help with her child. Her father, Lacy McCollum Jr., sees the fight as a rights issue. "We're talking about the right to water, the right to clean air, the right to a place to live," he said. "People like Taylor jump out here so the people behind them get to make progress."

Some people push her to run for office. She says she wants to clear her bills and get a home first. For now, she is suggesting other uses for the land — a sports site, cheap homes, or history tourism. But she admits none would bring in as much money as a data center.
