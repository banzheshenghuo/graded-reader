# The Rain

The first real rain of the season arrived on Monday at 5:40 in the morning, and the kitchen guard, which watches doors and temperatures, logged it anyway, in its way: the refrigerator held its four degrees, the balcony sensor with its one-month battery reported a drop of three degrees in eleven minutes, and the line on my phone bent like a straw. I lay in the dark and listened to the house learn about weather. Then, from the small room, a voice announced the rain officially, at full volume, in the language that is native to no country, and Monday began.

Rain days have their own arrangements, drafted years ago by the secretary of this family and never once amended, which is the arrangement of a person who has seen weather before: no park, but the long hallway becomes one; no market, but the umbrella stands by the door like a briefcase with a job; and the swimming question is set aside, which Bobby complained about at breakfast, at length, with gestures, to an audience of one tiger and one rabbit, both unmoved; his complaint was fair, and the answer was still no. The rain frustrated him for exactly one minute. Then he discovered that rain has a sound, and the window became his television, and the morning improved.

We watched it together, the two of us and the encyclopedia, volume I, open to the islands, which seemed fair. "Rain," said Grandma, from the kitchen, "is the sea visiting." Bobby pressed one finger on the glass, followed a drop down as far as it would go, lost it, and started again, a small quality-control engineer at the factory window. The drops won every time. He was not frustrated by this; his bias says games that cannot be won are the only ones worth playing. Consistency, he seemed to feel, was the whole point.

At nine I worked from home, which means I fought the rain over video calls with three foreign colleagues while the router, the hero of such days, quietly did the real work. The briefcase stayed shut. One project reviewed itself without me and left polite comments, which the rain made softer, or which I read softly; either way, no meeting frustrated me today, and by lunch I had fixed the tutorial's third step, the one that has frustrated the team since October, in eleven minutes, from the kitchen table, with a small boy asleep on my shoulder. Some bugs wait for rain; that is a consistency I can plan around.

The kitchen guard, meanwhile, had a real job for once. Rainday humidity is its favorite meal: the balcony sensor climbed, the export drew a mountain where the flat line usually lives, the refrigerator downstairs kept its four degrees like a professional, and at 11:20 it flagged one anomaly, a spike, ten minutes, then gone. I checked through the glass: the balcony door, blown open by wind, swinging like the park swing. The machine flagged the door. The machine cannot flag the wind. That distinction, I have decided, is the whole difference between a guard and a person, and I wrote it in the margin of my notes, where the best sentences end up, unread.

Lunch was soup, because Grandma's arrangements for rain days are written in the same ink as everything else: soup, and bread, and the smell of both in the hall by ten. She complained about the weather exactly once, for tradition, and then spent an hour teaching Bobby to put blocks into a jar and pour them out again, a tutorial in containers that he took more seriously than most of my meetings. Her evaluation of his progress: "He is ready for rice." My evaluation of her tutorial: better than most documentation I have paid for. Given the size of rice, this is either nothing or everything, and with her it is everything.

At four, the rain still steady, we held the ceremony of the cream. One small scoop of the day's cream, the constraint of the house, eaten slowly at the window with the gray light coming in sideways. He pointed at the rain, then at his bowl, then at the rain, connecting, I think, the two best systems in his day. The medicine of the hour went down without negotiation. Outside, the street had traded its colors for one gray, and the pines along the fence had gone almost purple in the wet, and the umbrella by the door dripped its small opinion onto the mat.

By six the rain had punched itself out, and the evening report read like a weather station's diary: humidity back inside its margin, temperature flat, the door counted and closed, battery fine, consistency restored. The anomaly of the day, properly speaking, had been mine: a foreign feeling, working well, at home, in the rain, under no constraint but the rain's own, and enjoying it, which the statistics of my year do not predict. Some anomalies you fix. Some you frame.

The briefcase comes back tomorrow, its one hard task unchanged, and the rain, the forecast says, comes back Wednesday, which Grandma has already written into the arrangements, in ink, with a question mark she will ignore. The encyclopedia stays at I, patient as furniture; the fish there, it turns out, complain about nothing, which makes them the best colleagues I have read about all year. the review pile, one hundred and forty-three strong, waits like a suitcase half packed. There was, later, one last round of small business, the evening audit. The purple swim cap, hung to dry by the purple coat. The battery drawer, found and counted: two new, one dead, to be recycled. The medicine shelf, full, its small bottles standing like a pharmacy with one patient; and the day’s real medicine, the cream, in its own small box, bound by the one-scoop rule, asked for by nobody, standing by for four o’clock. The cream, in its tub, constraint-bound, consulted by nobody, standing by for tomorrow at four. The encyclopedia, back at I, its islands holding. The foreign rain, still at it, softer now, foreign in a different way, the way a guest gets foreign after dinner, when the talking is done. The secretary of the family made her evaluation of the day in one word, "good," and went home under one umbrella, and the bias she carries toward soup weather was confirmed for the season. Constraint by constraint, evaluation by evaluation, the house put itself back in order, the way it does, quietly, the way rain does when it stops.

The boy slept with wet hair from his bath, one hand out of the blanket, pointing at nothing, still following drops, his cream supper settled, his tutorial of a day filed, the whole wet Monday carried up the stairs on a small shoulder that never once complained about the weight. And the house, dried and counted and uploaded, held its four degrees against the first cold night of the year — the refrigerator's promise, the secretary's plan, a father's bias toward warm lines on cold graphs, which is, when you add it up, the whole job: to be the warm line on somebody's graph while the weather does whatever the weather is going to do.
