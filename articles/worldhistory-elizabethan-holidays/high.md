# Holidays in the Elizabethan Era

During the Elizabethan Era (1558-1603), people of all classes greatly looked forward to the many holidays spread through the year. Most of these public holidays were also religious days, and going to the service was required by law. Still, the feasts that accompanied these "holy days" were anticipated with pleasure, and many secular traditions appeared alongside them — playing football on Shrove Tuesday, giving gifts to mothers on the third Sunday before Easter. Holidays were also a chance to visit town fairs or travel farther. The Elizabethan period was the first time the idea of a Grand Tour of Europe caught on among the rich — a way to widen a young person's horizons and round off their education.

## Holy Days: Off Work, At Church

The idea of a long holiday as rest from work is quite modern. In the Middle Ages, a worker's only time off was Sundays and holy days — days the Church set for saints, or events like Christmas and Easter. In the 16th century these holy days became known by the more familiar secular term "holidays." The Elizabethan period was also the first time such days were associated less with church services and more with a 24-hour break from everyday life — with better food and drink if possible. Still, attendance at church on the main holy days remained required by law.

In the second half of the 16th century, the Anglican Church listed 17 main holy days, some moving with the moon's cycle. The list ran from New Year's Day (the Circumcision) through Twelfth Day, Candlemas, Shrove Tuesday, Lady Day, Easter, May Day, Midsummer Day, Michaelmas and All Hallows, to Christmas. One might add Saint George's Day — the feast of England's special protector saint — though it was not an official holiday. The English church changes of the age, and especially the Puritan movement, toned down the showier old ceremonies: the grand candle walk at Candlemas was largely dropped. In contrast, secular traditions flourished: gifts on New Year's Day; pancakes and football on Shrove Tuesday (the origin of Mardi Gras); bonfires on Midsummer's Day. During Lent, people threw stones at a figure of a man set up by the road — perhaps to ease the frustration of plainer food. On the third Sunday of Lent, people visited or gave gifts to their mothers: hence Mother's Day. Maundy Thursday was for charity.

## Feasts, Fairs, and the Two Great Holidays

May Day and Whitsunday brought summer festivals with feasts, dancing and plays. Feasts were the heart of holy days — the part most awaited. That required prep work, which is why holidays grew their own "Eve," like Christmas Eve, today's only survivor: first a fast (usually just the evening, no meat), then the wonderful dishes of the feast day itself, like the autumn goose.

Fairs were another draw. Many towns held at least one a year — farm shows, travelling actors, plays, soldier displays and dances, plus goods from sellers across the country and abroad, wine sellers among them.

Easter was the greatest celebration of the year, set for the first Sunday after the first full moon on or after March 21. Everyone went to church — even the less eager took part, recorded by priests in an "Easter Book," since taking part at least three times a year was required by law. The feasts offered long-missed meat and sweets, and as Catholic traditions faded, older spring customs returned: eggs and rabbits came back alongside the Christian Easter.

Christmas began with Advent — originally a fast, growing laxer with time — then ran 12 days from December 25. School children had another two weeks off. Homes wore winter green branches; a Yule log burned through the whole holiday. Special dishes used costly ingredients: pies, spiced cakes, nuts and oranges — winter rarities — plus warm spiced drinks, and of course dancing, music and games.

## A Break From the Rules

Holidays were not only a rest from work but a chance to loosen the rules: men and women changing places, a poor worker made "king of the feast," young workers giving orders to their elders — all great fun, along with drinking perhaps too much. Yet, as historian J. Morrill points out, "Festive licence, while seemingly transgressing social boundaries, served in reality to underscore expectations about the appropriate behaviour demanded in everyday life." The break was temporary. The feasts also showed the gap between those who had and those who had not, and the rich were fixed in their place by the expectation that they show their money and give to the poor — paying a larger share of community celebrations.

## Travel and the Grand Tour

Travel for pleasure was only beginning in this era. Pilgrims had always visited holy sites and relics; now more people travelled purely for secular pleasure. Famous sights included the Golden Hind — Sir Francis Drake's ship from his trip around the world of 1577-80 — kept on show in London, and the Tower of London with its crown jewels.

Travel itself, however, was hard: no official road system, mostly dirt tracks, bridges that failed after heavy rains. Rich travellers could hire horse carriages; most rode horses, covering around 80 kilometres a day. Rivers offered another way past the bumpy roads. At night there were simple roadside stops — and dangers such as robbers, sometimes tipped off by the staff of the very inn where one had slept.

Although a trip to the local fair felt like a major expedition, the richer Elizabethans began the Grand Tour that later centuries made famous: months in Continental Europe visiting the ruins of antiquity and Renaissance highlights, learning languages, studying art, law or even gardening with noted teachers — ideal training for politics and diplomacy. Italy, France and Spain were the top destinations. And undoubtedly many young men fled England to escape bad debts or the authorities, or simply in search of adventure — a new life where every day was a holiday.
