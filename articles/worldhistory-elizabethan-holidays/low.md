# Holidays in the Elizabethan Era

In the age of Queen Elizabeth I (1558-1603), people of every class loved the many special days spread through the year. Most holidays were also church days, and going to church was required by law. But the big meals that came with these special days were what people waited for, and many fun traditions grew around them — football games in early spring, gifts to mothers before Easter. Holidays were also a chance to visit town fairs or even travel far. Rich young people began the fashion of a long trip across Europe, to open their eyes and finish their education.

## Holy Days: Off Work, At Church

The idea of a holiday as rest from work is quite modern. In the Middle Ages, a worker only got Sundays and a few church days off — days set for saints, Christmas, Easter. In the 1500s, these church days began to be called simply "holidays" — and for the first time they were less about church and more about taking a day away from daily life, eating better food than usual. But going to church on the main special days was still the law.

The Church listed 17 main special days, from New Year's Day to Christmas. Fun traditions grew around them: gifts on New Year's Day, pancakes and football before the fasting month, big fires in summer, and — during the fasting season — visits and gifts to mothers. That is where Mother's Day comes from. Fairs were another pleasure: travelling actors, plays, soldier shows, dances, and goods from sellers across the country and abroad.

## Easter and Christmas

Easter was the biggest celebration of the year. Everyone went to church — even people who rarely did — and priests kept records in an "Easter Book," because everyone had to take part at least three times a year. The holiday meals brought meat and sweets people had missed for weeks. And with fewer church traditions, old spring customs returned: eggs and rabbits joined Easter.

Christmas lasted 12 days, from December 25 to January 6, and school children got two weeks off. Homes were dressed with green branches, a big log burned all holiday, and the tables held pies, sweet cakes, nuts, oranges and warm spiced drinks — rare treats in winter.

## A Break From the Rules

Holidays were also a chance to bend the rules: men and women changed roles, a poor worker was made "king of the holiday meal," young workers gave orders to their elders, and everyone drank too much. One history writer notes the fun actually taught people the rules of everyday life — the break was short, and it reminded everyone of their place. The holiday meals also showed the gap between rich and poor, since the rich were expected to pay the bigger share and give to the poor.

## Travel and the a long study trip across Europe

Travel for fun was just beginning in this age. Religious travellers had always visited special places; now more and more people travelled simply to see the sights — like the famous ship that sailed around the world, kept for visitors in London.

Roads, though, were terrible dirt tracks, and bridges often fell down after heavy rain. Most people rode horses — about 80 kilometres a day. At night there were simple places to sleep and eat, where the staff might quietly warn thieves waiting on the road.

The richest young men began the a long study trip across Europe: months in Italy, France and Spain, visiting old ruins, learning languages, and studying with famous teachers. It was seen as the best education for a future in politics. And some simply ran away from England — from unpaid bills, from trouble, or for adventure — to find a life where every day felt like a holiday.
