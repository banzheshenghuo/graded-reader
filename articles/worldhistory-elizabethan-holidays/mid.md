# Holidays in the Elizabethan Era

In the age of Queen Elizabeth I (1558-1603), people of every class looked forward to the many holidays spread through the year. Most public holidays were also religious days, and going to church was required by law. But the feasts that came with these holy days were what people truly waited for, and many non-religious traditions grew around them — playing football on Shrove Tuesday, giving gifts to mothers on the third Sunday before Easter. Holidays were also a chance to visit town fairs or travel further. And the rich began the fashion of a "Grand Tour" of Europe, seen as a way to open a young person's mind.

## Holy Days: Off Work, At Church

The idea of a holiday as rest from work is quite modern. In the Middle Ages, a worker's only time off was Sundays and church days — days the Church set for saints, Christmas, Easter. In the 1500s they began to be called simply "holidays," and for the first time they were less about church services and more about a 24-hour break from daily life, with better food than usual if possible. But attending church on the main holy days was still the law.

The Anglican Church listed 17 main holy days in the second half of the 1500s, from New Year's Day to Christmas. Around them grew customs still familiar today: gifts on New Year's Day; pancakes and football on Shrove Tuesday (where Mardi Gras began); big fires in summer. During Lent, people visited or gave gifts to their mothers — the root of Mother's Day. The Thursday before Easter was for giving to the poor.

## Feasts, Fairs, and the Two Big Holidays

May Day and Whitsunday brought summer festivals with feasts, dancing and plays. Feasts needed prep work — which is why holidays got their own "Eve" (Christmas Eve is the only one left), a day of fasting before the feast, like the goose eaten in autumn.

Fairs were another attraction: farm shows, travelling actors, plays, soldier displays and dances, plus goods from sellers across the country and abroad.

Easter was the greatest celebration. Everyone went to church — even the less devoted, who had to take part at least three times a year by law — and priests kept records in an "Easter Book." The feasts offered long-missed meat and sweets, and as Catholic traditions faded, old spring customs returned: eggs and rabbits joined Easter alongside the Christian feast.

Christmas lasted 12 days, with two weeks off school. Homes were dressed with winter green branches, a big log burned through the holiday, and the table held pies, spiced cakes, nuts, oranges and warm spiced drinks.

## A Break From the Rules

Holidays were also a chance to relax the rules: men and women changed places, a poor worker was made "king of the feast," young workers gave orders to their elders, and everyone drank freely. Yet, as one history writer notes, all this freedom actually showed everyone the rules of everyday life — the break was short. The feasts also underlined the gap between rich and poor, since the rich were expected to display their wealth and pay the larger share of community celebrations.

## Travel and the Grand Tour

Travel for pleasure was just beginning. Pilgrims had always visited holy places; now more people travelled simply to see the sights — attractions included the Golden Hind, the ship that sailed around the world, displayed in London.

Roads, though, were terrible dirt tracks and bridges often failed. Most people rode horses, covering about 80 kilometres a day; rivers were another way past the rough roads. Travellers slept at simple roadside inns — and risked thieves, sometimes warned by the very staff of the inn where they had slept.

The richest young men began the Grand Tour: months in Italy, France and Spain, visiting ancient ruins and Renaissance treasures, learning languages, studying with noted teachers — seen as ideal training for politics and diplomacy. And some ran from England for other reasons: unpaid bills, trouble with the law, or the longing for adventure — seeking a life where every day was a holiday.
