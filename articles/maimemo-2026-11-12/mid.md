# The Blackout

Thursday, 7:40 in the evening, the lights went out in the whole block at once. Not a flicker, not a brown-out, but an honest, complete dark, the kind that makes you hear your own kitchen, and for a moment every room in the building was a foreign country with the furniture in the wrong place. The secretary of this family, four floors away at her own dark window, later said she had counted to eleven before doing anything, which she considers the correct rate for emergencies. Bobby was in the bath at the time. For one second he was a small wet person in a black room, and then I had him in a towel in my arms, and his first word about the whole affair was the only correct one, delivered in the dark with great feeling: "Oh."

The building's lights came back inside a minute, because the building has a generator, and the generator has a battery, and the battery, this evening, was the hero. Our apartment did not come back. Our apartment has the kitchen guard, and the kitchen guard had its own trouble: the refrigerator lost power, the balcony sensor lost its link to the router, and the little machine, which has no intelligence and no imagination, reported the only thing it could — that the arrangements it had been counting had all stopped at once. Its first anomaly of the evening was silence. Eight door events in two hours, and then silence, then a long flat nothing.

So we did what people in big buildings do when the lights die: we took the stairs, with a candle, the way my grandmother used to, and we knocked on the neighbor's door. The old man on our floor — the one who complains about the elevator, and about the price of the elevator's electricity, in that order, and who complains about both on a fixed schedule we have all learned to live with — had already set out three candles in jam jars on his table, and a kettle, and he opened the door before we finished knocking, as if he had been waiting for someone to be useful to.

Bobby had never eaten dinner by candlelight. He sat in a stranger's kitchen, in the purple towel of our own bathroom, and studied the three flames with the deep attention he gives to shadows and water, a tutorial in looking that he has been giving us for months, and then he did what he does with all new things: he pointed, and he made a sound, and he waited to see whether the world would answer. The world, tonight, answered in the form of an old man saying, "Yes. Fire." He pointed again. "Yes," said the neighbor. "Still fire."

The arrangement was improvised and it worked: soup from the old man's kettle, bread from our bag, a bowl of rice for the boy, and the constraint of the evening declared in one sentence — nobody leaves this table until the power comes back or the candles run out, whichever comes first. The medicine of the blackout, if you want to call it that, is that a dark room has no screens and a very good clock, and everybody in a candle's radius is available for conversation, at a rate the evening can support. The old man told us about 1976, when the whole city went dark for eleven days and people cooked on the street and the neighborhood became, in his words, "a village with worse plumbing." He told it without frustration, which was its own small consistency, and he told it twice: once for us, and once because he liked the sound of it. My evaluation of the story: better than anything on a screen. The briefcase, four floors down in the dark, had nothing to add.

My own version of the evening was the tutorial in using nothing: no laptop, no phone beyond the flashlight, no way to answer a message. I sat with my hands in my lap and understood, at last, what my briefcase has been trying to tell me for years — that most of what it carries can wait, and that the rest was never in it. A lesson, like most, that arrived only when the lid could not be opened.

Halfway through the rice, the neighbor said the thing that stayed with me. "Everything in this house," he said, waving at his own dark kitchen, "was bought to be reliable. Nothing here is reliable. The candles are reliable." He said it as an evaluation, not a complaint, and he said it with the calm of a man who has tested the claim many times and is fond of the result, which is a kind of consistency too, and rarer than the electrical sort. His consistency, for forty years, has been jam jars with candles in them, and one shelf's worth of things that work without a wire.

The evening's own arrangements held without being written down, which is how the best ones do. The foreign dark outside the window stayed foreign; nobody tried to explain it twice. The frustration that might have come with a cold dinner never arrived, and the purple towel was pronounced by its wearer to be the best towel of the year. The encyclopedia can wait for a better lamp, and the medicine cabinet can wait for a better hour; tonight the tutorial was given by the candles, and the cream, one scoop of the cream, was carried across a dark hallway on a saucer, which is its own ceremony, and the family's bias toward carrying things by hand was confirmed once more.

At 9:20, the lights came back, all at once, the way they left, and the whole neighborhood made the sound a neighborhood makes: a hundred refrigerators starting up inside a second, a chorus. Our own refrigerator came on with its four degrees to defend, the router lit up, the kitchen guard blinked awake and sent me a message that was essentially an apology in numbers. It reported one anomaly for the evening: a gap of a hundred minutes, unexplained, no data, and it was right to report it. The encyclopedia, four floors away, could not have filed it better. It had no way to know that the gap was the best part of the month.

The encyclopedia, that night, was read by candlelight, volume M, the moon — the secretary's choice, and a good one, in a purple room three floors up; the boy looked at the picture, then at the actual dark window, and made the comparison himself, which is the whole point of a book. The cream, one small scoop of it, was served at 9:30 instead of four o'clock, a small violation of the standing constraint of the hour, and nobody minded, because the constraint of the hour is ours, and tonight the hour was not.

Bobby fell asleep with the candle-smell still in his pajamas. The secretary sniffed him once, and said, in her flat voice, that he smelled like 1962. The bias of old people toward the past is usually nostalgic; hers is technical, and her evaluation of the smell was forensic. She was assessing, not sighing — that smell meant wax and winter and the particular kind of evening she had, once, every week, for years.

The kitchen guard has resumed counting the doors, the battery on the balcony has resumed reporting, and the machine has no record of the good part. Some evenings are for the record, and some evenings are the reason there is no record worth keeping. Tomorrow the router will be warm again, the messages will pile up, the briefcase will do exactly what it does, and the medicine of a hundred dark minutes will be filed away somewhere the machine cannot index. But for one hundred minutes, in a stranger's kitchen with three jam jars, we were the most reliable thing in the building, and the refrigerator, in the dark, kept its cold like a secret held for a friend, frustrating nobody, an anomaly the size of an evening, and the family's bias toward candles was confirmed for the winter.
