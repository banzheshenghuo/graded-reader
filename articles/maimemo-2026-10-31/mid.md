# The First Halloween

Halloween came to our street on Friday evening, and it came, as most foreign things do in this family, through Grandma, who took it up the way she takes up everything: completely, with a list. The arrangements were posted on the refrigerator at noon. Costume: ready. Pumpkin: bought. Candy for the door: two bags, one to give away and one to guard, because our neighbors, she had learned, take the second bag as a challenge. My briefcase took the evening off, which is the one bias against work that this house encourages, and nothing about the plan frustrated anybody, which, on a Friday, is its own small holiday. Bobby's first Halloween. He is one year old, which meant the costume was really for us, and we made the most of it.

He went as a small purple bat. The choice took one tutorial of a conversation, in which Grandma offered a pumpkin, I offered a mouse, and Bobby, holding up the purple bat pajamas from the laundry, proposed the only idea with any dignity in it. The secretary of the family ratified it in four seconds, stamping it, in effect, with the only bias she holds: whatever the boy proposes by gesture becomes law. The bat ears stood up on their own. He wore them all afternoon, at dinner, in the bath, and complained, loudly, at bedtime, and again at midnight, when we tried at last to remove them, bat ears having, in his opinion, been the whole point of the year so far.

At six, the doorbell rang for the first time, and the kitchen guard nearly lost its mind. Twelve rings in two hours, each one flagged as an anomaly, each one a small band of foreign children, ghosts and witches and one small robot, shouting trick or treat with the confidence of people who have trained for this all year. The refrigerator stayed shut all evening, its four degrees untroubled by history. The machine counted every knock. The machine has no idea what Halloween is; to the foreign sensors of its small world, a holiday is just weather that rings. It flagged the whole evening as a storm, twelve anomalies in two hours, and the storm was the best evening this street has had since summer, and the strangest thing in the arrangements book, which now has a Halloween page.

Bobby held the candy bowl with both hands. He did not understand giving. Giving frustrated him, at first, the way physics frustrates poets. He understood receiving perfectly, and when the first ghost took a candy from his bowl, he complained, briefly, from his whole chest, then watched her go, thought about it, and delivered his evaluation, which was more of a complaint than a review: this too was a kind of magic, apparently, and he was willing to allow it, this time. By the fourth group he was handing candy out himself, two pieces per customer, his own rate, generous to the point of bankruptcy. A witch complimented his bat ears. A small Frankenstein bowed to him. He bowed back. Somewhere in the hallway, his grandmother, who had planned to supervise, was laughing at the door with three other mothers like a woman released from a very long meeting.

My job was the pumpkin. I had bought a carving tutorial, in the form of a knife and confidence, and I followed neither, and the result was crooked but recognizable, with one tooth too many, which Grandma declared to be a self-portrait. It sat on our windowsill with a small battery candle inside, burning with more consistency than most of my code, and when the battery worked and the face lit up, Bobby looked at it the way he looks at the encyclopedia: a thing from the book of the world, finally in the room. The battery lasted the whole evening, and my evaluation of the pumpkin, revised at dusk: crooked, over-toothed, and exactly right. Most good things in this house run on batteries nobody remembers to check, and then, on the one night that matters, they simply work.

The candy constraint collapsed at eight. He found the bowl on the table, opened one purple wrapper with the focus of a surgeon, and tasted his first chocolate. The face he made was the face from the ice cream, and the ice cream, and the first swim: the face of a person meeting the good part of life in order. Cream is his medicine and his reward, the constraint of one scoop a rule with a smile in it; chocolate, we discovered tonight, is his Third Thing, and the briefcase, had it been home, would have been no help at all. The constraint of one scoop still stands for cream. Chocolate is governed, as of tonight, by a new law we have not written down yet, because the current text of the law is his face, and it is not negotiable.

At nine the street went quiet, the consistency of a street after a party. The ghosts went home to count their candy, the robot's parents carried the robot, and the guard's report, read later, was one page of storm and then the flat, ordinary line of a house with a sleeping boy in it: four degrees in the purple kitchen, no anomaly after eight, the night uploaded, the battery of the pumpkin candle still burning on the sill. Somewhere a foreign witch is asleep with her candy sorted by color, and somewhere a secretary's book has a new page, and a secretary's bias has a new exception, and both of these are the same story.

His evaluation of Halloween, delivered from the crib, was one long sentence in his own language, bat ears still on, spoken to the ceiling, to the tiger, and to the year in general. Grandma's arrangements book already has next year's page. The encyclopedia will reach H's neighbors eventually, but Halloween is not in any encyclopedia; it resists filing, which is exactly its medicine, it is in the door, and in the bowl, and in the small bat who gave away his candy and kept his ears. Consistency, the doctor said, is the best medicine, and the refrigerator swears by it, and eleven months of the year, so do we. But the last constraint of tonight is different: the briefcase stays shut, the tutorial stays unwritten, On one night a year, this family prescribes something else entirely: one purple evening, one crooked pumpkin, one bowl that empties because everyone it meets is a friend.
