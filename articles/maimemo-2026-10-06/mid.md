# The Purple App Goes Travelling

## One small ticket

Grandma's two weeks with us ended on a Friday, and on Saturday morning we followed her home — all three of us, one suitcase, one baby bag, and a box of gifts. The reservation was mine to make: two single seats and a small space for the baby carrier. The old secretary in me checked the booking twice, and my phone battery — fully charged — became our third family member for the day. "An aisle seat," the app told me, "is better for families." It was right. The aisle seat let my wife stand up ten times without climbing over strangers. "Sorry," said a man each time, moving his bag; a woman behind us coughed once, and the baby turned and studied her like a museum piece.

Unfortunately, the fare had grown again since spring. "Inflation," said Grandma, who now uses the word like a professional. I paid the fare with my phone, and Grandma paid for the coffee with cash from her wallet, and the small one slept through the whole ticket event, which was the best magic of the day.

The baggage was a science. My wife packed spare everything: diapers, bottles, small shirts, underwear, one warm coat. I packed the medicine box — Grandma's new prescription taped inside the lid — and my laptop. Grandma packed nothing; she had left half her heart in our apartment, she said, and it would keep the place warm. The train was sunny and slow. The baby chewed a piece of bread and watched the fields with serious eyes.

## Home again

Bobby met us at the door, turning circles. He chased the neighbor's cat twice before lunch, chased one pigeon outside the museum fence later, and slept like a winner all evening, and the cat, an old professional, simply walked away. The house was warm and full of smells from the kitchen. Bottles piled up in the sink; by evening the sink was a small mountain, and I washed them all while Grandma cooked, with the Sunday roast already waiting in the refrigerator and fresh milk cooling beside it. She had been a secretary for thirty years, and the kitchen, like an office, runs on her quiet order. "You wash, I cook," she said. "That is a protocol now." The protocol of the house, I have learned, beats any software protocol I ever wrote.

Moreover, there was one problem: her internet. The router in the hall was older than my whole career. Every video call froze; every page opened like a sleepy turtle. The router in the hall was older than my whole career. Every video call froze; every page opened like a sleepy turtle. "It has always been like this," Grandma said, as if that was a reason. On Sunday morning I bought a new router in town, plugged it in, and set it up with a simple password — her dog's name and her house number, nothing more. The new router is efficient; pages open in a blink now. Authentication should never be a wall between a grandma and her family. Credentials are keys, and keys should fit old hands; the rest is implicit trust. She typed it once — understands it better than any password I ever made — and the whole house went fast. "You are a clever boy," she said. I am thirty-two, but I took the compliment.

That afternoon we visited the small museum in her town — the big event of our week. Grandma pushed the baby cart past old clocks and old letters. In the garden behind the museum, a gray rabbit sat under a tree, chewing a leaf, calm as a stone lion. The baby pointed at it and shouted her whole opinion. "Yes," Grandma agreed. "The rabbit is the best part." We stopped at the supermarket near the museum for bread and milk — two dollars more than last year, said Grandma, holding her small handbag like a shield — and bought a small quantity of everything the refrigerator at home could hold. On the way out, we fed the ducks; the baby tried to feed me a leaf too, and I chewed it like a rabbit, to great laughter.

In the park next door hung the swings. We had carried her to the swing before, and she had only stared. This time — this time she was ready. Three small pushes, and up she went, up and down, and her whole face opened like a window. She clapped in the air. Grandma waved from the bench, and the baby waved back, one hand holding the swing rope, which is, I now believe, the bravest thing anyone in our family has ever done.

## A quiet Sunday of code

While the house slept, I opened my laptop at the old desk. One anomaly report had arrived in the night — a small, strange jump in the log — and I decrypted the backup to look closer. Conclusion: one user had typed her password with the keyboard in the wrong language. Her credentials were fine; only her keyboard was confused. I wrote her a kind note, added validation for the wrong-keyboard case, then a short script to say something gentle — a small specification, three lines, no more. I added one line to the specification: be kind to tired typists. Requirement done, code committed, pushed to the repository. Four hundred tenants, one small fix, and the night checks still run unsupervised while families sleep. Time with a baby is full of constraints, but the constraint of Sunday morning — one quiet hour — made me specific and fast.

Moreover, my newsletter came in at noon — an article about machine intelligence, with pretty pictures of word vectors, arrows flying through space. Words that sit near each other, the article said, mean similar things. In their map, "grandmother" and "warm" are neighbors; so are "spoon" and "family", I would guess. My brother, the nurse, called while I read it; meanwhile another family had left a thank-you note in the repository, with a photo of two happy grandparents. He had used the app's new tutorial to teach his own hospital team, and his team now handles the night reports with more care; through the window behind him I saw the first thin snow of the year falling on the hospital garden. "Your tutorial is robust," he said. "Nurses are a hard audience, and they liked it. Be specific when you write to tired people — they read with one eye." Then he sent me a locked photo of his team at the front desk; I decrypted it and printed it for Grandma, who understands photos better than passwords.

He asked about the week, and I told him about the sink, the router, the museum rabbit, the swing. "Write it all down," he said again — his eternal requirement. I told him the app had done it for me: every event, every bottle, every swing, logged with love and integers. There was, I admitted, one more thing. I had found the old box of my grandpa's instruments and opened it, very quietly, in the front room. The baby touched the small guitar with one finger — that old tune of my grandpa's, the one that represents his whole town, seemed to live inside it — and the string sang one low note, and she turned her head to me with wide eyes, as if the box had spoken.

"Keep the instruments where she can reach them," my brother said. "Music runs in our family like water." He was right. That evening Grandma hummed her old tune while cooking, and the baby, on the floor with two spoons, banged the rhythm like a small drummer. Two instruments, wooden spoons — the quality was poor, the joy was not. Quantity matters less than people think; quality of an hour matters more.

## Goodbye, again

On Monday we took the one-way option again — no, the two-way, because we will be back. At the station, the pharmacy had just delivered her last prescription of the season, and Grandma put a spoon in the baby's bag, and an apple in mine. "It represents what it always represents," she said — an implicit rule, as always — and gave herself away with a smile: come back soon. The tune followed us onto the train; my wife caught herself humming it before the second stop. Some software migrates from phone to phone; some songs migrate from heart to heart. Both, I think, are about staying close.

The baby waved at the window for a long time, up and down, up and down, until the town disappeared behind the green fields, where snow was still two months away. Then she slept, and I looked at myself in the dark glass — tired, happy, holding a warm small weight. In my notebook I wrote one line for the week: unfortunately, time is short; fortunately, love is efficient. The little purple app could not have said it better.
