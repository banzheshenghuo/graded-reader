# The Three O'Clock Batch

Ilse had opened the bakery on Kestrel Street at three in the morning for fourteen years, and she could tell what a loaf needed before she touched it. Fourteen years had done that to her without asking, and she never wrote it on any form.

The bakery belonged to a small chain now, which meant paper. The chain had a secretary named Mrs. Vogel who sent the orders twice, once by hand and once by post, and the secretary had never once seen the shop from inside, and who complained about the printer first, then the weather, then her back, always in that order. She had a theory that a bakery was kept by its first customer and not its window, and she had been testing that theory on Ilse for six years. When the chain sent a man with a briefcase to look at the shop, Mrs. Vogel looked at the briefcase and said, to Ilse, that the last man with a briefcase had lasted five months, and that the secretary before him had lasted eleven years.

At three Ilse did the round she had done since her first week. Lights, the tap, then the big refrigerator at the back, which held the overnight mix at four degrees and had to stay there. A battery in the door wrote down the temperature every hour, and she changed that battery on the first of every month, whether it complained or not. She kept the old ones in a drawer, which was not required and which she could not explain. A refrigerator that failed at four in the morning was the quiet nightmare of the trade and would frustrate her more than any customer could, and she had thought about it more than once with her hand on the door.

The mix went into the oven at half past three, and while it rose she stood at the bench with the old recipe encyclopedia her mother had left her, which was held together with tape and wrong about two of its pages, and which she trusted anyway for the plain words. The recipe book had been wrong before and she still trusted that encyclopedia more than the chain's list, which was updated by people who had never stood at a bench at three in the morning. A purple tab marked the page for the rye, and a second purple marked the page for the sweet one, and a third purple was stuck to the shelf where the cream went. The purple meant nothing official. It was her own label, for the things she did not want to forget.

The first customer was always the same man, and the second was usually a foreign student from the language school around the corner, who came in for a snack and stayed to practise his English on her. That side of foreign never once left Ilse frustrated, though the paperwork did, and the paperwork always did, and a machine that would not read a name could frustrate anyone, because the student's card wanted a name in letters that the machine would not read. Ilse gave him a snack and let him sit by the window with his book, and she gave the man at the door a second snack for the road. She had learned more foreign from that window than from any course.

At seven the chain's man came down with his evaluation form, which he called an evaluation of the shop's morning standard. His bias was toward numbers: how many loaves sold, how long each customer waited, how often the oven failed. The form had a bias built into it, and that bias favoured the shop that looked like the other forty. Ilse had a bias of her own, toward the customer who came back the next morning, and no box on the form asked for that. Neither of them had numbers to settle it. They drank coffee and agreed to disagree, and then he asked for further changes anyway. There was one further thing, which was the sign above the door, and further down the page there was a line for her signature, so she signed it.

The first change was a tutorial. Every new baker would watch a short video about the bench and then repeat the steps back to her, and the chain would keep a record of it. Ilse had trained six bakers in fourteen years without a video, and all six had learned the bench in a week. She said so. The chain's man said the shops required consistency across forty buildings, and that consistency was the whole point of the tutorial, and the consistency of the tutorial mattered more to him than the bread did. There was a constraint he did not say out loud, which was money, and she heard it anyway and let it pass. The constraint was not cruel. It came from the shape of the company, and it reached as far as the flour shelf on the back wall, and she had lived inside that constraint for six years.

Her hands were the part of the job that had a cost. The doctor had written her a prescription for a cream for the cracks, and she had carried the prescription in her coat pocket for a month before she filled it, and she kept the tube in the drawer next to the spare batteries, and she read the label the way she read the encyclopedia at the bench, for the plain words, and the prescription had said twice a day and she used it once. The medicine had changed its name twice since she started and the cream had not, and she had used the same medicine through both names. She used it at the end of the shift, when the last tray was out and the bench was wiped, and she used it slowly, because a hand that healed too fast would only be asked to do more, and the medicine worked better when she was not in a hurry.

At nine she made the closing arrangements, and she wrote her own evaluation of the shift in the book. She wrote them on paper, the same way her mother had, and she left the arrangements for the morning girl on the bench, and the arrangements were the same as yesterday's and she liked that. The refrigerator got its two lines in the book, the battery got a date, and the bench got a wipe. She put the day's forms and the tutorial notes into her briefcase, which was older than the chain man's and held more, and whose clasp she had never fixed.

On the walk home she thought about Mrs. Vogel's theory and the student at the window and the video she had not watched yet. A bakery is a set of small arrangements that have to be made again every night. The consistency of it is not a personality. It is a decision she makes at three o'clock whether or not the week has been kind. She would watch the video before she complained about it, and the purple tab would stay on the rye page either way. The nightmare of a dead refrigerator would follow her into sleep, and she did not mind that, because she had checked the door twice. The other nightmare, the one about the oven, had not come back in years.
