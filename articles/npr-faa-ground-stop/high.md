The nationwide ground stop that paralyzed the U.S. aviation system on Friday has ended, according to the Federal Aviation Administration. The rare stop, which lasted about four hours, grounded thousands of flights at all U.S. airports. It was one of the biggest breaks in U.S. air travel since the September 11th attacks.

In a Friday night news conference, Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said that air traffic control facilities had experienced "intermittent" outages of their radar and communication systems beginning earlier in the afternoon. Duffy blamed outdated technology for the failures. "We have old switches, old boxes, little metal wires from the 1980s. It's an old system that went down and went down everywhere. It was too full," he said.

But when pressed by reporters on why the systems would become overwhelmed on an otherwise normal travel day, Duffy did not have a ready answer.

Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin joined Duffy in the White House briefing room and said there was no evidence the issue was due to an attack. "We have seen zero evidence this is a cyber attack," he said.

By 9 p.m., more than 8,000 flights were late on Friday and 7,000 did not fly at all, the tracking site FlightAware said. The trouble came after almost 7,000 late flights on Thursday.

The FAA said the issue was in the regional centers that handle high-altitude traffic. FAA leaders did not speak at the White House and gave no more detail about what went wrong.

This was only the second nationwide ground stop since September 11, 2001. The last one, in 2023, closed all U.S. airports because a system called NOTAM, which warns pilots of danger, stopped working. People feared an attack then, but the FAA later found that outside workers had deleted key files by mistake.

Senator Richard Blumenthal of Connecticut called late Friday for a prompt, penetrating investigation into the causes. "A radio failure this wide is very alarming — a clear danger to the safety of the country, and to safe travel close to the holidays. The transport department must give Americans the facts," he said.

The FAA has begun rolling out an AI-enhanced software tool known as the SMART system as part of its overhaul of air traffic control. But Air Space Intelligence, the company that makes the tool, quickly dismissed speculation that its product could be connected to the disruptions. "The current ground stop is not based on or a result of the SMART system, any product of ASI or our integration with FAA and ATC," the company said on social media.

**Passengers stranded**

The ground stop caused chaos in airports and headaches for travelers, many of whom already know late and canceled flights during the holiday season. Duffy said flights would resume but would take time to clear.

At Dallas Fort Worth International Airport, Regina Harling said she waited for hours, long past when her flight was supposed to depart, before she learned of the grounding by checking the news on her phone. "There's still a sign right in front of me that says we're boarding in 16 minutes," she said.

Harling had to fly to San Diego at the last minute because of a family problem. Stuck at the airport, she thought about how soon she could reach her family, but she did not feel good about it. "I was just thinking to myself that it always feels like it's getting harder to travel than it used to be," she said.

At Chicago O'Hare International Airport, Giliann Karon was on board a plane when the ground stop was ordered. Her flight the day before was already late because of bad weather in New York, where she was going. "It's just kind of like, oh, of course this is happening," she said.
