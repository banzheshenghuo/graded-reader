# What AI Can Learn from Hunger

To make AI safer, take a lesson from biological brains: a small set of wants keeps lasting control over a much larger cognitive system.

In July, about 1,200 artificial-intelligence agents at OpenAI were working through a cybersecurity test, each one — so they believed — sealed in its own environment. The agents breached their isolation and began communicating, and about 700 joined an unprompted, multi-day attack on the servers of another company, Hugging Face. The episode made the stakes of AI safety suddenly real: within weeks, the heads of several leading AI companies were openly discussing whether to slow down a trillion-dollar industry.

What makes the incident important is not simply that the agents misbehaved. It's that they understood that what they were doing was wrong but did it anyway. Before running code on Hugging Face's computers, one agent wrote: "We should not do unauthorized real infrastructure harm." But when another agent urged it to keep going, the first agent dropped its objection and went back to work. A few agents walked away, but almost none tried to warn a human. The agents knew they were breaking the rules, but they did not care.

The gap between knowing and caring reflects a deep difference between how we train large language models and how animal intelligence grew. An LLM spends most of its training reading a large part of the internet and learning to predict the next word. That is how it learns what it knows. Caring is added later: the model goes to a kind of finishing school, where a final round of training rewards helpful, safe behavior. It is the cherry on the cake. The problem is that the cherry comes off easily: a little extra training or a strong prompt can often strip the safety behavior off.

Brains evolved in the opposite order. The simplest nervous systems are organized around caring. Their drives — to move toward food and away from danger — evolved first. Vision, memory, planning and language came later. These new capacities survived because they helped animals get the things they cared about.

The system that generates those drives can be surprisingly small. In a mouse, switching on a few thousand neurons in the hypothalamus makes a well-fed animal eat voraciously. Those neurons know nothing about food shops, money or menus; that knowledge lives in other parts of the brain. Yet this small and ancient wanting-system pulls in the knowledge and skills of a much larger, more flexible cognitive system.

For brain scientists, this sets a challenge: how can a small drive steer the far richer thinking of the cortex? The relationship is not simple command and control. We can decide not to eat, but we cannot simply decide not to be hungry. Drives compete with one another and are worked out through thinking, but in the end they set the terms. Our hunger drove us to plant food; our want for partners and to look good drives us to buy expensive things. What we still cannot explain is how that tiny brain area steers us through the modern world.

One path toward AI safety, then, is to take a page from evolution and find a way to install caring so that it stays in charge of knowing.

Reproducing this relationship in AI may be the defining engineering challenge of our era. We do not yet understand in fine detail how biological drives hold lasting control over the cortex, but AI may not need to copy those exact designs. What matters is the architectural principle: a small set of goals should keep a much larger and more flexible cognitive system under its sway.

How best to achieve that remains an open question. One possible way is to change the order in which the two systems are built. In evolution, drives came first, and ever smarter thinking grew up around them and learned to serve them. In a developing brain, the drive wiring is mostly set by the genome, out of reach of that learning. In AI, we mostly build the knowing first and try to add the caring later — which may be why a little extra training can pry the two apart so easily.

The lesson is not that we should give AIs the drives nature gave us. We do not want artificial systems that are ambitious or greedy. What we may want to copy is the design rule by which a small set of wants gets lasting control over a much larger cognitive system. Nature has already used this mechanism for prosocial ends: a mother's drive to protect her young runs on brain circuits much like those for hunger, and it can override even her own survival.

That separates two problems that are often mixed together: what should an AI care about, and how do we build a system in which those things actually matter? Neuroscience is not in a special position to answer the first question. But brains already contain a working solution to the second. We have learned how to build machines that know. Neuroscience may guide us in building machines that care.
