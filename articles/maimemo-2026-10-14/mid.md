# Cream Day

Wednesday morning, I took the briefcase out again. It has one broken zipper and a smell of old coffee, and it has carried my work through every job I have had. Grandma was already in the kitchen, making her arrangements for the day like a secretary with one good pen: soup at noon, a walk before dark, and at four o'clock, she said, the world would end or begin, depending on how much cream a small boy could handle.

At the office, the day was pipelines. A pipeline, for those lucky enough not to know, is a row of machines that pass your code along like buckets at a fire, and when one machine is slow, the whole row becomes a bottleneck and everyone stands and stares. The scope of my morning was one slow response from a widget nobody remembered writing. The widget has no intelligence; neither do its error messages. It had frustrated three people before lunch, who complained in writing. I touched up the script, fixed the schema, integrated the new column, and by noon the page rendered fast, the report exports clean, and the morning and noon numbers agreed with machine consistency. Nobody wanted to bring up the four o'clock meeting at stand-up, so I said it anyway, and my departure at three raised exactly one eyebrow. My calendar showed availability for one more task; the task was a scoop of cream, the constraint was one, not two, and the new model of the week, which is neural, expensive, and wrong about Tuesdays, could keep its opinion.

My approach to the afternoon was written in a specification of one line, and the second line was implicit: one scoop, not two, and do not argue. The constraint was medical, and the rest of Grandma's rules are implicit: cream after fruit, walk before dark, no arguing with doctors. Her list said so in red ink, which is her color for laws.

The subway home is eight minutes by the metro map and nine by the clock, and I spent all eight being nervous, in front of an advertisement for airport shuttles and a cartoon moon with a face, smiling down on a round green world. At 3:58 I stood at our door. At 4:00 exactly, from the kitchen, the little flag in the data said true, boolean and proud. The refrigerator in the purple kitchen, the one room Grandma repainted years ago and defends like a border, reported one opening, four degrees, all normal. It was not an anomaly. It was medicine, arriving on time.

Bobby was in his chair, bib on, spoon in fist. His second ice cream, his first legal one, arrived in a small bowl: soft, cold, allowed. He looked at it the way you look at a former enemy who is now paying your rent. His response was immediate and total. He ate one spoon, slow, then faster, then held the bowl up with both hands for inspection, and the face he made gave him away completely: best thing that had ever happened to anyone. One scoop is never enough and always right. When it was gone he said "Done," his word for everything. We had been scared of crying; we got a suggestion instead, from Grandma, a spoon of yogurt and a slice of banana, and he shared the banana with me, which is his newest skill and mine. No stomachache followed. We watched for one anyway, and thirsty work it was, so water all around.

"After this," said Grandma, "we walk. The doctor said fresh air. I checked the availability of the whole family the way a secretary checks a room: everyone is free at five." She combed her hair once with her fingers, looked at herself in the glass by the door, approved of what she saw, and put on her blue dress, the one she saves for occasions. Bobby had new sneakers, white, already dirty at the toes, because he had practiced walking in them all afternoon on the carpet. Practice, she believes, is the difference between shoes and shoes.

The evening was the best October can do. The trees along our street had turned red at the edges, the smell of cut grass still hung in the air from the weekend, and the sky was doing its slow purple-to-navy change. A late flight of birds crossed over, unhurried. At the corner, past the old shop with the fish sign that Grandma calls our landmark, a police car rolled by, and the officer raised two fingers off the wheel to her. She calls every officer "sir" or "young man," and he answered, with the charm of a man who likes his job, because she does nothing halfway. He was helping a foreign couple with a map when we passed, patiently, twice, in an accent that made the directions sound like a song; two more foreign tourists stopped, listened, and followed his hand like a choir.

The queue at the corner shop was the evening's only bottleneck, and we did not join it. At the park, the swing stood empty and waiting, but unfortunately Thursday is the swing day, and Wednesday is not Thursday, whatever your opinion. Bobby pointed at it, stated his case with one long gesture, and lost. He accepted the verdict with more dignity than I bring to most meetings, small and vulnerable and reasonable about it. The verdict frustrated him for exactly one second. We played on the grass instead: the ball, too big to throw, too loved to leave. Head down, he chased it, bumped it with his foot, sat down, and conducted his band from the stroller, all three instruments at once, drum and bell in a hybrid row, unsupervised music with total confidence. Three instruments, one conductor, no sheet.

The moon came up while he watched. It was round and low and orange at first, then white, then higher and smaller, as moons do when they decide to be professional. A star or two arrived, no competition. Bobby pointed at the moon with his whole arm, and I said the word, and he said something back that was almost the word, and for one second the park was exactly the right size.

Hunger drove us home before the moon was high. He walked the last stretch on his new sneakers, supervised at every step, slowly, like a man in no hurry to end a good evening, and I carried nothing but the ball and the empty snack bowl. My legs still remembered yesterday's gym; the bathroom scale will deliver its report on Friday, and I have decided to be out. The walk gave me time to count what the day had cost: one scoop of cream, one banana, one park. Grandma keeps a budget for Sundays and one for holidays, but she does not complain about evenings like this; they are free, which is her way of rounding up joy.

At home, Grandma's arrangements held to the minute, and the pipeline of the evening ran on schedule, with the consistency of a good habit and the punctuality of a tutorial that finally works. Shrimp for dinner, three of them, counted. Bath, pajamas, the last spoon of the prescription, which he swallowed like a professional, tongue out, not even a sour face, and I rinsed the spoon at the sink while Grandma found her place in the book. The encyclopedia had news: volume B had arrived, one week early, because she could not wait either. Bobby sat on my lap, freshly sleepy, and heard the encyclopedia's bear page as a bedtime story, twice, the way Grandma reads an encyclopedia and the way I read a tutorial: out loud, and again. "The bear," she read, "sleeps through the winter." Bobby looked at the tiger sitting next to the rabbit in his crib, considered his options, and put the tiger in the crib too, one colleague more for the winter.

There was one small emergency at bedtime, the good kind: Bobby lost his grip on the tiger over the crib rail, and the tiger was rescued, and everyone survived. No other emergency tonight. The data sheet from the purple kitchen's refrigerator is flat and boring, not one anomaly: protocol one counts the cold, protocol two counts the doors, the home server's gateway uploads the night to the cloud, and both of them, in a database somewhere, report a family that ate one scoop of cream and slept on time. The machine has no intelligence, which is why we trust it. My own pipeline runs without me in a data center across the river, one machine handing a bucket to the next, and I am here, not there to watch it, because my briefcase is by the door, my son is in his crib, and the moon, professional now, keeps the late shift for both of us.
