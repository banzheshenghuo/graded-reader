# The New Refrigerator

On Tuesday night our old refrigerator made a new sound. It hummed for a minute, then it stopped, then it started again, and that small anomaly drove me out of bed at three. In the morning the milk was only a little cold, and the butter felt soft. The warm milk was a second anomaly, so after Bobby's stomachache last week, we did not want to take a risk with food. I called my mother, and she said one sentence: "That machine is twenty years old. It is tired." So we decided to upgrade the kitchen.

Grandma arrived the next morning with her purple cloth bag and her small thick notebook. Twenty years as a school secretary gave her one habit: before you buy a thing, you write the numbers down. She measured the old fridge with a cloth tape, and then she measured the door and the space next to the wall. Her questions to the shops were specific: width, depth, power, price. After that she called three local shops and filled one page with numbers. She even rolled the bathroom scale against the old machine, because the new one must sit on the same floor. There was an implicit rule in her method: measure twice, buy once. I skipped my gym hour that morning, because nobody argues with the notebook.

On Saturday we took the subway to the shop near the park. That shop is a famous old landmark in our part of the city, because my father bought our first television there forty years ago. Grandma told me the story of that day on the train. A young salesman met us at the door, and he spoke with a foreign accent. He was proud of the middle model, a foreign brand with a small screen on its door. "This one is a hybrid," he said, "half refrigerator and half computer. There is intelligence inside it." The screen could show a shopping list, and it had a red button for emergency freezing. He gave us one sheet of paper with the specifications, and Grandma read every line. The salesman had a lot of charm, but the price still fell after her third question. She asked about it twice, and then she complained once, with a small smile, because complaining is her way of saying hello. Unfortunately, the cheapest model was small and weak, so we chose the middle one, because its freezer was big enough for Bobby's peas. The delivery availability was Monday or Thursday, and Grandma picked Monday; she was right, as usual.

Two men brought it up on Monday at three. "Sir, where does it go?" the younger one asked. The kitchen, I said, fourth floor, no lift. They looked at each other, and then they looked at the stairs. Our stairs are narrow, and the corner between the third floor and the fourth is the bottleneck of the whole building. The young man went first, and the older man followed; their approach to the corner was slow and exact. The stairs were busy that afternoon, and they held everyone up for ten minutes, because nobody could pass. A neighbor waited at the corner with his bicycle, and he did not complain. He kept the implicit rule of old buildings: never rush a man who carries something heavy. The older man wiped his head with a cloth and rolled one sleeve up. Near our door the box bumped the frame, and I was nervous for the wood, but the paint was fine. Bobby jumped in my arms at their shout; he had been scared of the loud corner, but he laughed soon after. I held him away from the rail, since a small child is vulnerable near heavy boxes. "Done," the older man said at the door, and he shook my hand.

Before the new machine could move in, the old one had to leave. It had worked right here for twenty years. First we emptied it. There was more inside than I remembered: two jars of jam, half a cabbage, and the last small bottle of sour medicine from Bobby's prescription. We threw the cabbage away, because it smelled old and tired. Bobby dug the last wooden spoon out of the drawer and kept it. The ice from the freezer went into bowls of water, and the cold water ran down the sink. After that the two men wrapped the old machine in a blanket and carried it away, down four flights of stairs. They gave it away to the guard of our building, who repairs old machines and sells them. We watched its departure from the window, and the kitchen suddenly looked bigger, white and empty.

I plugged the new machine in at four, and then came the screen. The screen asked for an account, then for a password, then for the long number printed inside the door. Unfortunately, the app asks for a new password every month, and that practice frustrated me more than the price did. The app on my phone argued with the refrigerator for twenty minutes about whether they knew each other, and the account screen was the real bottleneck of the setup. When they finally agreed on a protocol, the app rendered its first picture: one line for the freezer, one line for the door. The delivery page had looked like the board at an airport, and this picture was the same kind of art: slow, serious, and almost flat. Both lines held a flat, healthy consistency.

While the app updated itself, I read its settings, because reading settings is the bad habit of my job. The refrigerator uploads its temperature to the company server every hour, and a database somewhere keeps it, with a schema I would enjoy drawing. The table under the lines has seven columns, and one column, "energy today", says zero every day. Every option is a boolean: on, or off, and nothing between. The app has forty features, and I use two of them. I exported the shopping list to my phone once, and it arrived as a clean text file. It can also integrate with our singing speaker, so now the speaker knows how cold the milk is. A small white gateway box came with the machine, and the fridge talks to the internet through it. The app also watches the door; the kitchen is never unsupervised anymore. The tutorial for all this runs eleven minutes, and there is a second tutorial about the door seals. The salesman used the word intelligence twice in the shop, and the settings menu uses it once more. The top model has a camera that uses a neural model to name your vegetables; ours has no camera, and we name them ourselves. Every hour of cold becomes one number, and the week becomes a long vector of them. At work that morning, a script in my repository had failed. I changed one variable and merged the repair before lunch, and the small failure frustrated me for the rest of the morning.

That evening Bobby ate his noodles with his bib on, and he was thirsty after them, so I gave him cold water from the new door. The doctor said no ice cream for one week, and the week ended on Sunday, so he got his first ice cream since the illness. The cream was cold on his tongue; he swallowed the first spoon with wide eyes, made his clear gesture for more, and got a second one. Later he stood in front of the shiny door and looked at himself in the metal, and he touched the face there, and the face touched him back. He threw his red ball at the door, and the ball rolled back to him, so they played this game nine times. After the game, he put his purple sticker on the door, low, where his hand could reach it. Then he gave the fridge a short check-up with his toy instruments, drum first, because that is how doctors work now. After the check-up, the instruments went back into their basket, one by one. For one unsupervised minute, he also fed half a biscuit to the tiger, and nobody saw it but me. Grandma, still a secretary in her habits, wrote the shop's phone number on the last page of her notebook. Then she put one volume of her encyclopedia on top of the machine, to test the shelf. The shelf passed, and the encyclopedia has not moved since. The doctor's word for Bobby's food was consistency, and the new machine delivers it quietly. Before his bath, he bumped his forehead against the door, gently, once.

At nine Bobby was sleepy, so he carried the tiger to his crib by himself. I put the paper bear at the opposite end, because the tiger needs space when he is tired. I rinsed the bowls and the spoon while Grandma fixed her hair with her comb. Her plants on the balcony had their evening water, and after that she said good night to the kitchen and went home.

On Tuesday morning the grass in the park was wet, and a slow bee sat on the swing chain. Bobby pointed at the swing, I lifted him onto it, and he held the chains with both hands. His courage grew with every swing, and I counted eleven of them. When we came home, the refrigerator was quiet, and the milk was cold.
