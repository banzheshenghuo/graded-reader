# The AI Double Hit: Two Bad Things at Once?

Here is a worry from a money writer: What if the AI boom breaks — and that break makes companies replace workers with AI even faster? Two bad things hitting at the same time.

## Bad Thing One: The Boom Could Break

When a new technology arrives, people throw money at it. They even use money that isn't theirs to buy more. But if the technology turns out to be worth less than everyone hoped, prices fall, the borrowed money must still be paid back, and the whole economy can get sick.

This has happened many times before. A famous book about money disasters, first published in 1978, found the same pattern over centuries. One old example: the first railways. People went crazy for train companies, the bubble broke, and in 1873 the fall hurt the whole country.

Today, AI companies hold a huge piece of the U.S. stock market. One big bank found that just 42 AI companies produced about two thirds of the market's growth since early 2024. And building their computer centers takes hundreds of billions.

It might all end well — or it might not.

## Bad Thing Two: Bad Times Speed Up Job Losses

Here is the surprising part. You would think companies replace workers with machines slowly, year by year. But research says no: it happens in sudden jumps, and those jumps come mostly during bad economic times.

One study looked at American jobs between 1990 and 2017. It found that 88% of the job losses in jobs that machines could do happened during — or right around — the bad economic periods.

So here is the double hit: if the AI boom breaks, the economy gets sick, and companies cut costs by replacing workers with the cheap AI tools the boom left behind. Investors lose their money, and workers lose their jobs. Two hits at once.

## It Might Not Happen

The writer is honest: nobody knows. Maybe AI really does change the world without breaking. Maybe companies will earn back all that money. There are also ways to make the hit lighter — like special pay for people who lose jobs, or new taxes on AI to help pay for it.

Still, the writer says, putting these worries on paper felt good.
