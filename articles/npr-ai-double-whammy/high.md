# What if an AI Bubble Bursts — and That Accelerates AI Job Losses?

What if the AI bubble bursts and the sick economy that follows speeds up the handover of workers' jobs to AI? Call it the AI double whammy.

The writer has long doubted the sweeping claims about how AI will remake the world. But as the technology races ahead, one worry keeps growing: what it could mean for the ways we earn our living.

Two research results feed this worry. One is the history of financial bubbles. The other is a surprising relationship between recessions and machines doing human work.

## First: The Bubbles

In 1978, Charles Kindleberger published his famous book Manias, Panics, and Crashes, building on Hyman Minsky's ideas. Across whole centuries, he kept finding the same pattern.

A bubble often starts with a big possible change to the economy — a world-changing technology promising riches. People who put money in have real reasons to be excited. Then they get too excited: they put huge money down as if the sky were the limit, and they borrow to make even bigger bets, feeding the boom. The debt becomes a ticking time bomb. When reality sets in, prices fall but the debts remain; those who owe struggle, those who lent take losses, and a market crash can spiral into a crisis and a painful recession.

The key insight: a technology can be truly world-changing and still fuel a bubble that ends in a crash. The railroads did transform the world — and their fall triggered the Panic of 1873, whose troubles fed a people's rising and the first big immigration crackdown in American history.

Today AI is in effect holding up the U.S. stock market. A J.P. Morgan analysis this month found that just 42 AI companies accounted for around two thirds of the S&P 500's gains since early 2024 — while they borrow hundreds of billions of dollars to pay for data centers and the energy to power them.

None of this proves we're in a bubble. The first-internet bust brought only a light recession — borrowed money played a smaller role in that boom, which limited the damage, and its fall did not break the whole system the way the 2008 housing bust did.

Even without a crash, though, there could be a painful day of truth. Will these companies ever earn enough to cover what they borrowed? The technology watcher Benedict Evans has warned that fierce competition between companies offering much the same AI models could drive prices — and profits — down, even as they spend ungodly money building them.

## Second: Recessions Speed Up Automation

If the AI boom breaks, that alone would hurt. But studies add a darker turn: recessions may be the very moment when businesses turn to new technology to replace workers.

We imagine machines taking over work as gradual — technology improves, jobs slowly change. But the research suggests it happens in bursts, and those bursts come together around recessions. Nir Jaimovich and Henry Siu found that between 1990 and 2017, 88% of the fall in jobs open to machines happened during recessions or the months right around them.

Here's the strange irony: fierce price competition could keep AI companies from delivering the profits their backers expect — while making their technology cheaper for other businesses to use against workers. If an AI bust brings a recession, that downturn could accelerate worker replacement. Investors could lose their money, and workers could lose their jobs anyway.

It's a nightmare picture: first the bust smashes retirement savings, then businesses rush to cut costs and many jobs are permanently gone.

## Reasons Not to Panic

To be fair, much is unknown: whether AI will earn back the spending, whether a bust would really bring a deep recession, whether the old pattern will repeat with AI. A sick economy could leave businesses less money to invest in AI — and AI may yet prove better at making workers get more done than at replacing them outright.

Whether AI delivers one whammy, two, or none, policies can limit the damage when workers lose jobs: pay for the jobless, or — as Bill Gates has suggested — taxing AI and robots, making it cheaper to keep employing humans while paying for a stronger safety net.

So yes, this nightmare might never come true, and there are ways to limit the damage if it does. That hasn't stopped the worrying — but writing it all down washed some of it out of the system: getting the fears out of the head and onto the page.
