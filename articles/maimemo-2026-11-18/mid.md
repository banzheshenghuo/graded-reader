# The Suitcase

The suitcase came down from the top of the wardrobe on Wednesday, which is how the week announced that it was about to become a trip. Work wants me overseas for four days, the first time in a year, and Grandma's first act on hearing the news was to send me a list of what not to pack. Luggages, in her view, are a moral document: they show what a person thinks they might need. She has strong opinions about people who pack for the person they wish they were.

I packed badly, as always, and then sat on the floor with the open case and did the honest inventory. Two shirts for four days. One pair of shoes, good for walking and acceptable in a meeting. One pair of socks for every day plus one, which is the only rule I have ever kept. A bag with a toothbrush. A charger that has outlived three phones. Underwear in exactly the quantity my mother once described as "enough, and then one more, in case of a Wednesday." A toy elephant, because the boy will need him, and the boy will not be with me. That last item took the longest to pack.

The souvenir problem came up at nine. I am going to a city I have never seen, and I am expected, by some unwritten law of the office, to bring something back for the team. Grandma's position is that souvenirs are a waste of luggage weight, and that the only honest souvenir is a photograph. My colleague's position, memorised from years of exchange, is that a terrible pen with the city's name on it is worth more politically than a good gift that requires thought. Both are right. I packed neither, and will decide at the airport, which is where all my worst decisions are made in the most efficient way possible.

The concert season has begun at the hall across the river, and the first programme is something with a famous name in it, and my wife wants to go, and I want to go, and we have agreed to go on the Saturday after I come back. It is the kind of plan that sounds like a nice idea and then turns out to be the week's best evening. That is the fitting thing about plans made in advance: they arrive whether or not the week has been kind.

Bobby, meanwhile, is learning the word for the bag. He points at it and says a syllable that is nearly luggage, and then he tries to climb into it, which is a reasonable theory of travel from a person his size. I let him sit in the open case for four minutes while I found the socks. He filled it, entirely, and looked around for more room, and then looked at me as if I had built it wrong. He may be right. Forty litres is a small country.

The packing proper happened after he was asleep, which is the only honest hour for it, and it came with an audience of one: the family secretary, on the phone, going down her list. Do not forget the charger, she said. I have three. Then one good one. She has run four-day trips for other people for thirty years and has never once been wrong about the socks, and she has a theory, one of her several theories, that a person who forgets socks will forget something important within two years. I do not know what the evidence is. I have not asked, because I would rather keep the socks.

I also had a prescription to collect, which is not a sentence I expected to write this year, and a medicine bag to assemble for the four days, and a small snack for the train, which turned out to be the same snack I always buy and which Grandma criticised, mildly, before telling me to take two. Her evaluation of my packing, delivered at ten past ten and based entirely on a description over the phone, was: acceptable, with a bias toward optimism. The nightmare I had last week — the one about arriving somewhere with no shoes — she said is a normal dream for a man in my line of work, and then she said the useful thing, which is that the way to avoid it is to pack the shoes first. I put in the shirts, the shoes, the socks, the socks, the socks. I put in the charger with its terrible cable. I put in the notebook and the good pen. And then I put in the elephant, and I took the elephant out, and I put it back, and I took a photograph of the elephant in the suitcase, and sent it to my wife, who was in the next room, and who replied with a single word that I am not going to repeat because it would embarrass us both.

The router blinked in the corner and the server hummed in its small closet and the refrigerator held its forty degrees, which is the right number for a fridge and the wrong number for a suitcase. I mention this because the flat has a temperature and the flat keeps it, and I am about to leave the flat for four days and it will keep it without me, and that is the arrangement. The battery on the balcony sensor is a year and a half old now, which is older than the age limit on the packaging, and it has never once been the reason anything went wrong, and I have decided not to think about it until it is.

I called my colleague about the trip and he complained, briefly and accurately, about the conference hotels, and then he told me the thing that mattered: the console booking system had been down all morning, so the tickets were being issued by hand, and mine was a photocopy of a photocopy. That is the small nightmare of every trip: the document that looks like a document and is not one. He said he would debug the booking flow when he got back, and that the failure was probably a variable in a template nobody had touched since spring. Then he told me to drink water on the plane.

There is a hole in the lining of the suitcase, small, in the corner, and I found it while looking for the charger, and I have known about it for two years and done nothing. Tonight I did something, which is that I packed a spare cable so that the hole never becomes a story. This is, I think, the whole art of packing: not to fix everything, but to arrange the week so that the small broken things do not meet the important ones.

There is one more arrangement I should record, because it will look strange later. The purple scarf, the one I never wear and cannot throw away, is going in the suitcase, at the bottom, under everything. Not because I will need it. Because if the trip turns into a further complication, a missed connection or a hotel with no room, I will open the bag and see it and remember that somebody once gave it to me, and that will be worth more than the space it takes. The encyclopedia of a good trip, if it were ever written, would have one page, and this would be on it.

The last thing in was the book about the mountain, because the boy will ask for it, and I will be able to say I read it on a train, and the page about the bear will be the page he asks about first. I marked it with a boarding pass from the last trip, which I found in the inside pocket, which is where boarding passes go to be forgotten. It said a date in March. The bear has been waiting in that suitcase since March, which is a long time for a bear, and I am going to read him on Thursday, in a city I have never seen, and on Thursday I will notice that I am the sort of man who carries a bear in a suitcase and a hole in the lining, and that both are deliberate.

Before bed I did the last round, the one I always do the night before a trip, and it is the closest thing this flat has to a tutorial in leaving. The refrigerator at forty degrees, the router light steady, the battery on the balcony reporting, the kitchen guard counting doors that will open two hundred fewer times this week. The cream, one scoop, eaten at four as always; the constraint of the house does not travel with me, but the habit of it does. My briefcase is staying home, which for the first time in years is the right decision: this trip takes a suitcase, not a briefcase, and there is a difference, and the difference is that a briefcase has things that can wait in it.

One hypothesis I am not going to test is that nothing will frustrate me on this trip. It will. Something will: a delay, a bad coffee, a foreign plug that does not fit. What I am going to do instead is the thing the visitor told me, and keep the consistency of the ordinary: two words a day, water on the plane, shoes first. He learned that overseas, and he learned it the hard way, and he told me in a doorway; the consistency of the ordinary is the only thing that survives a strange city.

This flat runs on things I never look at. I do not look at the boiler until it complains; I do not look at the elevator until it stops; I subscribe to two things and can name neither. The desk by the window has one drawer I have not opened since we moved in. I know this because the packing turned it up: a hotel keycard, a ticket stub, a menu from a restaurant whose third dish I can still recall, delicious and slightly sour, which is the right way to remember a restaurant. And a photograph of a tour guide at a museum, mid-sentence, wrong about the painter, entirely right about the room.

What I keep from all of this, in the end, is a trade-off I did not think I was making: I am trading four days of my son's life for four days of work. I recall saying, at the beginning, that it was the first time in a year, as if the rarity made it cheap. It does not. The scopes of the two things are not comparable, which is the whole difficulty, and the answer is not to skip the trip but to land in it — to be there, wholly, on the day, and then to come home on Sunday and be wholly here. Any animation of the laptop screen this week is somebody else's Tuesday. Mine is in a suitcase.

The night before a trip, this family does a small audit, and I am the auditor. The secretary gives the list; I read it back. The prescription is in the side pocket with the medicine bag, and the medicine bag has two strips and a note in her handwriting. The purple scarf is at the bottom of the suitcase, further down than anything useful, where it belongs. The encyclopedia of my son's week will continue without me, at its letter, with somebody else turning the pages. The refrigerator will hold its four degrees for four days, and the battery will report whatever it reports, and nothing about any of this will frustrate me, because I will be somewhere else, being useful in a different building.

Her evaluation of the finished suitcase, when I described it to her at midnight, was two words, and I am quoting: foreign-ready. The constraint of this family is that we do not leave without telling somebody where we are going, and the arrangement of this family is that somebody will always want to know. That is not a small thing to have, at my age, at this hour, with a train at 6:40.

The one-way ticket is booked, for once, as a two-way, coming home Sunday. The variable in the packing this time is not the weather, because I have checked the weather and it is four days of the same grey, and it is not the meetings, because the meetings are already in the calendar with the wrong names in them. The variable is me, and how much I decide to notice. There is a boolean in every trip and it is this: will the bag close. Mine closes, tonight, one hand on the lid, and it closes because I took out the second pair of shoes and kept the purple scarf and put the cream where the cream goes, which is nowhere, because the cream stays here, one scoop, in the refrigerator, under the standing constraint. The tutorial of the suitcase, written by every parent who has ever left for four days, is one line: pack for the person you are, not the person you might be. My bias is toward the other one, the imagined better traveller with the better pen, and tonight I packed for the real one, and the real one is going to be fine.

The train leaves at 6:40 and the boy will be asleep, and I will not wake him, and he will find the suitcase gone in the morning and will look for the elephant first, and then for me, in that order, which is correct.
