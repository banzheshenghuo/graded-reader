Diane Lipscombe is a professor of neuroscience at Brown University and former president of the Society for Neuroscience. Graduate education has always interested her, she says, because it is a central part of research at universities and of their teaching.

She has spent many years running training grants, and in 2005 she helped start a shared graduate program between Brown and the U.S. National Institutes of Health. That history gives her a useful view at a bad moment: in the last two years, big cuts in government money have made many programs more careful about taking students, and teachers less sure they can pay for a student for all the years of a Ph.D.

The Transmitter spoke with her about how she thinks about graduate education, the trends that worry her, and her advice for new students. This interview has been cut for length and made clearer.

**The Transmitter: Money and rules have changed a lot. Is this a good time to think again about what a Ph.D. is for?**

Diane Lipscombe: It is very important to bring new voices and earlier-stage scientists into the system. They are bright, creative and innovative. Every new generation of scientists is better than the one before, so it would be very sad to stop new people coming in.

Whose job is it? Everyone's. Universities must keep giving students the chance to go deep into one subject. But the people who pay for science share the job, because you want to pay for the best science, and that is only sustained if young scientists keep coming in.

**TT: What trends do you see?**

DL: AI, for sure: how to use it, how not to use it, what it all means. The way you ask a question decides the output. The basic thinking is the same as before, data in and analysis out, but the fine parts matter more now.

Then there is integration across levels of analysis. In brain science it has been hard to go from the molecular level to a cognitive one, but I am hopeful about new ways of doing it. We all want what the human genome project did, joining so many levels at once. As people who train students, we should ask where the gaps are.

**TT: What are your biggest concerns?**

DL: How long a Ph.D. takes is much discussed. The plan is five years, but many students take more. There can be good reasons, and those students can become brilliant scientists. Still, there must be a safety net, so that going past five or six years is really best for the student.

I also worry about programs that want a second, higher degree before students join. In the U.S. students pay for that degree, and many such programs do not give aid based on need. If that becomes the rule, you shut out a huge number of students who cannot pay. That would be a big step back.

**TT: Applications from international students have fallen. Is that a concern?**

DL: We are at the start of that trend, and it worries me a lot. It is a real danger to the future of research in the U.S. Applications from students abroad to second-degree programs fell by about 40 percent between 2025 and 2026. Many of us feel we can do nothing.

**TT: You have said T32 programs had a profound effect. Why?**

DL: I am a big fan. They are one of the best tools the NIH has for training graduate students, and the public money is small for the effect it has, about $50,000 per student each year.

The effect is not only for the students who get the money. The push for quality spreads and reaches a much bigger group. Most programs let students work in two or three labs, and each student has a group of teachers, not just one guide. Because results are reported all the time, good habits spread to other universities.

The bad part is that T32 money can only go to U.S. citizens or people who live there for good. Students from abroad can join the group, but finding money for them is a gap.

**TT: Is the graduate degree losing its appeal?**

DL: Many graduate students have lost their belief in this path. That is natural when public support is suddenly questioned. Inside universities people worry that no one can plan ahead. Given a clear road and a road that is not clear, students will ask hard questions about their future. That is a big risk now.

**TT: Any words of wisdom for incoming students?**

DL: Keep going after what you love in brain science. There is no more exciting field, and it can improve health. Concerns about funding are real, but historically the money has always come back. Stick to your dream.
