Graduate education has long interested Diane Lipscombe, professor of neuroscience at Brown University, director of the Carney Institute for Brain Science and former president of the Society for Neuroscience. "I've always felt it's an incredibly important part of academic research and the educational mission of universities," she says.

Lipscombe has spent decades running many predoctoral training grants, and in 2005 she helped start a joint graduate program between Brown and the U.S. National Institutes of Health. That history gives her a useful view at a hard moment for graduate training: big federal funding cuts over the past two years have made many programs more cautious about admissions, and faculty less sure they can support new students for the length of a Ph.D.

Those pressures raise bigger questions about what graduate education is for and how best to keep it going. Lipscombe, who has won many awards for teaching and mentorship, spoke with The Transmitter about her thinking, the trends she sees, and her advice for new students. This interview has been edited for length and clarity.

**The Transmitter: Graduate programs in the United States are changing fast. Is now a good time to revisit the purpose of a Ph.D.?**

Diane Lipscombe: At heart, it is very important to bring new voices and early-career scientists into the system. They are bright, really creative, full of ideas. Every new generation of scientists is better than the last, so it would be very sad to stop new people coming in.

Whose responsibility is it? I think it's collective. Universities must continue to give graduate students the opportunity to dig deep into a particular discipline. But the funding agencies share the job, because you want to fund the best science, and that is only sustained if early-career scientists keep coming in with really exciting ideas. So it is a continual renewal of ideas and new knowledge in the field. I see no other way.

**TT: What trends are you seeing in graduate education?**

DL: Certainly AI: how to use it, how not to use it, what it means. The way you ask a question will decide the answer. It's the same kind of thinking, data in and analysis out, but the fine parts matter more now.

Then there's joining levels of study. It has been very hard in brain science to go from tiny molecules to a study of how the whole mind works, but I am hopeful and excited about new ways of doing that. We are all envious of the human genome project, which joined so many levels at once. We live on data, so which methods do we still lack that would let us measure rich data? That is another area where, as graduate educators, we should be asking the big questions about where the gaps are.

**TT: What are some of the biggest concerns in graduate education right now?**

DL: There's a lot of talk about how long a degree takes. The plan is five years, but many students take longer. There can be really good reasons, and those students can turn out to be great scientists. But we should think about it, because a student must be working toward something useful if they stay past five or six years. There must be a safety net around every student, so that staying longer is really best for them.

I also worry about programs that want students to hold a second, higher degree first. In the U.S. students pay for that degree, and many such programs are not need-blind. If that becomes the rule, you shut out a huge number of students who cannot pay. That would be a big step back. Graduate school is usually paid for by the university. You don't want to put a money wall in front of students entering a Ph.D.

**TT: One issue we identified in our survey was a decrease in applications from international students.**

DL: I think we're at the beginning of that trend, and it's very concerning. It's a real risk, even a threat, to the future of scientific research in the U.S. I was just looking at Council of Graduate Schools numbers: applications to master's programs from international students dropped by about 40 percent between 2025 and 2026. It's a big drop, and I think many of us feel a little powerless at this point.

**TT: You've said T32 programs have had a profound effect by creating a more student-centric model.**

DL: I'm a huge fan. I've been involved with them for a few decades, and I think they're one of the most important mechanisms the NIH has for supporting graduate student development and training. The public investment is pretty small, relative to impact: probably around $50,000 per student per year, give or take.

The impact isn't just for the students directly supported. The focus on quality within those programs acts as an amplifier of quality generally, so it reaches a much bigger group of students indirectly. Even if an institution doesn't have a T32, it's probably going to introduce some of those features, because it makes them more competitive for students.

Most of the programs encourage some kind of rotation, so students can do research in a couple of different labs rather than going straight into one. Students have a committee of multiple faculty around them, not just their adviser. They meet regularly, give presentations and get input from other faculty. And because you're constantly reporting out on outcomes and on the students' experience, you hear from faculty at other universities and think, "Oh, we should be doing that." So best practices get shared.

The downside is that T32s can only go to students who are permanent residents or citizens. International students can benefit from the cohort model if they're in the program, but finding support for them is a gap.

**TT: Do you think the graduate degree is losing its appeal?**

DL: There's a lot of disillusionment among graduate students. I think that's natural when people have been very committed to supporting graduate education through public funds, and suddenly that has been questioned. Within universities, there's anxiety about the lack of predictability and the number of changes being introduced. Given the option of a clear path and a path that's somewhat uncertain, students will question what their future looks like. I would say that's a big risk right now.

**TT: Any words of wisdom for incoming students?**

DL: I want students to continue to pursue their passion for neuroscience and research. There is no more exciting field with incredible opportunities to advance science and improve health. The lack of predictability and concerns about the future of funding for neuroscience research are real, but historically there has always been a recovery in funding. The economic and health benefits of public funding for neuroscience research are on our side, so stick to your dreams.
