# Your Writing Was Flagged as AI. What Does That Mean?

A professional writer was told her work "looked like AI" — but she wrote every word herself. Here's what happened, and why tools that check for AI make more mistakes than people think.

## The Accusation

Sally McGraw has written for a living for over 20 years. She sent a writing sample to a possible client. The client's reply: two computer tools said her writing looked like it came from AI.

"I was floored. I had not used AI," McGraw said. She got angry — her face turned hot. She told the client she had written every word, but didn't push harder because the job was small.

## Why Editors Are Using Detectors

Editors are buried under a flood of AI-written stories. One science-fiction magazine got about 1,100 stories each month in 2021. By early 2023, after ChatGPT came out, that number doubled. The editor had to stop taking new stories for a while.

But the tools editors use to sort out AI writing are not perfect. They sometimes mark real human writing as AI — and writers often have no way to show they are right.

## Why Detectors Get It Wrong

These tools work by asking: how easy is it to guess the next word? But here's the problem — writing that is very clear and well put together can feel "easy to guess" to AI. One science writer tested her own published book and one tool said it was 98.7% likely to be AI-written. "A clear, obvious false result," she said.

Some writing habits can also set off the alarm: using big words, using lots of dashes, or listing things in groups of three. These are natural habits for good human writers too.

## The Cost of False Accusations

A writers' group says checking for AI is like a hunt for people to blame — writers fear being marked as cheats when they did nothing wrong. A wrong answer could cost someone their book deal or their good name.

McGraw has already changed how she writes: she now avoids certain words and even drops marks that she used to add between sentences. She misses one type of dash she used for 20 years in her news work.

"It's like trying to trick a machine into thinking I'm not a machine," she said.
