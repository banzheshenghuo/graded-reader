# Your Writing Was Flagged as AI. What Does That Actually Mean?

Ghostwriter Sally McGraw was hoping to get a job writing a book plan for a new client. But after sending in a writing sample, she got a surprise: an email saying that several AI checking tools had marked her work as machine-made.

"I was floored because I had not used AI to generate the text," McGraw said. "I'd written it all myself."

After more than 20 years as a paid writer, McGraw said she was angry that her work had been pushed aside as AI-made. "You know where you see a cartoon character going red from their chin all the way up to their head? I felt my ears starting to burn," she said.

She told the client she had written it herself, but didn't push harder because the job was small. The client confirmed she had screened McGraw's work for AI, but said the results were not the deciding factor in hiring someone else.

## Why Editors Are Turning to Detectors

As editors face a growing flood of AI-made content, some are using checking tools to sort through what comes in. But these tools are not perfect: they sometimes wrongly mark human-written work as AI, leaving writers no way to prove otherwise.

"Everyone knows that these screeners are not 100% accurate," McGraw said.

Neil Clarke, who runs the science-fiction magazine Clarkesworld, says a flood of AI work has put real strain on his editing team. In 2021, the magazine got about 1,100 stories per month. By early 2023, after ChatGPT became widely available, that number had doubled, forcing Clarke to stop taking new stories for a while.

"It has brought a feeling of not trusting writers into the process," he said.

## Why Detectors Get It Wrong

AI checking tools have gotten better, but the companies behind them admit they are not perfect. Different tools can reach very different answers about the same text.

In a study from June, researchers tested four widely used checking tools on 160 school papers. The best one, Pangram, correctly found AI use in 37 of 40 papers where a human had rewritten AI text — but that still means 3 out of 40 slipped past it.

The deeper problem: many tools work by asking an AI model how easy it is to guess the next word. But writing that the model has already seen tends to be too easy to guess too. One science blogger tested her own 2019 book about AI — one tool said it was 98.7% sure the text came from AI. "A clear, bright, flashing wrong answer," she said.

The tools look for hints in word choice, marks between sentences, and how ideas are laid out. Words like "delve," lots of long dashes, and ideas listed in groups of three can set off the AI alarm. But those are also natural habits for good human writers.

## The Cost of False Accusations

The Authors Guild, which speaks up for writers, said checking for AI "feels like a witch hunt." A wrong answer could cost a writer their book deal; letting real AI work slip through can make readers lose faith.

McGraw has already changed how she writes: she avoids certain words and drops commas she used to add, hoping the uneven style will prove a human wrote it. "I miss my long dash. I learned to use it in writing school and I've used it for 20 years," she said.

"It's like trying to fool a machine into thinking I'm not a machine," she said. And she worries about facing another accusation when a bigger opportunity is on the line.
