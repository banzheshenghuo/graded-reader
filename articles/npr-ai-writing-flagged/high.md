# Your Writing Was Flagged as AI. What Does That Actually Mean?

Ghostwriter Sally McGraw was hoping to land a job developing a book proposal for a prospective client. But after submitting a writing sample, she got an unexpected response: an email telling her that multiple AI detection tools had flagged her work as machine-generated.

"I was floored because I had not used AI to generate the text," McGraw said. "I'd written it all myself."

Having spent more than 20 years as a paid nonfiction writer, McGraw said she was angry that her work had been pushed aside as AI-made. "You know where you see a cartoon character going red from their chin all the way up to the crown of their head? I felt my ears starting to burn," she said. "I thought, what is going on?"

McGraw said she told the client she had written the plan herself, but decided not to push the issue because she saw the job as small. In an email to NPR, the client said she had checked McGraw's work for AI, but that the results were not the main reason for choosing a different writer — she pointed to experience and fit instead. McGraw says she was paid for her writing samples, but believes the checking results cost her the chance to write the full book plan.

## The Pressure on Editors

As editors struggle with a growing flood of AI-made content, some are using checking tools to help sort through the growing pile of submissions. But these tools are not perfect: they sometimes wrongly mark human-written work as AI, leaving writers with no idea what set off the result or how to prove otherwise.

"Everyone knows that these screeners are not 100% accurate," said McGraw.

Neil Clarke, who runs the science-fiction magazine Clarkesworld, says a flood of AI work at the magazine has put real strain on his editing team. In 2021, the magazine got about 1,100 stories a month. By February 2023, after AI tools like ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini became widely available, that number had doubled, forcing Clarke to stop taking new stories for a while. He guesses his own workload has grown by about 30%.

"It's introduced an element of mistrust into the process," he said.

## Why Detectors Get It Wrong

While AI checking tools have gotten better in recent years, the companies behind them admit the tools are not perfect. Researchers have found that different tools can reach very different answers about the same piece of writing.

In a study from June in a school research journal, researchers tested four widely used checking tools on 160 academic papers in four groups: fully human-written, fully AI-written, a mix of both, and AI text rewritten by a human. Among the four tools, Pangram was the most steadily right, correctly finding AI use in 37 of the 40 mixed papers and in 37 of the 40 papers where a human had rewritten AI text.

Pangram CEO Max Spero said in an email that his company's tool can make mistakes, especially when there is not much text to look at.

Published writing can add another problem, says Chris Callison-Burch, a computer science professor at the University of Pennsylvania. "Many AI checking tools work by asking a large language model how easy it is to guess the next word in a passage," he said. "The catch is that these models also tend to find text from their own training data unusually easy to guess."

That could explain what science blogger Janelle Shane ran into in late 2022 when she tested pieces of her 2019 book about how AI works, You Look Like a Thing and I Love You. One tool said it was 98.7% sure a passage was machine-made. "This is a clear, bright, flashing wrong answer," Shane said. "I wrote this text myself."

A draft that has never been published, like McGraw's sample, would not face that particular problem, Callison-Burch said — but it could still be wrongly marked based on patterns in the writing style.

The tools look for hints in word choice, marks between sentences, and how ideas are laid out. Words like "delve," lots of long dashes, and ideas listed in groups of three can set off the AI alarm. So can starting sentences with words like "notably" or "crucially," or asking a question and then answering it yourself. At the same time, those are familiar habits for human writers. Callison-Burch says the tools weigh patterns across a whole piece, so one dash or one word alone doesn't necessarily mean a bot was used.

## Witch Hunts and Warning Signs

The Authors Guild, a group that speaks up for writers, said in May that using AI checkers "creates a feeling like a witch hunt" — writers worry about being wrongly blamed and not being able to defend themselves. "A wrong answer can cost an author their book deal and their name, and letting real AI work slip through can make readers lose their faith in writers and the whole industry," the Guild wrote.

Clarke sits somewhere in the middle. He doesn't personally use AI detectors to screen incoming manuscripts, but thinks certain detection tools could help overwhelmed editors manage the volume, provided they check the detector's findings. He cautions against turning suspicions into accusations that could damage a writer's reputation. "A story should end in a simple no, not in an accusation," he said.

McGraw says the fear of another accusation has changed how she writes. She now avoids using certain words and sentence constructions, and occasionally leaves out commas she used to include, hoping the inconsistency will help prove that a human wrote the text. "I miss my long dash. I learned to use it in writing school and I've used it for 20 years," she said.

The ghostwriter also sees the irony: large language models learn from human prose, yet she feels the pressure to make hers look less like AI. "It's like trying to trick a machine into thinking I am not a machine," McGraw said. And she worries about facing another accusation when a bigger chance is on the line. "I'm getting ready for the day when it's a job I really want and the result will make or break me," she said.
