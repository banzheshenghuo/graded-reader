# Daddy and the Foreign City

## The delayed morning

On Thursday I became a travelling daddy: one developer, one wife, one baby, three bags, and one plane ticket each to a foreign city where a big developer conference was waiting for me. My wife called it a work holiday. The baby called it nothing, because she cannot talk yet, but she packed her own opinion anyway: she refused to let go of her small toy router for the whole trip. My daughter's favorite toy is a plastic router, which is my fault entirely.

Unfortunately, the plane was delayed. We sat by the window for two hours, and the airport taught us its two great lessons again: every queue is a bottleneck, and every family ahead of you has more bags than you. The taxi fare into the city, the driver told us, had also grown this year; everything has a rate now, and every rate goes up. A woman in uniform walked by and said, "Madam, they will call you soon," and my wife said thank you with her eyes half closed. The baby chewed her biscuits slowly, like a small retired person with no plans.

I had packed with the care of a man who learned his lessons at the zoo last week: hats against the sunburn, spare socks, her medicine box with the new prescription taped inside the lid, four bottles, one wooden spoon. When the plane finally rolled out, the baby fell asleep before the city was even behind us. She slept over the clouds for exactly forty minutes, which is her standard unit of travel.

## The lobby with birds

The hotel was tall and quiet, with a lobby so big it had its own weather. Birds could have lived in there. The baby waved at a plant, then at a man with a suitcase, then at the plant again. The hotel had its own church-quiet protocol: breakfast by reservation, dinner by reservation, and a free string concert in the garden every second night — four instruments, always the same four. Behind the front desk, a young secretary with careful hands gave us our keys and a map of the old town, and marked, in green, the road she liked best for sightseeing.

Our room had a balcony. The whole first evening we lived on that balcony: the baby swallowed her milk, the city turned its lights on, and my wife and I shared one cold yogurt from the small refrigerator like two teenagers. In the room, the sink became our bottle-washing station; the elevator, I am happy to report, worked perfectly, and I told it so.

The conference filled three days. The sign-in desk was the morning bottleneck; the same careful secretary from the hotel found me a quiet corner before my talk. I was nervous in the good way. My approach to errors has not changed since the first nightmare week: catch them early, say sorry fast. I told the story of the little purple app: how it started as one weekend project, how a broken dependency nearly killed it, how I forked the repository and carried it myself, how the new protocol runs quiet checks at night. I talked about architecture as a small house: few rooms, strong walls. I talked about the trade-off between safety and simplicity, and about the constraint that rules everything I build — my users are seventy-nine and eleven months old.

The room was kind. Then came the questions, and the questions were the real conference. One man asked how the app handles a bad network. "Through a proxy," I said, "and with a small bundle; the whole app is forty kilobytes." A woman asked about data consistency across phones; a student demoed his transformer that adds voices to old silent films, and asked if our app could send it work — an agent, he called it, a small helper that checks each request before the proxy. Moreover, it could; we shook hands on a trial. Another one pushed me on memory: how much load can one old phone take? I showed the numbers, with the margin drawn in green. After the session, two students asked me to sign their conference passes, and a developer from the south asked whether the framework could compile his batch tools into his hospital workflow. I committed myself to one hour a night for his issue; he posted it before dinner. The fix took nine lines.

In the evening we met the other kind of conference: the hotel restaurant. My wife ordered shrimp, and the baby watched each shrimp like a small judge. The waiter, who spoke four languages, recommended the fish; his accent gave him away in the kindest way — Irish, it turned out, from a town near the sea, and he knew our waitress from home. The shrimp were delicious, the night was warm, and the baby chewed and swallowed her pasta with the seriousness of a professional. Unfortunately, the restaurant had no high chair, so my knees became the chair, as they do. Travel has its own prescription: pack light and expect nothing.

That night, over the video call, Grandma sang her old tune to the phone screen. The baby fell asleep mid-song. Grandma reported that Bobby had chased two pigeons and one cat that day. Moreover, her fridge — she refuses the word refrigerator, it is too long, she says — was full, because the neighbor had brought soup.

## Sightseeing, and one new word

Sunday was for sightseeing. We walked the old town slowly, the way the baby likes it: ten meters, a stop, ten meters, a stop. We approached the fountain square by mistake twice, which is the correct way to find it; the layout of the streets is five hundred years old and better than any map; the baby chased pigeons from square to square, and waved at every dog that passed. In the square, street musicians had set up their instruments near the fountain. Grandpa's old box would have liked this square. The tune they played was the kind that makes strangers stand closer, and it did. A man sold post cards, and we bought one for Grandma; I wrote three lines and posted it at the corner box. On the card I drew a small lion and a small monkey, because the zoo was her first city, and this was her second.

In a small park behind the church, the baby saw her first foreign swing. She pointed. She shouted. She pointed again, in case anyone had missed her point the first time. On the swing, she opened her whole face, and a passing grandmother said something soft in a language we did not know, and every word was understandable anyway.

Several things happened at once on Monday morning, on the balcony, while I packed. The baby stood up alone, holding the chair. My wife turned the camera on. And the baby, looking directly at me, said her second word: "Daddy." Her first word remains "hot," from the soup, but this one was aimed at me, and I gave myself away completely — I sat down on the floor of a foreign hotel room and laughed until my eyes were wet. The theory that I am a serious developer did not survive that morning.

## Home

The flight home was quiet. The baby slept over my shoulder inside my jacket, tired the way only small children and athletes ever get. At home, everything was where we had left it: the bottles by the sink, the broken elevator fixed, Bobby barking at his own reflection, Grandma's spoon in the drawer.

I thought about the week on the bus from the airport, paid the fare with a smile — at this rate, the baby will run the family by spring — and let the week settle. The whole trip was one implicit agreement between three people: we carry each other. A conference, it turns out, is a lot of people who normally build things alone, sitting in the same rooms for three days. They ask about your proxy, your bundle, your consistency, your margin; then they fly home and keep fixing their small corners of the world, one repository at a time. My own margin, I decided, will stay what it is. A boring life, most of it: tuneless mornings, one warm app, and a daughter who says Daddy. I would do the whole week again tomorrow.
