# A Tiny Worm Is Rewriting the Story of How Brains Evolved

A tiny sea animal, about the size of a grain of rice, has three light lines on its back. It hunts very small animals.

What makes it special is its head. It holds a tight group of nerves — a kind of brain. But unlike the brains of insects, mice or people, it has no separate parts. There are no special areas for seeing, smelling or moving.

Its family broke away from ours about 550 million years ago, near the very start of the family tree of animals. So it may be close to what the first brains looked like.

## It Can Grow Back

It can also grow a whole new body from a small part of itself. Mansi Srivastava at Harvard University has studied this for over ten years. Now Vikram Chandra, in her lab, uses a similar method to study its brain.

They talked to a science magazine about why these animals are easy to keep, and what happens when you cut half of one's head off.

## Why This Worm?

Srivastava first looked at simpler worms that other scientists already used. But she wanted to compare different kinds of animals, not just study one. She asked an expert which one to choose. He pointed to this one.

So she went to Bermuda with the exact map address where they had last been found. She caught some. "Then I got very lucky," she said. Many new lab animals fail. But she found ways to keep them alive and to work with them.

Looking after them is easy. They live in plastic boxes of salt water and swim about happily. They lay eggs on the sides of the box, so you can pick them up with a small tube. "For the first nine years of my lab, we didn't have any fancy equipment at all."

## Cut Off Half a Head — It's Fine

Normally, if you damage one part of a brain, you see a clear problem. Cut here, and the animal cannot move its leg. That is true for insects and for mice.

This worm is different. "We can cut off large parts of this brain, and the animal is fine," Chandra said. It can still catch tiny sea animals with half its head gone — sometimes even more.

## What This Means for Brain History

Many scientists have thought about how animals went from having no brain to having one. Most ideas say brains grew parts first, then came together in the head.

This worm suggests the opposite order: first everything came together in the head, and only later did the brain divide into special parts. "That would look sort of like this worm's brain," Chandra said. "Maybe that's what the first brains looked like."

It also shows a brain does not need special parts to do hard things. Even without them, it can hunt fast and find a partner.

## What's Next

Srivastava now studies the animal's babies. She is surprised by how bendable their cells are. Chandra is starting his own lab in London next March. He wants to find "the most interesting, complicated thing this animal can do," and then ask what in its brain makes it possible.
