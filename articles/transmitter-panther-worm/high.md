# The Three-Banded Panther Worm Is Rewriting the Story of Brain Evolution

The grain-of-rice-sized sea worm has three light lines across its back and is a fierce hunter of tiny creatures — hence its common name. Its nervous system is tighter than a simple net of nerves, with a tightly packed group of nerve cells in the head. But unlike the brains of other two-sided animals, such as insects, mice and humans, it has no specialized parts.

Its family line split from ours about 550 million years ago, near the very base of the animal family tree. So it may sit close to what the first brains looked like.

The animal can also grow back its whole body from a small piece of itself — something Mansi Srivastava, a professor of biology at Harvard University, has studied for over ten years. Now Vikram Chandra, a researcher in her lab, has adapted the same cutting method to look at the worm's nervous system.

## Why This Worm?

Srivastava started out studying simple flatworms, which had become the standard animal for this kind of research. But she wanted comparisons across species, not insights about just one organism. She asked an expert on acoels — a group of simple sea creatures — which species he would try to grow in a lab. He pointed them to this one.

So they went to Bermuda, using a paper that listed the exact map spot where the animals were last collected. "Then I got very lucky," she said. Many new lab animals fail; but they found ways of keeping them and of getting lab tools to work.

Caring for them is easy. "They're great lab rats," she said. They live in plastic boxes of salt water and swim around happily. You can keep them in a drawer. They reproduce all the time, laying eggs on the sides of the boxes that you can pick up with a small tube. "For the first nine years of my lab, we had no fancy equipment at all."

## Half a Brain, Still Hunting

In a normal animal, damaging one part of the brain causes a clear, specific problem. "Hofstenia does not have that," Chandra said. There is a dense group of neurons in the head, "but inside it, we find no separate parts at all — not in shape, function or make-up."

"We can cut off large parts of this brain, and the animal is fine, as far as we can tell," he said. It can still catch shrimp even when missing half of its head — sometimes 80 percent.

## What This Says About the First Brains

Many people have thought about how animals went from simple nerve nets to brains. Most ideas say separate parts formed first and then came together.

"Our work on Hofstenia suggests the opposite could be true — that you centralize first and regionalize later," Srivastava said.

Chandra added: "If you took an ancient nerve net and pressed it into the head, but you didn't yet put special cell types in special places there, that would look a lot like this brain — and maybe that's what the first brains looked like."

It also suggests you don't need the features we take for granted in normal two-sided brains, like special parts, to get complex behavior. The animal can hunt shrimp in clever, fast ways and carry out complex mating behavior.

## What's Next

Srivastava is going deeper into the study of embryos. Her work has shown an incredible level of flexibility in the cells. Next March, Chandra starts his own lab in London, using Hofstenia to understand how this new kind of brain is organized, how it works out problems, and how it controls behavior.

He wants to find "the most interesting, complicated thing this animal can do," and then ask what in its brain makes it possible.
