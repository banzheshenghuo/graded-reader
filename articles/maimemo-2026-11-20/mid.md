# The Purple Label

Mira had stood behind the same counter for nineteen years, and she could tell what a customer needed before the customer said a word. She never put that skill on any form, and nineteen years had done it to her without asking.

Then the chain bought the pharmacy. It had forms for everything, and it had arrangements for the first quarter before anyone had asked the staff a question. A man named Daniel arrived at eight on a Monday with a briefcase, a laptop, and the printed arrangements in a folder. His secretary had emailed the same file twice, in case the folder was lost. He was polite. He wanted an evaluation of the shop by Friday. He also wanted a second evaluation at the end of the month, and the arrangements said nothing about that one. Mira read the arrangements twice and found nothing to argue with, which annoyed her more than an argument would have.

Her mornings had run the same way since the year she started. Six forty: unlock, lights, the refrigerator check. The vaccine refrigerator had to hold its temperature all night, and a small battery in the corner of the display wrote down every hour of it. If the battery died, the record died with it, and the chain would ask questions nobody could answer. That was the quiet nightmare of the fridge, and she had thought about it at three in the morning more than once. So she changed the battery on the first of every month, whether it complained or not. She kept the old batteries in a drawer, which was not required and which she could not explain.

The refrigerator itself had a prescription taped to the door, a list of what could go inside and what could not. She had written it in her first year and never changed it. That was most of her consistency. Daniel's form had no box for it, and she did not offer one.

Mrs. Pratt came at nine for her snack and her news. She had been a secretary for thirty-one years before she retired, and she still kept other people's lives in a notebook, the way a good secretary keeps a diary for a boss who has forgotten how. She complained about the price of the biscuits first, then the rain, then her son, always in that order. She never chose; the snack was always the same biscuit. She had a theory that a shop was kept by its regulars and not its stock, and she had been testing that theory on Mira for a decade. When Daniel introduced himself, Mrs. Pratt looked at his briefcase and said, to Mira, that the last man with a briefcase had lasted four months. Then she took a second snack for the road and put it in her bag without asking, and Mira let her, because a theory needs evidence.

At ten a student came in from the language school down the road. He was foreign, nineteen, and holding a prescription written in his own country, which meant nothing here. He had a fever and no English for the word for it. This was the small nightmare of the job, a piece of paper that looked like a document and could not be read. Mira did not let him see her frustration. She found the medicine on the shelf, checked the dose against the old encyclopedia, and drew a clock on a card with three hands. The boy left with the card in his pocket and came back the next day with a bag of oranges. That side of foreign had nothing to do with papers, and it never once left her frustrated. She had learned more foreign from her customers than from any course. The till, by contrast, frustrated her once a week, and she had learned to expect it the way she expected rain.

Daniel watched all of it from the end of the counter and wrote something down. At four he showed her his evaluation form. His bias was toward numbers: how many prescriptions an hour, how long each customer waited at the counter. The form had a bias built into it, and the bias was toward the shop that looked like the other four hundred. Mira had a bias of her own, toward the customer who came back, and there was no box for her either. Neither of them could prove anything. They agreed to disagree over a cup of tea, and then he asked for further changes anyway. There was one further thing, which was the sign above the door, and further down the page there was a line for her signature, so she signed it.

The first change was a tutorial. Every new assistant would watch a short video about the till and then repeat the steps back to her, and the chain would keep a record of it. Mira had trained nine assistants in nineteen years without a video, and all nine had learned the till in a morning. She said so. Daniel said the chain required consistency across four hundred shops, and that consistency was the whole point of the tutorial. There was a constraint he did not say out loud, which was money, and she heard it anyway and let it pass. The constraint was not cruel. It came from the shape of the chain, though, and it reached all the way to the shelf, and it sat on the shop like a constraint on every choice that went through that door.

After lunch she went to the back room and opened the old encyclopedia of medicines, which was older than she was and wrong in several places and which she trusted anyway for the plain words. A customer had asked about a cream, and the cream came in a purple box on the third shelf. The purple meant nothing official. It was her own label, for the things she did not want to forget. She found the page and wrote two lines on a card for the customer. The medicine had changed its name twice since she started, and the shelf label had not. The encyclopedia had been wrong before and she still trusted it more than the chain's database, which was updated by people who had never stood behind a counter. Then she put the purple box back and made a note to order more of the cream before the winter.

At six she locked the door and did the closing round. The refrigerator at four degrees, the battery reporting, the till counted twice. She put the day's forms and the tutorial notes in the briefcase, which was hers and older than Daniel's and held more. She left the arrangements for tomorrow on the desk, and the arrangements were the same as today's, and she liked that. The till had crashed twice that month, and each crash had been a nightmare that ended before opening. On the bus she thought about Mrs. Pratt's theory and the boy with the clock card and the tutorial she had not watched yet. She decided to watch the video before she complained about it. That was not the same as agreeing to it, and she was old enough to tell the two apart.
