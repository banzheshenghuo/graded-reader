# "Sudden Wealth Syndrome 2.0": AI's New Rich Feel Alone

Mind doctors in the Bay Area invented a name for a strange feeling during the 1990s internet gold days — "sudden wealth syndrome." Now they say it's back, pushed along by the AI money wave.

Joan DiFuria and Stephen Goldbart started a center in the 1990s to help people who suddenly had more money than they could handle. "It's sudden wealth trouble 2.0," DiFuria said recently. "Everyone coming out of the AI boom is experiencing wealth on a different level, and having more complex emotions about it, too."

## An Industry for the Suffering Rich

Helping the rich is now its own business. TIGER 21, a small circle of rich members who meet for a full day each month, requires at least $20 million to join. Paracelsus Recovery, a live-in hospital for the mind in Zurich with a private cook and driver, charges about $131,000 a week. And one service that finds doctors for rich families starts at $30,000 for 50 hours.

## Guilt Makes This Wave Different

What makes this wave different from the 1990s, DiFuria said, is that the money comes from a technology many people consider harmful — including some of the people building it. The head of a top AI company has warned that AI could wipe out half of all first office jobs.

"If you're already feeling anxious about your wealth, and the whole world is saying, 'What you're doing is going to kill me or take my job' — how do you speak to your friends?" she said.

The numbers are hard to picture. There are hundreds of AI companies worth more than a billion dollars each. About 75 employees of one AI company walked away with $30 million each in a single share sale. A 2026 rich list counted at least 86 AI billionaires — 45 of them there for the first time.

## Rich, Connected, and Alone

With so many new rich, you might think they'd find each other. Instead, DiFuria and Goldbart said, many feel alone in a crowd. "It cuts people off from society," Goldbart said. Billionaires live in walled areas by Lake Tahoe and Hawaii — family compounds where each relative gets a separate house and pool.

The most famous examples: Mark Zuckerberg's Hawaii property, with a room under the ground, and Jeff Bezos's three homes on a private Florida island called "Billionaire Bunker," with its own police. Goldbart called the trend a "major social problem" — society is "sliding toward a great gap between those who have and those who have not, with the rich up on the hill, cut off from everybody below."

The damage passes to children too: "People are raising children who have barely ever left the house. They don't know what a poor person looks like," DiFuria said.

## What Helps — and Who Never Asks

The mind doctors push clients the other way: in six-hour first meetings, they ask them to write down their values and set clear goals like giving money away. But they admit the most cut-off people will probably never come — "most people with narcissism are not going to come to ask us about values or purpose," DiFuria said.

Some simply find their own kind. One billionaire family moved to an area full of other billionaires. "We found our people," they told her. "We don't have to hide that we have a plane, a boat and five homes."
