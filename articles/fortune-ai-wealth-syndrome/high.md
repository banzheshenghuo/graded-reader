# "Sudden Wealth Syndrome 2.0": Therapists Say AI's New Millionaires Feel Alone

Bay Area therapists coined a term for that feeling during the dot-com boom of the 1990s — "sudden wealth syndrome" — and they say it is back in the wake of the AI windfall.

Joan DiFuria and Stephen Goldbart started the Money, Meaning & Choices Institute in the 1990s to treat people struggling with a sudden influx of money from the first internet gold rush. "It's sudden wealth syndrome 2.0," DiFuria said recently. "All these people coming out of the AI boom are experiencing wealth on a different level, and having more complex emotions about it, too."

Fortunately for those who suffer from it, there's an industry for that. TIGER 21, a peer group whose members meet for a full day each month, requires at least $20 million in assets to join. Paracelsus Recovery, a live-in clinic in Zurich that provides a private chef, a driver and a therapist who lives in, charges about £100,000 — roughly $131,000 — a week. And one health service that finds doctors and mind care for rich families asks $30,000 for 50 hours to start.

## Why This Wave Feels Different

What makes this wave different from the dot-com era, DiFuria said, is that people are making money from a technology many consider harmful — including some of the people building it. The head of a leading AI company has warned that AI could erase half of all entry-level office jobs.

"If you're already feeling anxious about your wealth and the whole world is saying, 'What you're doing is going to kill me or take my job,' how do you speak to your friends?" she said. "It's really next level from what we've seen before."

The numbers are enormous. Fortune reported in August 2025 that there were 498 AI unicorns — private AI companies valued at $1 billion or more — worth $2.7 trillion together, by CB Insights' count. More than 600 current and former OpenAI employees sold $6.6 billion in shares in a single offering; about 75 of them sold the most allowed and walked away with $30 million each. And Forbes counted at least 86 AI billionaires worth $2.9 trillion together on its 2026 list — 45 of them there for the first time.

## Rich, Connected — and Cut Off

The very rich may have more peers than ever before, but DiFuria and Goldbart said many feel cut off. "It creates social isolation. People who got their money from AI very much have their own subculture," Goldbart said.

Billionaires today have walled-off places like Lake Tahoe and Hawaii — family compounds where each relative gets a separate house and pool. The most famous: Mark Zuckerberg's Kauai property, which Wired guesses cost about $270 million with the land, complete with a 5,000-square-foot room under the ground that he described as "just like a little shelter." Jeff Bezos has spent about $237 million on three houses on Indian Creek, a man-made island nicknamed "Billionaire Bunker," with its own police and a gated bridge.

Goldbart called the trend a "major social problem": society is "drifting toward a great imbalance between the haves and have-nots, with the rich people up on the hill, cut off from everybody else below them."

The damage reaches the next generation. "People are raising children who have hardly ever left the house. They don't know what a poor person looks like," DiFuria said. Fortune recently reported on rich parents' growing fear that their money will drain their children's drive.

## What Helps — and Who Never Asks

Goldbart and DiFuria try to push clients the other way. In a first meeting that often runs about six hours, they ask clients to write a "set of working values" and set concrete goals, such as volunteering or giving some of their money away — a path some AI leaders have promised in public to take.

But they admitted that the people who are most cut off will probably never look for them. "Most people with narcissism are not going to come to ask us questions about values or purpose," DiFuria said.

Some clients also choose to stay alone by choice. DiFuria described a billionaire family that went from feeling bad about their spending to owning boats and planes and moving to an area where other billionaires live. "We found our people," she said the family told her. "We don't have to be embarrassed or hide the fact that we have a plane and a yacht and five homes. We can finally be open about it."
