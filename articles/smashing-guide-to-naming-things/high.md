Naming is hard. The language we use shapes the way we think about things, and it shapes the conversations we have about them. That is why there is so much confusion about colors, icons, UI components and features, and why teams struggle so much to name design tokens, HTML classes and variables.

Sometimes the names we choose are too generic, so nobody quite knows what is meant. Sometimes they are too specific, leaving little room for flexibility and reuse. So how do we pick the right names? And which naming conventions actually work?

If you need ideas for HTML classes, CSS properties or JavaScript functions, Classnames is a wonderful resource. It offers word lists grouped by theme, with terms for behavior, likeness between things, order, grouping and association, plus collections drawn from nature, art, theater, music, architecture, fashion and publishing, words that would never come to mind while writing code.

How would you choose the right name for a given color? David Aerne keeps a remarkable list of color names, with more than 30,000 different names, taken from various references and thousands of user contributions. It comes with a tool for choosing colors and searching names, and it is a good reference to keep near you.

What makes a good name? Javier Cuello gathered a set of good naming habits that help you name layers, groups and parts in a steady way that still works as a product grows. As he points out, a good name has a logical structure: it is short, meaningful, known by everyone, and unrelated to visual properties. He lists do's and don'ts and walks through the small details to consider when naming sizes, colors, groups, layers and parts.

How do you build a naming system for design tokens that works across many products? That was the problem facing the team at Intuit, the company that owns Mailchimp, QuickBooks and TurboTax. Nate Baldwin wrote a case study on the system they built, covering the pain points of the old one, the criteria they set for the new one, and how it was made. There are plenty of takeaways for building your own robust system.

Looking at what other people call the same thing is a great first step when naming leaves you stuck, and there is no better source than other design systems. Iain Bean did the reading and made the Component Gallery. It shows more than 50 UI components, from accordion to text you cannot see, and it lists the other names each part goes by.

Features often have low adoption because of poor discoverability. Adoption takes time: a feature must first be discovered, then understood, then tried and learned, and finally built into an existing workflow. Erin Gannon wrote a guide on how to make all of that happen.

Good names are driven by user needs and problems. They should signal the value or outcome of a feature and, most importantly, use the user's own language. So ask people to explain a feature in their own words, then use the words they use.

The Vodafone UK Design System team offers a fine example of naming guidelines for a complex, multi-brand system. Their Variables Taxonomy Map breaks the anatomy of a design token into four collections, from brand and raw values to meaning and pages. It builds on Nathan Curtis' work and lets anyone see where a token is used and what it stands for, just from its name.

Want more ways to do this? There are lists of Figma kits and other resources worth a look. Romina Kavcic built a small tool for naming design tokens, from learning how they work to setting up your own naming plan. And Onym is an open-source repository of tools and guides for naming products, from listing and checking name ideas to the history of words and stories of names that failed.

The right name is the one that the team and its users both understand and use. So much time is wasted talking about the very same thing in different tongues: the designer's tongue, the developer's tongue, the product manager's tongue and the user's tongue. That is what causes most of the anger and doubt.

So if you see low adoption, the name may merely be the problem. Watch for names that clash and put them on a list to fix later. It is one of those fine details that reduces frustration and helps teams work together better.
