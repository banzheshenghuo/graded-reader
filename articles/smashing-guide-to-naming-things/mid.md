Naming things is hard. The words we use shape the way we think about things, and they also shape the way we talk about them. That is why we often feel lost about colors, icons, interface components and features. It is also why we fight so much about what to call design tokens, HTML classes and variables.

They are too general, so nobody knows what we mean. Or they are too narrow, so we cannot use them again in another place. So how do we choose good names? And which naming rules work best?

If you need ideas for naming CSS properties or JavaScript functions, Classnames is a useful site. It offers word lists sorted by theme: behavior, how alike two things are, order, grouping and connection. It even has collections of words from nature, art, theater, music, architecture, fashion and publishing, words that would not come to your mind when you write code.

What about colors? David Aerne keeps a huge list of color names, with more than 30,000 different names, many of them sent in by users. It also comes with a color picker and a name search. It is a good reference to keep close to hand.

So what makes a good name? Javier Cuello collected a set of good habits for naming layers, groups and components in a steady way that still works when the product grows. According to him, a good name follows a clear pattern. It is short, it says something, everybody knows it, and it does not talk about how the thing looks. He shows what to do and what to avoid, and he walks through the details you need to think about when you name sizes, colors, groups and layers.

Intuit, the company that owns Mailchimp, QuickBooks and TurboTax, had to build a naming system for many products. Nate Baldwin wrote a report about the token system they built. It explains what hurt in the old system, which rules they set for the new one, and how they built it. There is a lot to learn for your own work.

Another good way to start is to look at what other teams call the same thing. And what better source than other design systems? Iain Bean did the reading for you and made the Component Gallery. It shows more than 50 parts of a page, from the accordion to text that you cannot see, and it also lists the other names that people give each part.

People often do not use a new feature because they cannot find it. Use takes time: first they must find it, then understand it, then try it, learn it, and in the end put it into their daily work. Erin Gannon wrote a guide on how to make all of that happen.

Good names come from user needs. They should show what a feature gives you, and above all they should use the user's own language. So ask people to explain a feature in their own words, and then use the words they use.

The Vodafone UK Design System team offers a great example of naming guidelines for a complex, multi-brand system. Their Variables Taxonomy Map splits a design token into four groups, from brand and raw values to meaning and pages. It builds on Nathan Curtis' work and lets anyone see where a token is used and what it stands for, just from its name.

Want more ways to do this? There is a list of Figma kits and other resources worth a look. Romina Kavcic also built a small tool for naming design tokens. Onym is a free set of tools and guides for naming products, from listing name ideas and checking them to the history of words and stories of names that did not work.

So the right name is the one that your team and your users both understand and use. A lot of time is wasted when people talk about the same thing in different dialects: the designer's dialect, the developer's dialect, the product manager's dialect and the user's dialect. That is what makes people angry and unsure.

If people do not use a feature, the name itself may be the problem. Watch out for names that are too close to each other, and write them on a list to fix later. It is a small detail, but it can save a lot of anger and help teams work together better.
