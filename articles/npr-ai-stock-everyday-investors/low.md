Last week, Anthropic sold shares to the public for the first time. The company raised 107 billion dollars. Its seven founders each became worth billions on paper. It is the largest public offering in history.

Austan Goolsbee runs the Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago. He said it is good that new ideas may make the country more productive and rich.

But now that Anthropic shares trade on the open market, ordinary investors can finally join in. You do not even need to buy single shares. Many retirement accounts hold mutual funds and index funds, and those funds already include Anthropic and other big tech companies.

So how does this big wave of wealth affect everyday investors? Financial adviser Matt Morrow explains. He runs a wealth management firm.

First, did people who bought shares at the start make money? No. That is the surprising part.

Morrow said the usual price of Anthropic was 83 dollars. It opened above 100 dollars. Now it trades near 78 dollars. So even those who bought at the start are down on paper.

Old Wall Street wisdom says the stock market moves money from the impatient to the patient. The Anthropic offering proves this over time.

Can ordinary people still benefit without buying at the start? Yes, in small ways. Some Fidelity mutual funds already held tiny amounts of Anthropic before the public sale. Many pension funds that invest in private companies also owned some.

If you buy one of those funds, the fund may hold 80 to 100 companies. A manager chooses what to buy and sell inside it. So a normal investor can get exposure to Anthropic, and to companies like SpaceX, without picking single stocks.

Why are these funds so heavy in tech now? Because tech has dominated life for five to 10 years. Google, Microsoft and Amazon are the largest companies. They take the biggest weight in the main market indexes. So they end up in most broad stock funds.

Pension funds like CalSTRS, the big California teacher fund, also owned some Anthropic early. Is that risky?

Morrow said there is always risk in any company a fund buys. But these pensions are hundreds of billions of dollars. The Anthropic part is very small compared to the whole. Even if that part does poorly, the harm to the whole fund is smaller than people expect.

Many people believe getting into a huge offering on day one makes you rich. What is the reality?

Morrow said it is mostly a feeling. The real numbers are striking. A little over half of new offerings are down 12 months after their first-day close. The attitude should be hurry up and wait. Prices may jump on day one because of excitement. That does not mean it is the best time to buy.

Is it sometimes better to wait and not buy at the start? Yes. Buy some first if you like the company, then wait. You will likely get a chance to buy more at a better price.

This is common-sense advice. Invest steady amounts over time. That way, whether the stock goes up or down, you spread your cost. This is called dollar cost averaging.

Look at NVIDIA. Its CEO quit, then was brought back. The stock swings up and down. In emotional times, people often freeze. They see a stock fall and want to buy. Then it rises and they feel they missed out.

Morrow said the advice is simple. Step back. Stay disciplined. Think about what you are buying and how long you will keep it. Ask yourself if you will keep buying over time instead of buying all at once. That steady habit smooths out the waves.
