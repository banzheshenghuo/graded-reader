Last week, Anthropic held its initial public offering. The company raised 107 billion dollars from that offering and a private placement with large investors. Its seven founders alone are now worth many billions of dollars. It is the largest IPO in history.

Austan Goolsbee heads the Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago. He said it is fabulous that we are coming up with innovations that might raise the country's productivity growth and might make us rich.

But now that Anthropic stock trades publicly, everyday investors can get a piece of the action. That is true even if you do not buy individual shares. Many of our retirement accounts are invested in mutual funds and index funds, and those funds already include Anthropic. CONSIDER THIS from NPR: Anthropic's historic IPO has shaken up the U.S. stock market. How will all that action affect everyday investors?

Financial adviser Matt Morrow explains. He is the co-founder and president of Munroe Morrow Wealth Management.

First, did people who bought shares at the IPO make money? No. That is the most interesting part about IPOs.

Morrow said there is an old adage in Wall Street. The stock market is a device to transfer money from the impatient to the patient. IPOs have proved that over time. Anthropic's IPO opened above its offer price of 83 dollars, over 100 dollars a share. As of now, it trades around 78 dollars. So on paper, even those who bought at the IPO are down.

Can the public benefit without getting in at the start? Yes, in small ways. A handful, actually dozens, of Fidelity mutual funds already owned small percentages of Anthropic before the IPO. So if you owned those funds, you had small exposure even earlier. The same is true in many pension funds that invest in private equity or venture capital funds. They also may have some exposure to Anthropic pre-IPO.

When Morrow says Anthropic is in some mutual funds, he means pooled baskets of stocks and bonds. Many regular investors and people with retirement accounts hold these pooled baskets.

If you buy one mutual fund, that fund may own 80 to 100 different companies. The manager chooses what to buy and sell inside, which stocks and which companies. They get exposure at certain firms to pre-IPO companies like Anthropic. SpaceX is another great example that was widely held before the IPO, inside mutual funds available to everyday investors.

Why are these funds so heavily weighted toward tech now? Because tech has dominated our lives for the last five to 10 years. The tech-based companies, whether Google, Microsoft or Amazon, are the largest companies. They take the heaviest weighting in the broad investment indices. So they end up held very heavily in most broad U.S. stock mutual funds.

Pension funds like CalSTRS, the largest pension fund in the country, also had some pre-IPO investment in Anthropic. Is there risk for public employees and their future pensions?

Morrow said there is always risk in any company a pension plan invests in. But when you look at these pensions that are hundreds and hundreds of billions of dollars, that small exposure to Anthropic or SpaceX is tiny relative to the overall portfolio and the diversification these funds hold. Even when it does not do well, the negative impact on the whole pension fund is not what you would expect.

There is a public perception that getting into a huge IPO like Anthropic before it makes you rich. What is the reality of striking it rich through an IPO?

Morrow said it is all perception, and it is one of the most interesting parts of investing. The statistic for IPOs is striking. A little over half of IPOs are down 12 months later from their first-day close. Most people assume they have to get in on day one. The real attitude is hurry up and wait. IPOs may pop on the first day because there is a lot of excitement. That does not necessarily mean the price is the best entry point.

Is it sometimes better not to get in at the IPO and watch the stock first? Yes, and that should be most people's approach to buying any stock. There is not only one good time to buy. If it is a company you want to own for the long run, buy some on the first day, then wait. You are likely to get an opportunity to buy more at a better price.

This is the common-sense conventional wisdom many investment advisers support. Invest steady amounts over time. Whether a stock goes up or down, you get a dollar cost averaging effect.

Look at NVIDIA. Its CEO resigned, then was brought back. The stock bounces up and down. People worry about how their investments perform during those shifts.

Morrow said NVIDIA has obviously been a wild ride in the last two weeks. It is a reminder to investors to try to follow discipline over emotion. These are very emotional times. When you see a stock swing by large amounts in a given day, you get stuck with that feeling. I am missing out. The stock is going back up. I need to get in.

The thing we advise clients is to step back. Stay disciplined. Think about what you are buying and how long you want to own it. Are you being disciplined? Are you willing to buy this over a period of time, instead of having to buy it all because in that moment it feels like the thing to buy? That steady habit smooths out the waves.
