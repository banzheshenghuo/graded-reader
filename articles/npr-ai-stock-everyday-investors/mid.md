Last week, Anthropic held its first public offering. The company raised 107 billion dollars. Its seven founders each became a billionaire on paper. It is the largest IPO in history.

Austan Goolsbee heads the Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago. He said it is wonderful that new ideas may raise the country's productivity growth and make the country rich.

But now that Anthropic stock trades on the open market, everyday investors can get a piece of the action. That is true even if you do not buy individual shares. Many retirement accounts are invested in mutual funds and index funds, and those funds already include Anthropic.

So how does this historic wave of wealth affect ordinary investors? Financial adviser Matt Morrow explains. He is the co-founder and president of a wealth management firm.

First, did the people who bought shares at the start make money? No. That is the most interesting part about IPOs.

Morrow said Anthropic opened above its offer price of 83 dollars, over 100 dollars a share. As of now, it trades around 78 dollars. So on paper, even those who bought at the IPO are down.

There is an old Wall Street adage. The stock market is a device to move money from the impatient to the patient. The Anthropic IPO has proved that over time.

Can the public still benefit without getting in at the start? Yes, in small ways. A handful of Fidelity mutual funds already owned small percentages of Anthropic before the IPO. So if you owned those funds, you had small exposure even earlier. Many pension funds that invest in private equity or venture capital also owned some Anthropic before the IPO.

When Morrow says Anthropic is in some mutual funds, he means pooled baskets of stocks and bonds. Many regular investors and retirement accounts hold these pooled baskets.

If you buy one mutual fund, that fund may own 80 to 100 different companies. The manager chooses what to buy and sell inside. They get exposure to firms like Anthropic before the IPO, or SpaceX, another example widely held before its IPO. You can buy those same funds as an everyday investor.

Why are these funds so heavily weighted toward tech now? Because tech has dominated our lives for the last five to 10 years. Companies like Google, Microsoft and Amazon are the largest. They take the biggest weight in the broad investment indexes. So they end up held heavily in most broad U.S. stock mutual funds.

Pension funds like CalSTRS, the big California teacher fund, also had some pre-IPO investment in Anthropic. Is there risk for public employees and their future pensions?

Morrow said there is always risk in any company a pension fund invests in. But these pensions are hundreds of billions of dollars. The Anthropic part, that small slice, is tiny compared to the whole portfolio and the diversification these funds hold. Even when it does not do well, the negative impact on the whole fund is smaller than you would expect.

There is a public perception that getting into a huge IPO like Anthropic before it makes you rich. What is the reality of striking it rich through an IPO?

Morrow said it is all perception, and it is one of the most interesting parts of investing. The statistic is striking. A little over half of IPOs are down 12 months later from their first-day close. Most people assume they must get in on day one. The real attitude is hurry up and wait. IPOs may pop on the first day because of excitement. That does not mean the price is the best entry point.

Is it sometimes better not to get in at the IPO and watch the stock first? Yes, and that should be most people's approach to any stock. There is not only one good time to buy. If it is a company you want to own for the long run, buy some on the first day, then wait. You are likely to get a chance to buy more at a better price.

This is the conventional wisdom many advisers consider common sense. Invest steady amounts over time. Whether a stock goes up or down, you get a dollar cost averaging effect.

Look at NVIDIA. Its CEO resigned, then was brought back. The stock bounces up and down. People worry about how their investments perform during those shifts.

Morrow said NVIDIA has been a wild ride in the last two weeks. It is a reminder to investors to try to follow discipline over emotion. These are very emotional times. When you see a stock swing by large amounts in a day, you get stuck with that feeling. I am missing out. The stock is going back up. I need to get in.

The thing we advise clients is to step back. Stay disciplined. Think about what you are buying and how long you want to own it. Are you being disciplined? Are you willing to buy this over a period of time, instead of buying it all because in that moment it feels like the thing to buy? That steady habit smooths out the waves.
