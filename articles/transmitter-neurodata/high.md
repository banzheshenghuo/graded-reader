# Govern Neurodata by How It's Used, Not Where It's From

Many experts considered it a landmark ruling for "neurorights." But the detail most people miss is how the court actually reached that decision. Chile had changed its constitution in 2021 to add an explicit neurorights clause — the first country to do so — and yet the court did not decide this case on it. Instead, it grounded the ruling in rights already on the books — physical and mental integrity, and privacy, under Article 19 of Chile's 1980 constitution. Consent to record data for one purpose, the court decided, is not consent to use it for another. What mainly protected the man in the case, in other words, was not so much the fact that the data came from his brain — but rather how they were used.

The court's decision implies that neurodata in general should be governed more by what is done with it than by the organ it came from. That reaches basic research directly. Data recorded in a lab is used for scientific study rather than, say, to profile customers or target ads, so it typically poses lower risks. The safeguards should therefore be lighter, and research data should be able to move from the lab that recorded it to the colleagues and collections that can build on it (including across borders) with fewer restrictions. The same signal recorded by a headset sold online, and sold on again, is a different matter.

## Why Consumer Laws Reach the Lab

As a scientist, you might ask why laws written for things people buy are your problem at all. They are, because the definitions those laws are settling on could easily spread into the institutions that govern your work, and could eventually shape what you can gather, share and reuse.

This neurotechnology shift has fed the powerful intuition that neurodata is different. As Marcello Ienca and his colleagues have argued, it's among the most direct correlates of our mental states, it sometimes escapes our conscious control, and it can be written to as well as read, opening a path to influence the mind directly. On that reasoning, brain data seems to demand its own special kind of protection. This natural instinct is shaping much of the legislation now taking form.

Source-based rules treat neuroscientists' recordings like an ad-tech dataset. Use-based rules regulate basic research at the level of risk it actually poses.

## A Patchwork of Definitions

Consider a concrete case: an EEG dataset recorded from a few dozen people doing a memory task. You might like to share the data with a colleague abroad, pool them with a partner lab's recordings, or let a graduate student use them again for a new question three years from now. Differences in how states govern neural data could get in the way of all of this. California and Colorado have changed their consumer privacy acts to define brain data by where it comes from — the nervous system — and to list it among sensitive data. Connecticut has added neurodata to its sensitive-data list, and Montana folded "neurotechnology data" into another of its privacy laws. On their face, none reach you, because they are written for companies handling consumers' data, not a lab.

Their meanings, though, may reach you by another route. Think about the position this could put a university checking office in: asked to review a technology it may never have seen before, with only legal meanings of neurodata written for things people buy. A careful office reaching for the best available meaning would be reaching for one built for a different problem. The same is true for a university legal office drafting a data-use agreement or a funder revising its data-sharing policy. No board has done so yet; the point is how easily one could. None of this would require the law to apply to you. Just the definition.

The meanings are also inconsistent. California leaves out anything inferred from nonneural information; Colorado does not. Montana leaves out pupil size and breathing rate as downstream effects, not neural activity; Colorado leaves out nothing. Connecticut covers only the central nervous system. The same pupillometry channel may be called neurodata in Denver and not in Helena. So which meaning an office picks will decide how your recordings are treated. And if your pilot study uses an EEG headset sold as a consumer product, the law applies to the company that made it — your recording follows the company's terms before it ever becomes your dataset.

## The Federal Picture

At the U.S. national level, the picture is still taking shape. The MIND Act, put forward in the Senate in September 2025 and still waiting, sets no rules; it directs a national office to study how neurodata should be governed. Its own text, however, hints at something telling: it asks that office to consider sorting neurodata by sensitivity, applying stricter oversight to high-risk uses and weighing benefits against risks. That's the beginning of a use-based governance framework.

Picture the same EEG recording under different uses. If a company sells it as a product to infer a shopper's mood, the risk is high and the good to society thin — even close to none — so the safeguards should be strict. In clinical studies and basic research, the risks are lower, and the data builds knowledge that returns to science and eventually the public.

Source-based rules treat neuroscientists' recordings like an ad-tech dataset. Use-based rules regulate basic research at the level of risk it actually poses. No more, no less.

## The Call to Show Up

Use-based governance protects basic researchers and the people whose data are used, without freezing science. But the framework will not build itself. It needs mixed-field research projects, practical tools and office rules able to turn "govern the use" into standards someone can apply on a Tuesday morning. Scientists who support this kind of framework need to help make it happen. Talk to the scientific societies that write position statements, the money givers that revise data-sharing policies and the law groups that hear expert testimony. These rooms are rarely filled with people who have recorded an EEG. Show up to one.
