# Govern Brain Data by How It's Used, Not Where It Comes From

Many experts called it a famous decision for "neurorights." But the detail most people miss is how the court actually reasoned. Chile added the world's first brain-rights clause to its highest law in 2021 — yet the judges did not decide this case on it. Instead, they grounded the ruling in rights already in the law: keeping body and mind safe, and privacy. Permission to record data for one purpose, they decided, is not permission to use it for another. What protected the man in the case was not that the data came from his brain — it was how the data was used.

## The Principle: Judge the Use

The decision points to a rule for all brain data: it should be governed more by what is done with it than by the body part it came from. That reaches research directly. Data recorded in a lab serves science, not customer profiling or ads, so the risks are lower and the rules should be lighter. Research data should be able to move — including across borders — to the colleagues and collections that can build on it. The same signal from a headset sold online, and resold, is a different matter.

## Why This Reaches Scientists

"You work in a lab, why should shopping laws concern you?" Because the meanings those laws choose could spread into the offices that govern research, and shape what you may collect, share, and use again.

The belief driving new laws is that brain data is special. As researcher Marcello Ienca and colleagues have argued, it is among the closest mirrors of our inner states, it sometimes works without our control, and it can be written to as well as read — a path toward influencing the mind directly.

## The Mess of State Laws

Several U.S. states have defined brain data inside consumer privacy laws, and they disagree. California leaves out guesses made from non-brain data; Colorado does not. Montana leaves out eye size and breathing; Colorado leaves out nothing. The same eye reading could count as protected brain data in one state and not in another.

These definitions could reach science indirectly. Picture a university checking office asked to judge a technology it has never seen, whose only ready-made legal meanings were written for things people buy. No board has done that yet — the point is how easily one could.

## The Federal Picture

In the U.S. Senate, the MIND Act — put forward in 2025 and still waiting — sets no rules. It asks a national office to study how brain data should be governed, weighing dangers against good. That is the beginning of judging by use: strict rules for dangerous uses like selling mood guesses, lighter ones for open research whose results return to the public.

## The Call

Good rules will not write themselves. Turning "judge the use" into rules someone can apply on a Tuesday morning needs mixed-field work and real tools. Scientists who support this should show up: the scientific societies, the money givers, the groups that hear expert advice. Those rooms rarely include anyone who has recorded brain data.
