# The Leak

Friday morning smelled of plans. Grandma arrived with two lists, one for today and one for tomorrow, and the tomorrow list was longer: the zoo, she announced, at nine sharp. Bobby has now heard the tiger page, the bear page, and the sea page, which is salty, and Grandma has decided that a boy who knows the encyclopedia's foreign animals by heart should meet a few of them in person. Her bias against zoos on weekends lasted until she saw the weather forecast. She checked the availability of the whole family twice. Horses he knows from the park carousel; the elephants can wait for volume E and surprise him anyway. The backpack was already half packed: bottle, snacks, the small blue swimsuit retired for the week, and a change of socks, because zoos, in her evaluation, are socks events. "The elephant," she said, "is in the encyclopedia's volume E, which arrives Monday. Until then, he can be a surprise." She made the arrangements the way a secretary books a wedding: no questions, no gaps, and everyone informed twice.

The briefcase and I reached the office at nine, and Friday is the day the week gets small. The scope was one screen: the console, a loading animation nobody loved (I took its cousin out in the spring), one old widget running unsupervised. I minified the build until it weighed less than its own name and the pages loaded fast, touched up the script behind it, added one hook for the analytics people, integrated one column into the weekly pipeline, and merged a pull request so empty it approved itself. Then a code review that ran deep, forty minutes on a single function, which frustrated the author and improved the function, both by the same amount. The author, fluent in patience, forgave me. A colleague's suggestion was to turn the guard's tutorial into a blog post; the slow response to this idea, mine, is on record, the machine learning team offered to make it smart, and I said no, thank you, twice, and the platform survived another week on boring. The guard has no intelligence to upgrade, and that is its whole character. My last job of the day was to derive one number from three logs, which is the least glorious and most honest work in the building.

At six, as promised, the swing was free, on the grass side of the park. Bobby and I kept our appointment, he in his sneakers, I in my work shoes, and at the top of the arc the chain knocks the frame, one small tick, which he now waits for like a drummer. His shoulders, the swim guide said, are swimmer's shoulders; whatever they are, they are growing. We took the metro home from the far stop, the fare unchanged in years, the terminal quiet on a Friday, a police car rolling past the gates on its unhurried rounds, the sky going purple-to-navy above the corner shop and the red-edged trees; and I carried him away, asleep, over the last stretch, the whole day's rent paid in one lift.

At nine the phone made its small sound. The kitchen guard had flagged an anomaly, and this one was new: humidity under the sink, rising, three readings in a row, off the chart the machine keeps in its small head. I stood in the kitchen and heard it before I saw it: a knock, very quiet, at the wrong hour, which was not a knock at all but a drip, gathering a small lobby of water on the cabinet floor, once a second, patient as a bill. The machine had given it away an hour before the drip did.

The evaluation took one minute. The trap under the sink was wet at one corner, and that corner was the bottleneck of the whole system, and it was wet because the washer inside had worn thin, half a year of hot water and soap doing what time does to everything Grandma owns. The immediate need was a coin-sized washer; I committed myself to the eleven-minute version of the repair. Wear is honest, unfortunately only in stages, and by the time it speaks, it has been talking for weeks. I turned the water off at the valve, derived the exact seat of the leak from the wet's shape, and walked to the corner shop for a washer the size of a coin.

The man at the counter knocked the price into the register. "You fix it yourself?" he asked, and I said a machine had found it and a man would finish it, which he declared, in his kind foreign accent, the correct order of the world. Back home, the repair consisted of a tutorial of six steps and eleven minutes; the pipe wrench frustrated me at step four, and the constraint on my language was Grandma, on the phone, the whole time. Grandma called in the middle of it, heard the word leak, and complained for one full minute about pipes, water bills, and the general direction of the century, then asked, in the next breath, whether the refrigerator and its medicine shelf had stayed dry. It had. Four degrees, all evening, the consistency of a good guard.

Here is the part I want in the blog post, if I ever write it: the guard caught it. Not me, not Grandma, not the drip's own little knock. A sensor with no intelligence, a battery I keep meaning to check, and a number going wrong in the night. The same machine that once flagged a grandmother and her midnight yogurt flagged a worn washer under a sink, and both times the flag was true, and both times the emergency was small because it was caught early. I shared the story with the family chat; my brother replied with one word: "Promotion." Medicine, the doctor said, is mostly consistency; plumbing, it turns out, is the same medicine with different tools.

Bobby woke at the noise of the wrench, or of my opinion of the wrench, considered crying, declined, and stood in his crib calling one word until the door opened. I held him up to watch the last two minutes of the repair, solemn, taking notes; the wrench bumped the pipe once, he patted the pipe once, terms accepted. Then the water went back on, the trap stayed dry, the drip stopped its knocking, and the house went back to its Friday night: I took my work shoes off at the door, he had three shrimp, his yogurt in the good bowl, and one small scoop of cream, thirsty work, all of it. He rinsed his own spoon, which is new.

His goodnight ran long tonight, through all three instruments, in his language, which is native to no country, and the style of it was all shoulders and joy. Bottle, small lamp, the tiger and the rabbit and, since Thursday, a small elephant from the shelf, one row of colleagues now, three wide and dressed for sleep. The sky outside the window was clear, the moon late but coming, one star already on duty, and the boy beneath it sleepy but stubborn about the last verse. Tomorrow is the zoo, the arrangements standing. The backpack waits by the door, packed by a secretary who does not miss: bottle, snacks, socks, the good camera, and a budget for ice cream, constraint and promise both, that we can afford and he cannot, being fourteen months old and rich in everything but money. Grandma will complain about the ticket price out of principle, immediately, at the gate.

The guard's last report of the night is boring, which is the best thing a report can be: no anomaly anywhere. Humidity under the sink: falling. Battery: low, flagged, noted for Sunday. The refrigerator in the purple kitchen holds its four degrees; the machine has no intelligence, and tonight it did not need any. It has ears, and the house, for one more night, had nothing to say. The briefcase, by the door, holds tomorrow's map.
