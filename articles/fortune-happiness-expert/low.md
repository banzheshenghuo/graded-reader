# The Happiness Expert Who Studies Deep Sadness

Carol Graham studies happiness for a living — and lately, that has meant studying deep sadness. She is a top researcher in America who has spent years asking one strange question: why does a rich country that keeps growing feel worse and worse?

## The Strange Numbers

Her newest study looked at 44 countries between 2006 and 2024. In the U.S. over that time, faith in government fell by about a third, and fewer Americans feel free to live how they want. Rich countries usually turn money growth into better lives. The U.S. does it worse than almost any other country — the researchers even have a name for it.

## Why Being Poor Hurts More in a Rich Country

Graham was born in Peru and started her career studying real poverty there. Her finding surprised many: poor people in Latin America are more hopeful than poor people in the U.S.

Why? In a rich country, being poor can feel like your own doing — especially in America, where the culture says anyone can get ahead by working hard. Fall behind, the story goes, and it is your own doing. Nobody mentions bad luck.

Even the famous old American success stories hide this. In the well-known novels about poor boys who rise, the boy always gets help from a rich friend at the key moment — a detail people forget when they tell the story again.

## China Saw It First

Graham first noticed the pattern in China during its fast-growth years. How happy people said they were fell hard even as the economy exploded — and far more people began killing themselves. Once the fastest, most shaking part of the change was over, that number fell back.

The American version is slower, but similar: strong growth for two decades, while the gap between rich and poor quietly grew until it began to poison how people feel. By 2024, only 12% of Democrats were happy with how the country is run.

## Hope Is a Skill

Hope is not the same as happiness, Graham found. In a study that followed 25,000 Australians for 14 years, people with more hope did better later in health, education and work — and bounced back faster from bad events.

Her research on deep sadness keeps returning to a hidden group: men in their best working years who have left the job market and don't appear in the usual numbers. Many live in their parents' homes, fight quiet battles, and see no path forward.

## Not Everyone Agrees

Other researchers push back. One top money expert argues Americans simply feel worse than facts support — pay for lower-paid workers has actually grown faster than for higher-paid ones. Another found the 2020 drop in happiness hit the educated and rich hardest.

Graham answers that simple survey questions hide the true range of feeling. Her bottom line: "Our narrative and our numbers are really out of touch with how people are living right now."
