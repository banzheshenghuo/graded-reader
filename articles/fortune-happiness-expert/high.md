# How a Close Look at the American Dream Turned a Top Happiness Expert into a Specialist in Despair: 'It's Harder to Be Poor in a Very Rich Country'

Graham is a leading fellow in economic studies at the Brookings Institution, a professor at the University of Maryland's School of Public Policy, and a senior scientist at Gallup, whose World Poll supplied the data for her latest paper. She started in development economics, as a visiting fellow at the World Bank and a special adviser at both the International Monetary Fund and the Inter-American Development Bank.

Her early books were about poverty and market reform in Latin America, but she has wrestled more and more with deeper questions of how societies make their people feel. You can see the shift in her book titles: Happiness Around the World: The Paradox of Happy Peasants and Miserable Millionaires (2009), Happiness for All? Unequal Hopes and Lives in Pursuit of the American Dream (2017), and The Power of Hope: How Wellbeing Science Can Save Us from Despair (2023).

"I've done a lot of hope and despair, or maybe despair and hope," she told Fortune. "I go between them so I don't go crazy."

She has served on a world-body expert group on measures of progress beyond GDP and on a National Academy of Sciences panel on well-being measures. As Fortune reached her, she was preparing to fly to Geneva to give a lecture titled "Hope, Despair, and Life Outcomes" — invited, she said, because "the young people, the graduate students were worried about despair."

## The Paradox of Unhappy Growth

Her latest paper, written with Douglas Harris, Anjana Nair and Isabel Shaheen O'Malley, applies that question to the United States. The authors used Gallup data from 44 middle- and high-income countries between 2006 and 2024. In the U.S. over that period, trust in government fell 31 percentage points, trust in the courts fell 24, and the share of Americans who feel free to choose how to live fell 19. The authors found that the U.S. turns income growth into well-being less efficiently than the average country — the most extreme case of what they call "the paradox of unhappy growth."

Graham's explanation starts in Latin America. "I was born in Peru and I worked initially on development economics," she said. "And I can tell you what real poverty is." That experience still shapes how she reads the American numbers. "Poor people in Latin America are much more hopeful than poor people in the U.S. And I've concluded that it's harder to be poor in a very rich country, where being poor marks you. And the American Dream story adds to that feeling, too."

She first spotted the pattern in China. In research published in World Development in 2017, she found that people said they were happy less and less — a drop "as sharp as a cliff" — during the early stages of the country's fast growth. "They were the fastest-growing country in the world and more and more people were taking their own lives," she said, recalling how happiness fell as hard as in the Great Recession or the COVID pandemic. The numbers improved once the most shaking part of China's change to markets was over.

The American version has played out more slowly. The U.S. has grown faster than nearly every other rich country for two decades, but inequality has kept "slowly creeping" up "to the point that it's corrosive." By 2024, only 12% of Democrats and 52% of Republicans were happy with the country's system of government.

## The Hidden Benefactor

Happiness for All? traced how unevenly hope is spread across America. The idea of the American Dream, Graham said, makes falling behind feel like a personal failure: "So if you fall behind and you're poor, it's your own doing, because you didn't work hard to get ahead. Nobody ever talks about bad luck."

She pointed to the founding myth of American self-making — the Horatio Alger stories of poor boys who rise on pluck and hard work. In Ragged Dick, Alger's best-known novel, a New York shoeshine boy gets his break when he saves a rich man's son from drowning. "Even the old famous Horatio Alger story, he had a benefactor," Graham said, "and they never talk about that when they tell the story."

The Brookings paper makes the same argument in scholarly terms: the U.S. has a "thinner social contract" than its peers, and the authors blame the drop in happiness partly on "a deeply planted culture of individualism and distrust of social support."

## The Science of Hope

That spread matters. In a study with Redzo Mujcic of Warwick Business School, Graham followed 25,000 Australian adults for 14 years. Those with high hope had better later outcomes in health, education and work. They were less shaken by bad events, and they came back faster and more fully.

Hope, Graham explained, is different from happiness and optimism: it carries an element of agency and what she called an "internal locus of control" — an inner sense of being in charge. People with more hope adjusted faster to bad shocks — "they were able to cope better with adversity" — and the effect turned out to be remarkably steady over time.

Her research on despair keeps returning to people the standard numbers barely see. Men in their best working years who have left the labor force are "not counted in the data we usually use. And they're in very bad shape." In a 2021 paper, Graham found such men were "very unhappy and very pessimistic" and turned up far more often than average among painkiller users. Many "still live in their parents' basements," she added — and without a college degree, the chances of finding good work are low, which also keeps them from moving to where the jobs are.

She links the rise of popular anger movements, at home and abroad, to that gap. "The macroeconomists go on and on and on about how strong the labor market is," she said, while for many Americans "their lives are so far from what the numbers are telling." The system, she argues, is "leaving large parts of society behind" — not penniless, but without hope.

## The Other Side

Not everyone reads the data that way. Philipp Carlsson-Szlezak, the top economist at Boston Consulting Group, has argued that the American mood is worse than American reality. In a September report, "The Kids Are Alright," he and his co-authors found that since 2020, pay for the lowest-earning quarter has grown faster than for the top in nearly every year. "Nothing is ever caused by one thing alone, but the biggest challenge is distorted expectations, in my view," he told Fortune.

Retired University of Chicago economist Sam Peltzman adds a wrinkle: in the General Social Survey, he found the 2020 crash in American happiness hit hardest among the most schooled and the richest. Graham notes that the GSS asks people to choose among only three answers, so "you lose a lot of the range" — Gallup's question about how happy people are uses a 0-to-10 scale.

On that scale, the Brookings data shows an America pulling apart: the share giving their lives 7 to 10 out of 10 barely moved, while the average fell faster. Many Americans "continue to be quite satisfied," the authors write, while "others are more and more unhappy."

"Our narrative and our numbers," Graham said, "are really out of touch with how people are living right now."
