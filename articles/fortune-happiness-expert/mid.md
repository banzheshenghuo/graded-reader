# The Happiness Expert Who Studies Despair and Hope

Carol Graham is a leading researcher at the Brookings think tank and a top scientist at Gallup, whose World Poll supplied the data for her latest paper. She started in development economics, at the World Bank and the International Monetary Fund.

Her early books were about poverty in Latin America, but she has moved toward bigger questions of how societies make their people feel — you can watch the shift in her book titles, from Happiness Around the World (2009) to The Power of Hope: How Wellbeing Science Can Save Us from Despair (2023). "I've done a lot of work on hope and despair, or maybe despair and hope," she told Fortune. "I go between them so I don't go crazy."

## The Paradox of Unhappy Growth

Her latest paper applies her question to the United States, using Gallup data from 44 countries between 2006 and 2024. In the U.S. over that period, faith in government fell 31 points, faith in the courts fell 24, and the share of Americans who feel free to live as they choose fell 19. The U.S., the authors found, turns income growth into a good life worse than the average country — the most extreme case of what they call "the paradox of unhappy growth."

Graham's explanation starts in Peru, where she was born and first studied real poverty. "Poor people in Latin America are much more hopeful than poor people in the U.S.," she said. Her conclusion: "It's harder to be poor in a very rich country, where it marks you" — and the American Dream story itself adds to that feeling, because it says anyone who falls behind simply didn't work hard enough.

She first saw the pattern in China: during the fastest growth in the world, people said they were happy less and less — as hard a fall as in the Great Recession — and more and more people took their own lives. Things improved once the most shaking part of the change was over.

The American version has moved more slowly. The U.S. has grown faster than nearly every other rich country for two decades, while the gap between rich and poor crept up until it began to poison how people feel. By 2024, only 12% of Democrats were happy with how the country is run.

## The Hidden Benefactor

The idea of the American Dream, Graham found, makes falling behind feel like your own doing: "Nobody ever talks about bad luck." Even the famous old Horatio Alger stories about poor boys rising through hard work hide a secret — in the best-known one, the boy gets his big chance after pulling a rich man's son out of the water, and the rich man gives him a job. "Even in the old famous story, he had a benefactor. And they never talk about that."

A new study with 25,000 Australians, followed for 14 years, adds the science: people with high hope did better later in health, education and work, and got over bad events faster — hope, unlike happiness, carries a feeling of being in charge of your own life.

## The Hidden Men

Graham's despair research keeps returning to prime-age men who have left the labor force — invisible in the usual statistics, and in very bad shape. Many "still live in their parents' basements," she said, and without a college degree the odds of finding good work are low.

She links the rise of popular anger movements to that gap between numbers and lives: "The macroeconomists go on and on about how strong the job market is," but for many Americans the numbers "are really out of touch with how people are living right now."

## The Other Side

Not everyone reads the data her way. BCG's top economist argues the American mood is simply worse than American reality — pay for the lowest-paid quarter has actually grown faster than for the top. And another researcher found the 2020 drop in happiness hit the schooled and rich hardest. Graham notes those surveys offer only three answer choices, hiding the full range of feeling.

On Gallup's 0-to-10 question, the picture is an America pulling apart: the share giving their lives 7-to-10 hardly moved, while the average fell — "many continue to be quite happy," the authors write, while "others are more and more unhappy."
