# Aisle Four

Noor had worked the night shift at the convenience store on Alder Road for eleven years, and she could tell what a customer was going to buy before the door closed behind him. Eleven years had done that to her without asking, and she never wrote it on any form.

The store belonged to a chain, which meant paper. The chain had a secretary named Mrs. Barrett who sent every notice twice, once by email and once by post, and who had never once stood in the shop after dark. She complained about the printer first, then the weather, and then her knees, always in that order, and the secretary kept the shop's paperwork in a notebook the way a good secretary keeps a diary for a boss who has forgotten how. She had a theory that a small shop was kept by its night staff and not its shelves, and she had been testing that theory on Noor for six years. When the chain sent a man with a briefcase to look at the store, Mrs. Barrett looked at the briefcase and said, to Noor, that the last man with a briefcase had lasted four months.

At eleven Noor did the round she had done since her first week. The door, the till, the small refrigerator behind the counter where the milk lived, then the drink refrigerator along the back wall, which had to hold its temperature all night. A small battery in the corner of the cold case wrote down every hour of it, and she changed that battery on the first of every month, whether it complained or not. She kept the old ones in a drawer under the till, which was not required and which she could not explain. A refrigerator that failed at two in the morning was the quiet nightmare of the job, the one she never said out loud, and she had thought about it more than once with her hand on the glass door.

The night customers were not the day customers, and half of them were foreign in one way or another. The first was always a foreign driver from the depot down the road, who came in for a snack and a coffee and, on Fridays, a second snack for the drive back, and who never once left Noor frustrated, though the self-checkout did, and the self-checkout always did, and a machine that would not read a wet label could frustrate anybody, including her, most nights. She had learned more foreign from that counter than from any course, and she had learned it a word at a time. The second was usually a nurse coming off a shift, who wanted the medicine aisle and a cream for her hands. Noor kept a prescription in the drawer for the nurse's mother, because the daughter could only collect it at three in the morning, and the pharmacy down the road was shut.

The chain sent its notices about the shelves, and Noor read them the way she read the old product encyclopedia under the counter, which was three years out of date and wrong about half the prices, and which she trusted anyway for the plain words. The encyclopedia had been wrong before and she still trusted it more than the chain's list, which was updated by people who had never worked a night shift. A purple sticker marked the shelf for the bread, a second purple marked the shelf for the coffee cream, and a third purple was stuck to the drawer where the guest prescriptions went. The purple meant nothing official. It was her own label, for the things she did not want to forget.

At one the chain's man came in with his evaluation form, which he called an evaluation of the night standard. His bias was toward numbers: how many baskets an hour, how long each customer waited, how often the till broke. The form had a bias built into it, and that bias favoured the store that looked like the other four hundred. Noor had a bias of her own, toward the customer who came back the next night, and no box on the form asked for that. Neither of them had numbers to settle it. They drank coffee from the machine, and then he asked for further changes anyway. There was one further thing, which was the sign above the door, and further down the page there was a line for her signature, so she signed it.

The first change was a tutorial. Every new night clerk would watch a short video about the till and then repeat the steps back to her, and the chain would keep a record of it. Noor had trained seven clerks in eleven years without a video, and all seven had learned the till in a night. She said so. The chain's man said the stores required consistency across four hundred branches, and that consistency was the whole point of the tutorial, and the consistency of the tutorial mattered more to him than the shop did. There was a constraint he did not say out loud, which was money, and she heard it anyway and let it pass. The constraint was not cruel. It came from the shape of the company, and it reached as far as the shelf in aisle four, and she had lived inside that constraint for six years.

By three the store is a different building. The lights hum, the cold case hums, and the only sounds are the door and the till. Noor takes her break standing up, with a snack and a coffee with cream, and she reads the label on the cream the way she reads the encyclopedia, for the plain words. The medicine in the drawer has changed its name twice since she started, and she has used the same medicine through both names, and the prescription has not changed at all, and the prescription is the only paper in that drawer she has never had to sign.

At five she makes the closing arrangements, and she writes her own evaluation of the shift in the book. She writes them on paper, the same way her mother wrote a list, and she leaves the arrangements for the morning girl on the counter, and the arrangements are the same as yesterday's and she likes that. The cold case gets its two lines in the book, the battery gets a date, the bread shelf gets a wipe. She puts the day's forms and the tutorial notes into her briefcase, which is older than the chain man's and holds more, and whose zip she has never fixed.

On the walk to the bus she thought about Mrs. Barrett's theory and the driver who said four words and the video she had not watched yet. A shop at night is a set of small arrangements that have to be made again every night. The consistency of it is not a personality. It is a decision she makes at eleven o'clock whether or not the week has been kind. She would watch the video before she complained about it, because a video could frustrate her for an hour and a complaint could last a month, and she would keep the purple sticker on the bread shelf either way. The nightmare of the dead cold case and the nightmare of the empty till would follow her into sleep, and she did not mind that, because she had checked the glass door twice.
