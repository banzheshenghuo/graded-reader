# The First Snow

The snow came early this year, before anyone was ready and before the arrangements of the week had been read out, on a Sunday night while we were asleep. The briefcase, which had spent the weekend by the door, was about to become a piece of furniture. I found it at six because Bobby found it first: he was standing in his bed, both hands on the rail, making a sound I had not heard from him before, a low, interested hum that did not depress him at all, only puzzled him. The window was white. The whole street was white. He turned to me with the expression of a man who has discovered that the world has a second version, and that somebody installed it during the night without asking him. Grandma, a secretary for thirty years and a secretary in retirement, had told us the snow was coming, in writing, on Tuesday, as part of the week's arrangements. Nobody had listened. She did not complain; she wrote it down.

Then he sneezed, and the day changed its plan, and the flat turned silent in the particular way flats do when someone small is ill. By eight the thermometer said thirty-eight and two, which is not high, but is high enough for a boy who has never had a fever before. He was warm and heavy and unusually quiet, and he wanted to be held in a particular way, upright against the left shoulder, facing the window. So that is where we spent the morning: me on the bed, him against my shoulder, the refrigerator humming in the way refrigerators hum when they are the only ones on duty, both of us watching the snow do the only thing snow does, which is fall. The frustration of a cancelled Monday never got a chance to form, and neither did mine; a snowed-in Monday asks nothing and therefore frustrates nobody. The prescription for the day was one line long, and I did not write it down.

Grandma arrived at nine with her emergency bag, which is a real bag with real contents: soup, bread, lemons, a new pair of underwear in a size he will grow into by spring, a snack for the boy and a snack for me, and a bottle of the good medicine from the pharmacy nearby. She took one look at his face and gave her evaluation, in the flat voice she saves for weather and illness: "Not much. Keep him warm, keep him quiet, and feed him soup." Then she went to the stove and did not come out of the kitchen for two hours, and the whole flat began to smell of the main dish she only makes in winter, which is a soup with too many ingredients and no name.

The snow kept coming, and it had the charm of all weather that nobody can argue with. From the third-floor window it covered the river first, then the gate of the little park, then the library roof, in that order, like a slow argument being won. By three the light through the glass was purple, the particular purple of snow at the end of the day, and the whole flat looked like a photograph of somebody else's life. I sent a photograph to my brother, who was on a platform somewhere waiting for a train and replied with three words: "I hate you." Fair. He has no window, and no fever to excuse him, and no soup.

Bobby slept from ten to twelve, which he never does, and woke up better and hungrier and slightly cross, which is the correct order of recovery. Outside the window the street had become foreign: the same cars, the same gate, everything reversed in colour and quiet. He ate half a bowl of the soup, a slice of bread with nothing on it, and then a whole yogurt, and Grandma declared the fever beaten on the evidence of appetite alone, then excused herself to wash the pot so that nobody could see how relieved she was. She has a theory about this, and she has another theory about boots, and both will be shared with anyone in the room; the first she has defended for forty years: a child's appetite is the only honest fever chart. She may be right; she has been right about most of the things she says in that voice.

In the afternoon we did the only honest thing available to a family in a small flat in the snow: nothing. I read to him from the library book — the one about the mountain, which he chose himself because of a bear on the cover, and which the librarian, our guide to the children's shelf, had recommended without being asked, and which I now know by heart in two languages, one of them invented, one of them native to this flat only. The bear in the book climbs, sleeps, and wakes up in spring, and every time the bear wakes up, Bobby makes the same gesture with both hands, which is his way of applauding a plot he already knows. The ending never stops surprising him. That is what a plot is for.

The concert was at four, on the radio, and it was the only thing the snow could not cancel. Grandma gave it her evaluation from the kitchen door, two words: "Too many strings." She stayed for all of it, and so did the encyclopedia, propped open at the letter S but unread, a book being patient in the way books are. A small orchestra, playing something with too many strings, broadcast from a hall we have never been to. Grandma turned it up louder than I would have, and said that in her occupation, which she still names that way, the concert season was the only thing that made winter bearable. She named a driver she used to know, who drove the orchestra's instruments from city to city; she mentioned his boots, and how he kept them; she said he was the most careful man she ever met, and she said the word careful as if it were a profession.

The fever came back at five, small, and I did not call it a setback. The cream was served at four as usual, one small scoop, the constraint of the house untouched by weather and unbothered by a fever; the medicine went down after the soup, and he held the spoon himself, which is his newest dignity. A fever that goes and comes back within a day is like a train that stops at a station and then continues: annoying, but on the route. We did the early night: bath, the good pajamas, the pants with the elastic that he can now pull up by himself if he concentrates, which he did, with tremendous seriousness, and then applauded himself.

By evening the snow had stopped and the street had been quietened into something that looked like a photograph of itself. Our neighbour came up to the door with a loaf of bread, because that is what neighbours do here in the first snow, and complained about the salt on the steps, addressing Grandma as madam, which she allowed, and did not leave until he had been given a bowl of the soup, which he ate standing up, which is how he eats everything.

I did the week's small accountancy at midnight, because the fever had settled and the boy was asleep and the flat was finally quiet. This is the part of the month when I count things. I counted the routes I had walked since the weather turned: the same four, in the same order, and I could not disagree with any of them; the app that counts steps is the only feature of my phone I still trust. I counted the hats by the door, the boots under the bench, the baggage in the hall, the underwear drawer, the floor I had swept twice. I thought about the driver Grandma mentioned, who knew every road to every concert hall in the province.

The week's reading had been thin. Two pages of a book about a mountain, read aloud in the invented language; one page of a workflow document I did not finish; a morning of the library with the boy, where the librarian, a frank woman with a graduation certificate on the wall and a flight timetable pinned over her desk, told us the snow route home and added, as advice, that we ought to walk the long way. I shared the walk with the boy in the carrier and the boy shared his snack with me. Late in the afternoon I thought about my occupation, which I name in the old way when I am tired, and about how the export of one's day into a shape somebody else can read is the only real work.

The numbers, when they were all in a row, said something I did not want to read. What I found was this: I had written nothing for eleven days, not one page of anything, and I had not missed it. That is the leak I did not want to look at, and I am the one who should have heard the drip. So I made a request of myself, in writing, on the back of the librarian's note: two words a day, no excuses about the snow. The account, when you read it honestly, says that the habit of working had been quietly replaced by the habit of being available, and that both feel like virtue from the inside. Two words a day, I decided, and no more, and no excuses about the snow.

The server in the corner did its small work all evening, and the kitchen guard did its small work, and the battery on the balcony sensor reported the cold falling and kept reporting it, hour after hour, a small prescription of numbers, and the refrigerator held its four degrees through a night that had nothing to do with four degrees. Consistency, at this point, is the house's only opinion about the weather. The one problem in the flat was a brand of cough syrup that Grandma does not trust, which she replaced with the one she does. Machines and grandmothers have this in common: they do not care whether the day was interesting. They keep the temperature, they count the doors, they insist on the good syrup.

At ten I looked in on him. He was on his back with both arms above his head, the way he sleeps when he is well; the briefcase was still by the door, untouched, and the tutorial I owe nobody at all could wait another night, and did, without complaint. The fever was gone and the snow was on the ground and the soup was in the pot and the whole business of the day had resolved itself into a small, warm nothing. I did not write anything. Two words, I said to myself, tomorrow. Two words is a start, and a start is not a promise to finish anything, and tonight that was exactly the amount of courage available to me and I took it.

Before I closed the curtains I wrote the day down in the notebook, two words, as requested of myself, and then a few more, because the habit once started is hard to stop at two. The battery numbers were on the same page as the fever numbers, which is unfair to both. The tutorial I owe nobody had grown a fifth page in my head. The cream was in the refrigerator, one scoop left in the tub, and the constraint of the house stood over it like a small flag. Consistency, I am learning, is not a personality; it is a set of small offices that somebody has to keep open. The bias of this family — toward warm rooms, toward soup, toward keeping the boy indoors — held for one more day, and the foreign weather outside did its foreign thing without asking anybody's permission.

The window at midnight was clear and cold and showed the street as a set of white rooms with no walls. Somewhere behind the buildings a river was doing what rivers do in this weather, which is to keep moving, quietly, under a lid. The boy slept. The stove was still warm. The encyclopedia stayed shut, and for once that felt less like a gap and more like a rest, which is a bias I am trying on.
