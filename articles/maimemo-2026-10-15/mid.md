# Swing Day

Thursday came in like a holiday. At breakfast, Grandma announced the arrangements twice, once for us and once for the door: swim class at four, the park at five, and after that, the swing. The swing. For three days Bobby had pointed at it from the far side of the fence like a man pointing at a postponed wedding, and today, finally, Thursday is Thursday, and the swing belongs to him. She had made the week's plan the way a secretary makes a seating chart, and she reserved the right to complain about the water temperature later, out of principle. The swimsuit hung on the radiator all morning, small and blue, the size of a letter.

The briefcase and I reached the office at nine; my calendar showed availability for one swim class at four, and until then I had one job and two requests. The scope of the morning was the console, which has an animation that plays when the numbers load; the animation had been frustrating everybody who watched it for longer than its own runtime. The response from the widget was slow, the script under it was older than the desk, and the whole thing ran unsupervised, like a small animal someone left in the walls. I touched up the script, merged two branches, integrated one new column into the report, upgraded a small dependency, and took the animation out entirely, which fixed the bug the way silence fixes a debate; the merge pipeline ran clean, for once. The bottleneck of the day was a review that took three hours and four people; the fix itself took eleven minutes. My colleague from the machine learning team sent a request for my platform numbers; the export ran fast, and I told him the platform is boring, which is the highest compliment in my line of work. He is fluent in three languages, has a neural model with intelligence of a kind and opinions of several, and none of this helps him with our ticketing system.

I left at three. The subway fare has not changed in years, which Grandma calls the one honest price in the city. My train started at the terminal near the office, the metro map on the wall confirmed two stops, and I got off at the platform by the pool, past the corner shop where the fish man nods to me now; unfortunately for him, I am still the man who buys nothing. A police car waited at the lights outside, and the officer gave Grandma's kind of nod to a boy on a scooter, and the boy sat up straighter immediately.

The swimming pool was warm and loud and smelled of clean. Our guide for the class was a foreign young man from Brazil, fluent in Chinese, with an accent that made every instruction sound like an invitation to a party. Grandma took one look at the water, took off her coat, and put on her purple swimming cap, the one she bought years ago for a trip that never happened. She had declared, all week, a bias against swimming pools in October, and brought it up every morning; her suggestion had been a warm bath at home instead. The bias lasted four minutes. She looked at herself once in the pool glass, approved, and put on the cap. "The water is a constraint I can live with," she reported. "Thirty-two degrees, and it holds that consistency all winter. I withdraw my complaint." The changing room was the building's only bottleneck, but the rules of this family are implicit, and Grandma walks past queues the way water walks past stones. The lifeguard, a serious man she addressed as "sir," weighed Bobby on the baby scale first, nine kilograms plus a swimsuit, and showed us the shallow end, which was also, today, the deep end of Bobby's courage.

Bobby sat at the edge in the arms of the guide and looked at the water the way he once looked at ice cream: a former enemy, now paying rent. Then he committed himself, all at once, leaning forward with his whole body, and the water took him in and carried him a small distance away, and for one second my heart stopped, and then he laughed, loud, his face going red with joy, and the lifeguard, who had taken one step toward us, took it back. No emergency. Only a boy discovering that water holds you up. He did not cry; he splashed. He kicked. He drank a little of the pool and made a face, because pool water is not salty, and he knows salty, and the difference frustrated him briefly. There was one floating toy, a red ball, and he shared it with a girl in a green swimsuit, generous for exactly one minute, which is a long minute at fourteen months; when she rolled it away, his face gave him away completely. The guide sang a song about a small fish, in Chinese, then in what I think was Portuguese, and the two languages sounded like cousins at a party. Bobby answered with his own, which is native to no country and understood by everyone in the water. His style is all shoulders.

Afterward: a warm shower, a towel the size of a tent, and the swimsuit, wet again, in a bag. He was sleepy before his hair was dry, the good sleepy of a body that has worked, like mine after the gym, and thirsty enough to drink half a bottle of warm water. He fell asleep in the stroller on the way to the park, slept through the whole walk, past the old landmark with the fish sign, under the trees with their red edges, and woke at the park gate like a small machine restarting, ready for the second half of his appointment.

The swing was free. That never happens. It stood there in the last of the light between two round lamps, and Bobby, who had practiced pointing at it for three days, pointed once more and got in, and I pushed, gently at first, then properly; at the top of the arc the chain bumps the frame with a small tick, and he braced for it like a drummer and his shoes pointed every way at once, and the sound he made was not a word in any language but needed no translation. Higher, said the shoes. Grandma supervised from a bench, hands folded, dress on, sneakers on, a secretary at her own event. The moon came up early and pale over the grass, one star next to it like a comma, and the park emptied slowly around us, and I understood something about the availability of Thursdays: the whole city is at dinner, and the swings are free, and no one knows this except babies and their fathers. Nobody drove us home; we walked, slowly, the ball under my arm.

Grandma sat on the bench with the encyclopedia, volume S, which had arrived that morning, one volume early again, because she has a relationship with the bookshop now and cannot be stopped. "The sea," she read to him later, at home, when he was clean and fed and in pajamas, "is salty." Bobby looked at her with deep respect. He had been in water today. The water had not been salty. The sea, therefore, was somewhere else, foreign, and worth thinking about. It was his bedtime story, and it was also, I realized, his first tutorial in geography.

The rest of the evening ran on rails, the order implicit, done and on time, and Grandma's arrangements held to the minute. Shrimp for dinner, three of them, counted and budgeted by Grandma, out of the refrigerator and into the pan, eaten by Bobby with deep suspicion and deeper appetite. Yogurt after, in the good bowl, the one with the bear on it. His bib retired early, too old for shrimp, he believes. I rinsed the swimsuit in the sink and hung it up, and the last of the prescription went down his throat, tongue out, professional to the end; the bottle goes to recycling, and the illness goes to the encyclopedia of things this family survived.

He found his instruments and played the day back: drum for the splash, bell for the swing, and the third of the instruments, the one with no name, for the song in two languages. Then the crib took him, and the tiger and the rabbit received him, and the house went quiet.

At eleven the kitchen guard sent its report, punctual as a tutorial that finally works. One anomaly, small: a door opening at 8:52, two minutes before we left for the pool, boolean and flagged. It was Grandma, checking that the swimsuit was really in the bag, which is the kind of anomaly a security system cannot understand and a son can. The encyclopedia is right, though: it was an emergency by her standards. The refrigerator in the purple kitchen held its four degrees all day with machine consistency; the gateway uploaded the night to the home server; the machine has no intelligence, which is why it is the most honest member of the family; and I, the second most honest, am sitting here with a cold tea, a sleeping house, one small scoop of cream, constraint observed, standing in the bowl as tomorrow's promise, and a briefcase by the door that contains nothing but a dry swimsuit and tomorrow's lunch.

Tomorrow is Friday. The guide said Bobby has a swimmer's shoulders. The swing will be free again at six.
