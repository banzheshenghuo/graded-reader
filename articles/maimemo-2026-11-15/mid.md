# The Camping Weekend

The camping weekend was Grandma's idea, which surprised everyone, including Grandma, who then spent a full month proving it was a good one. The arrangements of a woman who was once a secretary and remains one in her bones arrived in three stages: a plan, a revised plan, and a list of things that would go wrong and what we would do when they did. The briefcase stayed home; a suitcase of a different kind came instead, and it held nothing that needed answering. The tent was borrowed, the sleeping bags were those foil things that fold into the size of a fist, and the food was, in her words, "camp food, not restaurant food, and if anybody complains they can walk home." Nobody complained. The walk home was eleven kilometres.

We drove out on Saturday morning, and the boy fell asleep before the city was behind us, which is his way of endorsing a plan. The road went ahead of us for ninety kilometres of plain and field; the satnav, which I had exported to the phone in a fit of preparedness, told us to turn left into a lake. There is no worse feeling than a machine deriving its confidence from a map that stopped being true. The campsite was a field with a view of a lake and a sign that said, quietly, that fires were allowed in the metal rings only. Eight families, four tents with dogs, one family with a small tent that they had clearly bought online and never opened. Bobby woke at the gate and looked at the field with the expression of a small inspector arriving at a factory of grass.

Setting up a tent is a tutorial in humility, and the tutorial is not for beginners. The instructions were pictures. The pictures were wrong. We were three adults and one baby with a plush elephant, and we got it up in forty minutes, which frustrated nobody and surprised everybody, and which Grandma recorded in her notebook under the heading "speed, actual," followed by her evaluation of the process: acceptable, once. Our neighbour in the next field, a man with a better tent and a louder radio, watched us the whole time with the calm of the permanently superior. His exchange with Grandma over the fence was two sentences long and settled the whole weekend, and by lunchtime he had become a contributor to our salad, a man with a spare chair and a mall-sized radio that he kept, mercifully, at the volume of a conversation: he said something about the wind, and she said something about the wind, and after that they were allies.

The afternoon was the salad, the lake, and the wind. The salad was made on the tailgate of the car, with tomatoes and a dressing that Grandma would not name, and the first bite produced the only word in the boy's vocabulary that means anything on a picnic: yum. The wind put two leaves in my hair, which I found at bedtime. Somewhere behind us, a family with a guitar played a dance tune badly and happily; further off, a father gave his children a sightseeing tour of the lake's three rocks, with a commentary that lasted longer than the tour. Bobby met the lake the way he meets everything: hand out, then both hands, then the whole arm, then the knees. A foreign thing, cold, and worth turning one's head for, which he did, twice, to check that the lake was still there. The water was cold and his speech about it was one word long, repeated, with variations; the rest of the camp, to its credit, stayed calm, which is the only reasonable response to a baby's opinion of a lake. Grandma's speech was longer: she sat on a folding chair and explained, to anyone in earshot, that a lake is a pond with a better public relations team. Two people laughed. One of them was me; the other was our neighbour, who had by then become a contributor to the general mood.

At six the light did what the light does in a field, going purple at the rims of the clouds and then gold, and everybody stopped and looked, and nobody said anything clever, which is the correct response. We lit the metal ring, and the fire did its fire thing, and the camp went quiet in that way that camps do when the day has been long and good. The boy fell asleep on my arm before the fire was properly going. Grandma took him, and carried him into the tent without one complaint about his weight, which I noted, and she noted that I noted.

The night was the honest one. The prescription for the weekend, as written by Grandma and followed by all of us, was one page and no exceptions. The foil sleeping bags did their job with the consistency of foil, the torch battery lasted the whole night, and the tent zip held, which it did not deserve to. the air turned to the temperature the forecast had promised, and at 3:20 I woke because something was breathing near my head. It was a dog, a very polite dog, who had escaped a zip and was looking for company. We looked at each other for a while. Then I let him out, and he went home, and I did not sleep for the next hour thinking about the tent zip, which is the part of camping that nobody puts in the brochure. I excused myself from the second half of the night entirely.

Sunday morning: bread, eggs, the remains of the salad, a black pan over the fire, and a small boy in a purple fleece, painting. Breakfast went into the same bowls we use at home, which felt like a small betrayal of camping and a large comfort; the medicine of the morning, in Grandma's view, was coffee, and in mine it was watching the fire with my hands empty. Painting with what, you ask. With a brush that Grandma had, apparently, brought for exactly this purpose, and a small pot of paint that had been left by the campsite's own staff, and a sheet of paper from her notebook. He painted a red shape and a green shape, and declared it finished, and then painted his own arm, which is how you know the artist is serious. The arm stayed painted until Tuesday. The paper went home in the boot, carefully, with a book on top of it, and now it is on the refrigerator, in the purple kitchen, held by a magnet shaped like a fish, where anyone may please look at it, and where the answer to "what is it" is straightforward: a red shape, a green shape, and a Tuesday.

We packed up in the order that had been written down, which is the only reason we got out before noon, and the constraint of that list was that nobody argued with it, least of all me. The tent went down faster than it went up, which is the rule of tents. The camp field, when we left, looked exactly as it had when we arrived, minus one armful of grass, which had been pressed into a shape and taken home in a paper.

The drive back was silent for the first ten kilometres, because the boy was asleep and the adults were tired and the radio had no signal; a poor excuse, and an honest one. Then Grandma said, to nobody in particular, that this was the first time in a month she had spent two days without once looking at her phone. Silence, she said, is a luxury you have to go somewhere to find, and you have to know the spot. Bake it into the plan, she said, and I wrote it in the notebook, on the next page, under "important."

On the drive home, the official review happened, in the front seat, at the rate of one point per kilometre. The foreign part of the trip was over first, and it rankled: the field, the neighbour, the dog at 3:20, all strange again the moment we passed the city sign, which frustrated me more than the tent had. Grandma made her evaluation with a secretary's care: tent, good; fire, good; salad, acceptable; the wind, uninvited; the boy, excellent. My own evaluation was shorter and less useful. The medicine of two days in a field, I decided, is that it neither fixes anything nor needs to.

Then the small accountancies, the ones that keep a household consistent. The battery on the balcony sensor had held all weekend, and reported itself twice a day like a soldier, and the refrigerator in the purple kitchen had kept its four degrees without a single anomaly, which is the closest thing a machine has to loyalty — the encyclopedia of household virtue, it turns out, has very short entries and no adjectives. The constraint of my own week was the same as the family's: keep it small. The tutorial I owed the team had gone out on Friday, and I had not opened the briefcase once, which is either discipline or cowardice, and either way it kept the weekend clean; a boarding pass was all it had held since Friday, and a boarding pass is the most honest thing a briefcase can carry. The cream was eaten on Sunday at four, in a field, out of a plastic cup, honouring the arrangement to the letter. Even the bias of a man who prefers a desk to a tent got exactly what it deserved: a good sleep on the second night, and no argument.

Nobody has calibrated the week in a while, so let me report it. Six words a day for the last two days, a habit resumed after a long pause, the kind of pause that makes a man angry at himself in a quiet, unproductive way. The rate is slower than it was in September. The plan is not to catch up. The plan is to keep the habit small enough that it survives, the same lesson the tent taught: stand it up in forty minutes, take it down in twenty, leave it in the boot until the next field.

The prescription for the family, this month, is simple: keep it small, keep it warm, keep the materials cheap, keep the photograph on the refrigerator, and keep one scoop of cream in the plan as a bias, not a reward. The month has a week left. The week has a weekend in it, already spoken for. The boy's arm is clean again now, but he still points at the painting and makes the sound he made at the fire, and nobody in this house has ever pretended that is nothing.

The encyclopedia has reached N: the night sky, which is a chapter she skipped because it belongs to the field rather than the book, the sky being the native country of nobody and everybody. She was right that the field is a better teacher, and wrong that the chapter can wait, and right again that some pages you should read, then put down, then go outside and check for yourself. The night sky she saw at ten had a moon in it and eleven stars that she counted out loud. One of them, she said, was probably a plane.
