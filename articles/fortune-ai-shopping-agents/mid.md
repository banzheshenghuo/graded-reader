# Visa Tested AI Shopping Agents — and They Failed Nearly Half the Time

The results cover 22 AI programs across 15 trips. The programs powered by the newest AI models scored highest: Google's Gemini and Meta's version each succeeded 82% of the time. A Claude agent came in third at 76%, while an OpenAI agent finished 6th at 64%.

Visa's chart shows all 22 programs. One line shows the success rate; another shows the cost. (The five most expensive models succeeded 67% of the time on average, while the cheapest five scored 50%.)

## Why Is Shopping So Hard for AI?

The core problem is simple: the reasons behind any purchase are complicated, and many buyers don't know what they want.

Visa's test captured this reality. TV shoppers were given a list of needs — the right plugs for a game machine, a 30-day return window, a certain picture quality. As with real consumers, the shopper volunteered some information but held back the rest.

So for an AI program to succeed, it had to ask the right questions to draw out the missing details. Success meant buying a TV that matched the shopper's needs. Instead, many programs bought the wrong product or failed to suggest anything suitable.

"We're not there yet," said the head of growth. "But we'll soon get to a point where people can hand the job to AI."

## Beyond Product Discovery

Birwadker noted that agent-driven shopping is still young but advancing fast. Product discovery — the focus of Visa's test — is just one piece of this new commerce that retailers and AI companies are trying to figure out.

Companies also need safe payment systems. Payment companies are already building these.

There are also questions no one has answered: who pays when an AI program buys the wrong thing? Birwadker said these questions will get answered over time, just as earlier generations learned to trust ecommerce — though agent-based shopping will be a very different experience from online shopping today.
