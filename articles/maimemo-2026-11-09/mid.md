# The Shadow

November light comes in low, and low light makes long shadows, and long shadows, this week, made a discovery. It happened on Monday afternoon in the hallway, at the hour when the sun gets under the buildings and finds the one window we forget to curtain, sending a thin foreign light across the purple runner carpet. Grandma, who keeps the schedule of the sun the way a secretary keeps the schedule of a board, had predicted the hour and was not disappointed. Bobby was walking — new skill, two weeks old, still a little drunk with it — and something came with him along the floor, dark and long and shaped like a boy.

He stopped. The shadow stopped. He lifted one arm; the shadow lifted its arm. He turned to look behind him, and the shadow was there; he turned back, and it was gone, or rather, it had gone where shadows go. The arrangements of his face went from surprise to careful study, and then, in the correct order, to joy. He was meeting a foreign thing in his own hallway, and he was being polite about it. He had found a friend who does everything he does, at exactly the same rate, and who never once argues.

I sat on the floor and watched my own shadow do the same trick, longer than his, duller, and felt a small regret about how few things still follow me around. Grandma came to see what the laughing was about and stood in the doorway, and her shadow arrived first, stretching into the hall like a secretary arriving ahead of the schedule. The secretary of this family had come to inspect the audit, and found it acceptable. "Ah," she said, "the other one." She meant the shadow. She said it the way she says things that are obvious and therefore worth saying out loud.

For twenty minutes the three of us negotiated with light. Bobby chased his shadow to the end of the hall, where it folded itself into the wall and vanished — the first frustration of the afternoon, brief and real, and the kind that teaches — and then came back when he turned. He tried to pick it up. He tried to give it his spoon. He complained, twice, when it would not take the spoon, and the refusal frustrated him into a new idea: he put the spoon down and patted the floor instead. The medicine of the afternoon, in this house, is that a shadow has no appetite and no obligations; a hard lesson, and the day's real medicine, and he learned both facts in one sitting, which is a fast rate for anybody.

Grandma made her evaluation from the doorway, in her flat, final voice: "In my day we called that the dark twin and were told not to talk to it." Then she added, more quietly, that she had been four years old and had believed it, and had spent one whole summer walking backwards to see if it would walk in front of her. Her bias against folk wisdom has been steady ever since, and she defends it with the same energy she defends everything she has decided once and forever; she complained about her own childhood superstitions the way she complains about the price of pears, with affection and precision.

At five the sun moved on, the hallway lost its window hour, and the shadow went with it. Bobby looked for it. He checked behind himself, twice, then at the wall, then at me, and his evaluation was a small indignant sound that needed no translation, and his bias, which is toward things that stay where you put them, took its first real knock. I told him the truth as plainly as I could: the shadow would be back tomorrow, in a different hallway, at a different hour; the shadow does not keep appointments, but it never misses a day. He accepted this the way he accepts all constraints, which is to say, he sat down, thought about it, ate a banana, and asked, by pointing, whether there would be cream later, under the standing constraint of one scoop.

The kitchen guard had its own view of the week, and its view was flatter than ours. No anomaly for two days running: the doors counted and ordinary, the refrigerator holding four degrees with its professional calm, the battery — the new one, three weeks old — reporting itself healthy, and the fridge door opening at its usual rate, three times a day, all three of them mine. The encyclopedia reached L: the lions, which Grandma read twice, between the cream and the bath, once for the facts and once for the sounds, and which Bobby greeted by holding up his plush tiger for comparison, a mistake the tiger survived. The medicine shelf was full, and the evening report carried no anomaly, which is the report I like best.

In the evening, the tutorial I had promised the team — the one the encyclopedia of my trade will never contain — came back with one last comment, and the comment was a question, and the answer was a sentence, and the sentence is now the fourth page. My evaluation of the whole month of writing: four pages, mostly right. The briefcase stands by the door with that page in it and takes up no more room than it needs to, which is the only good lesson a briefcase has ever taught me; the consistency of that lesson is its whole value.

The evening audit, as usual, was small. The purple runner was straightened, its foreign light gone. The refrigerator was checked and thanked in silence, four degrees, the constraint of one scoop still posted on the door like a law nobody minds. The arrangements for Tuesday were confirmed in under a minute; the briefcase took its one page and closed; the battery reported healthy again, a small consistency worth having, and the cream, one scoop, went down at four with no negotiation and no tutorial required, his favorite medicine of the day. No anomaly. The tutorial stays at four pages, and my evaluation of it is: enough. The bias of this house runs toward things that answer when called, and tonight everything answered, including, in its way, the dark.

Tonight the hallway is dark in the ordinary way, with no sunlight to make anything of it. Bobby is asleep with one arm outside the blanket, the arm he lifted for the shadow, and I have checked it — twice — for no reason at all. Tomorrow the light will come in low again at four, and the friend will be back on the floor, and the most reliable thing in this house, more reliable than the refrigerator, more reliable than the guard or its battery, will do exactly what the boy does, one second late, all afternoon, for free, without frustration and without a plan.

Some consistencies are machines, and hum in the purple kitchen all night. Some are light, and visit at four in the afternoon. The encyclopedia files both under the same letter. The ones worth writing down are the ones that teach somebody something, and this month's teacher has been an inch of November sun on a hallway floor, showing a small person the first proof he has ever had that the world answers. It answers with a copy. It is not always the answer you wanted, and sometimes the copy folds into the wall and disappears, and the boy complains, and the shadow does not care. But it comes back. That is the arrangement, and it holds.
