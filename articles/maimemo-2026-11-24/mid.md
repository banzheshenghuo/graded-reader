# The Night Shift

Elena had worked the night shift on the fourth floor for eleven years, and she could read a chart before she read the name. She never wrote that talent on any form, and eleven years had done it to her without asking.

At seven she did the round she had done since the year she started. The kitchen refrigerator was first, then the medicine refrigerator, which had to hold its temperature, and a small battery in the wall unit wrote down every hour of it. If the battery died, the record died with it, and the hospital would ask questions nobody could answer. So she checked the battery on the first of every month, whether it complained or not. She kept the old ones in a drawer, which was not required and which she could not explain.

The ward had a secretary, Mrs. Okafor, who had run the front desk for twenty-two years and kept other people's lives in a notebook the way a good secretary keeps a diary for a boss who has forgotten how. She complained about the printer first, then the elevators, and her grandson came last, always in that order, and she kept a snack in her drawer for the nurses who forgot to eat. She had a theory that a ward was kept by its nurses and not its equipment, and she had been testing that theory on Elena for a decade. When a new doctor arrived with a briefcase and a plan, the secretary typed his name into the board twice, and Mrs. Okafor looked at the briefcase and said, to Elena, that the last man with a briefcase had lasted four months.

At ten a foreign patient came in from the language school down the road, and he asked for a snack from the kitchen before he asked for anything else. Elena gave him a snack and then a fever strip, and she read the prescription he had brought from home. He was twenty, and he had a fever, and he had no English for the word for it. This was the small nightmare of the job, a chart that looked like a document and could not be read. Elena did not let him see her frustration. She found the medicine on the shelf, checked the dose against the old encyclopedia, and drew a clock on a card with three hands, the way she wrote a prescription for someone who could not read one. The boy left with the card in his pocket and came back the next day with a bag of oranges. That side of foreign had nothing to do with papers, and it never once left her frustrated, though the paperwork itself would frustrate anyone. She had learned more foreign from her patients than from any course.

At two the resident came down with an evaluation form. His bias was toward numbers: how many vitals an hour, how long each patient waited at the desk. The form had a bias built into it, and the bias was toward the ward that looked like the other four hundred. Elena had a bias of her own, toward the patient who came back, and no box on the form asked for it. Neither of them had numbers to settle it. They agreed to disagree over a cup of tea, and then he asked for further changes anyway. There was one further thing, which was the sign above the door, and further down the page there was a line for her signature, so she signed it.

The first change was a tutorial, and the resident called it an evaluation of the ward's training in his report. Every new nurse would watch a short video about the ward and then repeat the steps back to her, and the hospital would keep a record of it. Elena had trained four new nurses in eleven years without a video, and all four had learned the ward in a morning. She said so. The resident said the hospital required consistency across four hundred wards, and that consistency was the whole point of the tutorial, and the consistency of the tutorial mattered more to him than the ward did. There was a constraint he did not say out loud, which was budget, and she heard it anyway and let it pass. The constraint was not cruel. It came from the shape of the hospital, and it reached all the way to the shelf, and she had lived with that constraint for eleven years, and it pressed on every choice that went through that door.

After the morning round she went to the library alcove and opened the old encyclopedia of medicine, which was older than she was and wrong in several places and which she trusted anyway for the plain words. A patient had asked about a cream for his hands, and the cream came in a purple tube on the third shelf. The purple meant nothing official. It was her own label, for the things she did not want to forget. She found the page and wrote two lines on a card for the patient. The medicine had changed its name twice since she started, and the shelf label had not, and the prescription pad had outlived three of the labels. The encyclopedia had been wrong before and she still trusted it more than the hospital's database, which was updated by people who had never stood behind a counter. Then she put the purple tube back and made a note to order more of the cream before the winter.

At six she locked the door and did the closing round, and she wrote her own evaluation of the night in the log. The medicine refrigerator at four degrees, the battery reporting, the monitor counted twice. She put the day's forms and the tutorial notes in the briefcase, which was hers and older than the resident's and held more. She left the arrangements for the next shift on the desk, and the arrangements were the same as yesterday's, and later she would make the arrangements for the weekend too, and she liked that. The monitor had crashed twice that week, and each crash had been a nightmare that ended before the morning. On the bus home the nightmare came back to her once more, the same crash, the same silence. On the bus she thought about Mrs. Okafor's theory and the boy with the clock card and the tutorial she had not watched yet. She decided to watch the video before she complained about it. Watching it would not mean she agreed with it, and she was old enough to know that.
