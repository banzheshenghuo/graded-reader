# The Purple App and the Broken Elevator

## Saturday: lions, monkeys, and one small concert

Last Saturday we took the baby to the city zoo. Several families from my wife's office were coming too, so I made the reservation on Thursday; the zoo asks groups to book ahead. We approached the gate slowly, and a woman in a green shirt checked our phones there, quick and exact, the way a good old secretary works. My wife read the guidebook like a novel the whole way in. "Madam," she said to my wife, "the baby is free." The baby, who understands free things better than we thought, clapped.

The zoo was a big field of small worlds. We started with the lions, asleep like old kings, and a sign near their gate said: QUIET, PLEASE. The zoo has a feeding protocol too. Grass goes to the rabbits; nothing at all goes to the monkeys. Near the bird pool, the birds swallowed their lunch in one move, and then came the monkeys, who were the show. They chased each other up and down, they chewed apples with their whole faces, and one small monkey sat by the window of his house and waved at the baby. The baby waved back. Two animals of the same age, I said to my wife, with the same theory of fun.

In the rabbit corner, the baby fed a rabbit one long piece of grass, and the rabbit, with great intelligence, pretended to think about it before eating. The sun was strong that day. Unfortunately, I forgot my hat; by noon my head was the color of the monkeys' food, and my wife wiped my face and said the word "sunburn" in a doctor's voice. At the shop we bought hats, at zoo prices, and a cold yogurt each. "Delicious," said the baby, with her whole body, and then most of her yogurt went to the field.

At three o'clock a small summer concert began on the grass near the bird pool, where the band had reserved the front part for families with small children: four instruments, one singer, a hundred families on blankets. The tune was simple and warm. The baby sat on my knees and did not move for thirty whole minutes, which was a record for her. Behind us, the swings moved softly in the wind; she is still too small for them, but she pointed and shouted her opinion. Four instruments, I thought, could represent the whole idea: small things, done together. Grandpa's old box of instruments came back to my mind; one day she will hear it live. At home, Bobby was probably chasing pigeons in his dreams.

## Saturday night: six floors

We came home tired and happy, with one small suitcase of zoo things: spare pants, one sock, the new hats. Unfortunately, part two was waiting. The elevator in our building was broken, and there was a paper on the wall saying SORRY, BACK ON MONDAY. So I carried the baby, my wife carried the bags, and we climbed six floors like a small tired team moving house. Even the small hotel of last weekend, with its slow balcony door, felt like a palace now. On the third floor the baby fell asleep on my shoulder, which helped nobody's arms.

At home, the bottles were already a small mountain in the sink. My wife started the bath; I washed the bottles, washed the baby's socks, washed my own sunburned head, and thought about nothing. That night, after standing practice on the living-room floor, the first small step toward the swings, the baby slept eleven hours, a quantity of sleep we did not know existed. Her old night protocol is history; the new prescription of our evenings is simple: bottles from the refrigerator, bath, bed.

## Sunday: a broken week of code

On Sunday morning our small luggage was still packed by the door, but work does not wait: I opened my laptop and found my week already broken too. I decrypted the week's error logs first, washed yesterday's cups in the sink, and read. The main dependency of my little project had died on Friday night; its owner had stopped caring, which happens in open source. The build was red, the users were writing in, and my choices were the usual trade-off: wait for someone else, or fork the repository and carry the weight myself.

I made coffee and committed myself to the fork. It was a quiet kind of work: read the old framework, understand its layout, fix the broken part, keep the rest alive. My theory of small code applies here too: change one thing at a time, and the architecture stays honest. I wrote a short specification for the fix, three lines, no more, then compiled, failed, compiled again in small batches, and by noon the build was green. The new workflow felt like the zoo path: one world, then the next. By evening I had deployed the fix, signed the new version, and pushed everything to the public repository. Moreover, the night check still runs unsupervised.

The afternoon brought one small link of good news: my newsletter had a story about a neural transformer that turns old family photos into short moving pictures. I tried it on a photo of Grandma and Grandpa, young, in front of their first refrigerator. The photo breathed. Grandma, on a video call later, watched it three times and said nothing, then: "Play the tune." The app cannot play that tune yet, but it is now, officially, a requirement.

My brother called at dinner. He had read my fix (he reads everything now) and used his nurse voice: "The dependency is your patient, not your baby. Do not marry the code." He also reported that the hospital wants a tutorial for the night nurses, and their budget has no extra cost for software, so the free version must be robust enough. "Send me the vector of what you need," he said, meaning a list; nurses talk about lists the way mathematicians do. On his way home he had brought Grandma's new prescription by hand, because the doctor had changed one pill and he wanted to explain it himself.

## Small Monday

On Monday the elevator came back to life, and the whole building said sorry to it in small happy voices. On the bus, I paid the fare with my phone; the fare had grown again, as fares do, and I took the aisle seat out of old habits from the train. A retired secretary next to me helped me fold the small chair; old hands know every trick. A man across us coughed twice and smiled at the baby; she studied him for a long moment, then waved. I waved too. The window showed the city going by, the same route as always, somehow new.

At work, my team lead approved the fork and put it in the cluster plan for next month. "Authentication stays as it is," he said. "Your credentials, your door." The fund for new servers is small this year, he added, so the fix must be efficient. It is, I said; the night light in the baby's room costs more to run. He laughed at that, which in our office is a signature.

Sunday's concert came back to me on the way home: four instruments, one tune, a hundred families on the grass, and one baby listening with her whole small body. Every app I want to build is somewhere in that picture. The elevator will break again; dependencies will die again; the sun will burn my silly head again. Moreover, there will always be monkeys. The baby has taught me the deepest constraint of all, and it stays implicit because nobody has to say it: things break. You wash the bottles, you carry what you love up six floors, and on Monday the elevator comes back.
