# The Zoo

The zoo happened on Saturday, and I am writing about it on Tuesday, because that is how weekends work: you spend them, then you pay for them in diary entries. Grandma's arrangements had held to the minute. Backpack, bottle, socks, camera, the encyclopedia's volume T for reference, a budget for cream as the first line of the plan, tickets printed by a retired secretary who trusts paper the way farmers trust rain. The briefcase stayed home, which is my own arrangement for Saturdays, the only one she delegates. Three days of planning sat in that bag, and the whole morning ran on it like a train on rails: gate at nine, elephants by ten, tigers before the crowds, home before the tears. She was, for one morning, the secretary of a very small and very loud company. At the gate she complained about the ticket price, out of principle, immediately, as promised, and paid it, as planned.

Bobby fell asleep in the stroller forty minutes before the tiger, which frustrated nobody but me. He woke at the exact fence. The real tiger was asleep too, flat as a rug, and Bobby looked at it, then at the toy tiger in his fist, then back, and his face gave the whole evaluation away: the book version was better. He cannot complain in words yet, so he complained with his whole face, and the sleeping tiger was the day's first anomaly, being the only animal off duty. We stood at the fence for a while anyway. The tiger had the good manners to breathe slowly, and Bobby matched his breathing to it, and for one quiet minute the two of them, big and small, were doing the same nothing together. "The encyclopedia does not sleep," Grandma admitted. "That is its unfair advantage."

The elephants frustrated us all by hiding behind a wall for twenty minutes; the wall was the constraint of the design, the elephant's arrangements did not include us, and a foreign family next to us narrated the wait in a language full of soft vowels, kindly letting us borrow their joy. When the elephant finally came out, gray and slow and enormous, Bobby stood up in the stroller and conducted it with one arm, and the elephant, professional, took its time. Grandma's evaluation was one word: worth it. She said it twice, which for her is a standing applause, and wrote nothing down, which means she plans to say it for weeks. Her bias against souvenir shops lasted until she saw the small plush elephant, and, well, the backpack came home heavier.

At the penguin pool, a keeper explained the cold room in the back: a refrigerator the size of a small garage, four degrees, checked every hour, holding its cold with the consistency of a promise. I felt my professional bias wake up, and I wanted to tell the keeper about the tutorial of my own tiny cold room, and stood there grinning like a man who builds small versions of big things. The keeper, fair man, gave my hobby a serious nod and a full professional evaluation: same job, he said, different size of elephant. I have been called worse. Our own refrigerator, the one in the purple kitchen, holds four degrees too, and I am aware that this sentence should embarrass me. It does not.

Ice cream at noon, under a purple umbrella: the budget held, the constraint held, one scoop of weekly medicine for Bobby and one for me, and he shared his with the plush elephant, which is generous to a fault. He fell asleep on my shoulder before the monkeys, and we carried him out past the flamingos and a foreign tour group, past the monkeys he never met and the giraffes he will meet next time, his battery fully spent, mine too. In the car he held the plush elephant with both arms and slept with his mouth open, and Grandma said the trip had gone exactly to plan, and then corrected herself: to plan, plus one elephant.

The past two evenings, the house has been quiet and the reports have been boring, which is the best kind. Monday looked like Sunday from the inside: porridge, park, bath, bed, the same small bells in the same small order, and nobody anywhere in a hurry to be anywhere else. No anomaly anywhere: the doors opened once each for milk and once for medicine, the sensors agree to the decimal, the consistency of a good week, and the battery flag sits in my notes where I left it, waiting for Sunday. The blog post about the leak is still a tutorial in my head, six steps and no sentences. A colleague asked for it on Monday; my briefcase went to the office and came home with the request still unanswered, which frustrated nobody and was also a kind of consistency. The secretary in me files it under someday; nobody complained, not even about Monday; my battery sits at half.

Grandma's purple swimming cap has retired for the season. The encyclopedia gained volume E on Monday, and the elephant page was read twice that night, in two voices, one local and one almost foreign, once for facts and once for memory, and Bobby patted the picture the way he once patted the pipe. He is learning that the world comes in two editions, one flat and one that hides behind walls, and both of them are worth the ticket. At bath time he made the elephant drink from the toy cup, then the tiger, then the rabbit, one round for the whole office, and Grandma watched from the door and said nothing at all, which in her language is a long and satisfied letter.

Tonight the house is warm and ordinary, my bias toward quiet Tuesdays holding nicely; the refrigerator keeps its four degrees, the medicine shelf is full and cold, no anomaly in two days, the briefcase by the door, arrangements for Saturday already forming in Grandma's head, my battery at half and charging. Tomorrow, if the week cooperates, we swim again. The guard will count the night, the tutorial will wait, and the cream, as always, is one scoop, not two, which by now is less a constraint than a promise we keep in this family, on schedule, with a small plastic spoon.
