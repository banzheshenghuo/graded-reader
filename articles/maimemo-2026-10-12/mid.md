# The Monday Doctor

Bobby woke us at five with a cry that was new to me. Not the hungry cry, not the tired cry. The stomachache cry. He lay on his back, both hands on his stomach, and his whole small body complained. I did the math in the dark: one first ice cream, one bowl of yogurt eaten like a man paying off a debt, one dessert made of cream. The source of the trouble was no mystery.

He refused the bottle and took three spoons of soft rice porridge, and not one spoon more; enough, he said with both hands. The bowl went back to the kitchen almost empty. His bib, usually a decoration, did honest work this morning. His sun hat from Sunday still hung on its hook, and I understood it would hang there all week. I searched the toy basket for the rabbit, his everyday partner; he pushed it away and dug the tiger out of the corner instead. When you are not well, you do not want your usual friends; you want the one with the loudest roar.

At seven I called Grandma, and at seven twenty she was at our door with her bag, her list, and her opinion. "Please, let me come," she said. "A clinic morning. I know the good one, the local landmark, famous for its children's window, up a short flight of stairs past the bread shop. They know me there." Twenty years as a secretary taught her everything about waiting rooms: which window moves, which hour is quiet, and how to arrive with every paper already in order.

The clinic opened at eight and was busy within minutes. The lobby smelled of soap and cold hands, with rows of chairs like a small airport hotel. Grandma took one look at the queue screen, walked past everyone to the far window, the right one, the one the whole room had decided was closed. It was not. Three minutes later she held a number, a paper sheet, and a seat by the window. "People always queue at the wrong bottleneck," she said. I translated Bobby's small gestures for her: hand on stomach, then on mouth, then on stomach again.

He was nervous, and I was scared, though only one of us showed it. He kept his eyes on the doctor's door and held the tiger by the ear. My knee wanted to jump; I made it wait. When the nurse smiled at us, Grandma patted my hand. "You look worse than he does," she said quietly. "You gave yourself away an hour ago."

While we waited, my bad habit woke up. The number screen refreshed every eleven seconds, and I caught myself wondering about the database behind it, the inputs, the server it talked to, the credentials it checks, the vector of Monday numbers, how the screen renders them, the throughput of a full waiting room, whether the clinic had ever upgraded the system or added one feature in all these years. The screen called number forty. I put the whole thought away. Unfortunately, today was not for that.

The doctor was an older man with a soft foreign accent that rounded every number into something kind. He greeted the tiger first, tapped its belly twice with two fingers, and wrote something pretend in the file. Bobby laughed for the first time that morning. It is a doctor's approach I would like to borrow for my day job: check the toy, win the patient.

Then it was Bobby's turn. On the scale: nine kilograms and a bite of breakfast. The doctor felt his head with the back of his hand, looked at the tongue, which said the opposite of alarming, and the four busy teeth. The cold circle of the stethoscope traveled around his small stomach, and the doctor listened with his eyes closed, like a man reading sheet music. He bumped a finger here and there, asked his questions short and specific, asked about the ice cream before I could mention it, and delivered the evaluation, straightforward as a receipt: "Too much party, too little sleep. Not an anomaly, a Sunday. The stomach wants consistency, not medicine."

His prescription was short. A bottle of something sour, a brand Grandma did not fully trust yet, half a spoon after meals, two days of soft food. "No milk tomorrow," he said, "and no ice cream for a week." In that soft foreign accent, the word week came out round and slow, like a full month. Unfortunately for Bobby, the sour bottle was waiting for him at home by noon. I dug through the kitchen drawer for the small spoon while Grandma held him. He swallowed the first half spoon with the face of a man tasting a lemon, one sleeve wet to the elbow. The second spoon frustrated us both, and then, because he is fair, he offered the spoon to the tiger.

The paper sheet from the visit was a small work of art, a clean schema of lines: weight as a clean integer, temperature as a clean integer, two medicines, one word I could not read. I rinsed the spoon at the sink and put the bottle high, far from small hands. Grandma folded the sheet into her bag with the care of a woman who has filed ten thousand papers. Our departure was slow: coat, tiger, shoes, and the queue number kept as a souvenir. At the door the doctor waved, the nurse gave Bobby a paper bear for bravery, and Bobby waved back with the tiger's paw.

We walked home through the wet street. A last bee of the season sat on the bread shop window, half asleep. Bobby pointed at the park gate, then at the empty swing. I shook my head. "The swing can wait," I said, and he accepted it, which surprised me more than the stomachache had. A small sick person has a soft kind of courage; they take the small pains without argument, for a day or two.

At home he slept like a closed book. Grandma sat next to him and did not read, did not knit, just watched, unsupervised and unpaid, which is a secretary's way of working. "I am here," she said. "Go, work." So I opened my laptop in the kitchen and tried to merge my morning with his nap. It went badly. I wrote one small fix, changed one variable, and started a push to the repository; he coughed twice, the push waited, and the morning slipped away. My approach to work did not survive contact with a sleeping child, and it frustrated me more than I want to say. The kitchen table, half laptop and half medicine, was the true bottleneck of the day, and the old sink dripped once and stopped, like the rest of us.

At four, the hour I had been awaiting, the phone made its small sound. The kitchen guard had something to say. Protocol two, the talkative one, now counts every door opening, and last night it counted three: 8:40, 8:52, and 9:03, each tagged with a small red flag, each an anomaly by the machine's strict standards, all context ignored. I knew all three. The first was the ice cream. The second was the yogurt Bobby was sure he deserved. The third was Grandma checking on the milk for tomorrow. The machine watched the whole party, unsupervised, and said nothing at the time. Some guard.

The night report also had the temperatures, flat as a table, every hour one gray pixel in line, the refrigerator sensor and the balcony one agreeing within their usual margin, consistency of the boring, healthy kind. I sent the sheet to Grandma. She read it once and wrote back one line: "The refrigerator behaves better than the child."

By evening Bobby was back. Not all the way back, but two thirds, still sleepy at the edges. He ate a whole bowl of soft noodles that were, by his face, delicious, and asked for more by pointing at the empty bowl and then at his mouth, a gesture clearer than most languages. He carried the tiger away to bed and set it next to the rabbit, arranged the way you arrange two colleagues who must now learn to share an office. Then he found his toy instruments, all three of them, gave the tiger a check-up with each of the instruments in turn, drum first, then the bell, humming a small tune while he worked, tapping the way the doctor had, twice, with two fingers, and nodding like a man who has seen this before. I posted a short video of the check-up for my brother, who replied with one word: "Promotion."

Before his bath he bumped his forehead against mine, gently, once. It hurt him more than me, I think, or maybe the other way. He is small and soft and, this week at least, a little vulnerable, and it turns out that watching a small person be brave about a sour spoon does something to a father's chest that no database has a field for.

Tomorrow: soft food, no milk, the prescription's sour bottle after breakfast. Grandma has a vision of a quiet soup week, has already called the restaurant to move Sunday's family table to a soup place, no shrimp for a while by doctor's order; moreover, she complained happily about the price of soup, inflation being what it is, for one full minute. The tiger sleeps on; the rabbit waits. Somewhere in the kitchen, protocol one counts the cold and protocol two counts the doors, and when morning comes, I hope they have nothing to say.
