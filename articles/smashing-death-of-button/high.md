# The Death of the Button: Why the Best Interface Is No Interface

For over forty years, software has depended on point-and-click interfaces. If you wanted to book a trip, buy shoes, or check a health symptom, you had to find your way through a maze of menus, sliders, multi-step forms, and pop-ups. We grew used to spending more time managing the software than reaching our goals. That deal is now changing. Thanks to AI and large language models, a new generation of web tools is built around human intent — what you want — instead of buttons.

## The "Ten-Click" Process

Think about buying a flight online. The experience is made to be hyper-interactive: you type your cities, pick dates from a calendar, set price limits, choose seats, and enter payment details across several pages. Want to compare two airlines? Open a new tab and start over. The computer sits there as a passive box of data, and the human does all the work. This has been the ruling idea of software design since the early graphical systems: the machine shows, the human drives.

Intent-driven design flips this. You state a goal — "find me a direct flight to Chicago next weekend under $400" — and the system does the searching, comparing, and booking steps for you. The screen stops being a dashboard of choices and becomes a conversation.

## Three Examples Replacing Buttons with Intent

1. **Perplexity AI: search without open tabs.** Old search engines were lists of links; you opened ten tabs to check one fact. Perplexity answers the whole question at once, with its sources shown.
2. **Vercel v0: web design without dragging boxes.** For years, building a site meant moving elements around in a design tool. v0 builds a working page from a plain-English request, one you can refine by talking.
3. **Goblin.tools: cutting big tasks into small steps.** Made for people who feel lost facing a big task — and popular with users on the autism spectrum — it takes "prepare dinner for guests" and breaks it into a clear, calm list.

## The Three Pillars of Intent-Driven Design

1. **High-level goals.** You express what you want, not how to get it.
2. **Generative pages.** The page you see is built for your question, not fixed in advance for everyone. Two users asking different things meet different pages.
3. **AI agents doing the work** — filling forms, comparing prices, writing drafts — while the person stays at the level of goals.

## The Hidden Risks

Taking away menus and forms feels freeing, but it creates new dangers.

**The illusion of control.** When a site decides for you, it can quickly feel patronizing — as if the software knows better than you do. Jakob Nielsen's classic rules say users should always feel in charge. Good design keeps the human in charge: show what the system is about to do, and make it easy to say no.

**The black-box problem.** In a traditional interface, when you get a strange result, you can usually find the cause: a wrong filter, a missed field. With an AI-driven system, you cannot see inside the machine's reasoning. Designers must show the steps the system took, not only the final answer — or users will stop trusting it, and one bad guess can end the relationship.

## From a UX Perspective

Web design is not dying; it is moving. Instead of drawing buttons and forms, designers will shape how machines ask questions, show their work, and step back. The old measures of success — engagement, time on page, clicks — will matter less when the goal is to spend less time inside the software at all.

We are entering an era where web tools are judged not by the beauty of their interfaces, but by how well they make those interfaces unnecessary. The future of the web is not more interaction — it is calm, invisible completion.
