# How AI Is Transforming Hurricane Forecasts

Hurricane forecasters are using AI more and more to make their predictions — and they say it is quickly changing their field.

When the first hurricane of the Atlantic season formed this month, an AI model beat traditional models at predicting where it would go.

In early October, Hurricane Isaias made landfall near Destin, Fla., as a Category 2 storm. Older models at first said it would head toward southeastern Louisiana. But Google DeepMind's AI program called it right away: the hurricane was going to hit the western Florida Panhandle.

"Google DeepMind, in particular, has proven itself these past few years as the gold standard," says Michael Lowry, a storm expert in Miami who has advised both FEMA and the National Hurricane Center. "That model has been, by far and away, the best performing computer model we've ever seen. Period."

The National Hurricane Center first started using the DeepMind program in its official reports last year. By the end of the 2025 season, it was already one of the best at predicting a storm's path and strength, says Brian McNoldy, who studies storms at the University of Miami. Usually, new programs take years to improve after first appearing.

"It's something we're not used to — to have a model, the new kid on the block, very quickly becoming the best model out there," McNoldy says. "This pretty much came out of the gate in first place, and that's a pretty impressive feat."

Traditional physics-based programs use live readings from the air — temperature, moisture, pressure and wind — gathered by satellites and planes, then use complex math to predict a storm's path and strength. An AI program works very differently: it depends on history, finding patterns by studying thousands of past storms and their weather data.

"The AI programs don't really know anything about the air beyond the current picture," Lowry says. "Beyond that, they don't use the laws of the air — the physics — to make predictions." Most of the people designing these programs aren't even weather experts: "They're software makers building brain-like programs" — sets of instructions modeled on the human brain and trained to find patterns in huge piles of data.

As a result, these programs can seem like a "black box," Lowry says: "We don't know why they might get a forecast right. We also don't know why they might get one wrong." It can be worrying to depend on a system you don't fully understand — especially when millions of people might be relying on your reports.

"It makes us a little uncomfortable, because we like to know why you're getting the right answer," McNoldy says. "And I feel like we don't — or at least I don't know why."

Not all AI programs have done well, though. Many untested weather AI programs exist. The two most trusted, despite some problems, are Google DeepMind and the U.K.-based European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts.

Forecasters at the Hurricane Center use several trusted programs — AI and older ones — when building their official reports. "Every storm has different challenges. That's why we take a mix-and-match approach and use the best of both," says NHC Director Michael Brennan.

And it's still people who read the results before deciding what to tell the public. The programs can show big changes between updates; forecasters turn those shifting predictions into clear advice people can act on. "That's what the human forecaster gives you: steady guidance," Brennan says. "We use our know-how to turn that forecast into real-world dangers, and that's what people need to watch for."

The DeepMind program still needs work: while it beat other programs in tracking Isaias, it didn't do as well as the NHC's mixed report in judging storm strength. Brennan's team sends regular notes to Google DeepMind to help improve it. Google didn't answer questions from NPR.

"The future of forecasting has always been changing," Brennan says. "It's never just one thing driving those improvements." Not long ago, forecasters couldn't predict storm strength even a couple of days ahead — that changed through better programs and data. Errors are about half what they were when Hurricane Katrina hit.

The exciting thing about AI, Lowry says, is the speed of improvement: updates come in months or weeks, unlike older programs updated once a year at best. "They are making progress in one year that would have taken us ten or more," he says.

But even with AI coming in fast and strong, Lowry isn't worried about AI taking people's jobs. "For me, I don't feel some deep fear about AI. If anything, I think it helps our work."
