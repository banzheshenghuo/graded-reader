# A Korean City Is Using American Style Reading Classes to Fight AI

"Is Frankenstein's monster human?" a teacher asks a class of children at a public primary school in Chuncheon, a city in South Korea. The children are talking about Mary Shelley's famous book.

"I think we can call it a human with no heart," one student says.

Then the talk turns to a newer question: is AI human?

"No," the student answers. People made AI, and although it is clever, it "cannot create new things."

## What Is the "Great Books" Way?

The school uses a method from St. John's College in Maryland, USA. In this method, teachers do not give talks. They only guide the talk. There are no special subjects to choose. Students do not look up answers on the internet. They use only their books, their own thinking, and talk with other students.

He likes this. "If only the teacher talks we can have one idea, but if we all talk together, we can get many ideas," he says.

## Two Problems It Tries to Fix

First, South Korean children used to be among the best readers in the world. But tests since the virus years show their reading has gone down.

Second, South Korea now uses AI in school a lot. Half of the students say they use AI chat tools to study at least once a week. There is also hard competition to get into good universities, so many families pay for extra classes, or send children to schools abroad.

"We all feel this system leaves students with no time to read and think," says Lee Yonghwa, a professor who started South Korea's first such program in 2019. Students are too busy learning facts for tests.

And now, he says, "Korean students are even losing their old skill of remembering facts, because they collect facts and ideas mainly from AI." If they cannot think alone, they cannot judge whether AI's answers are good.

## The City's Plan

The city's leader, Yook Dong-han, went through this system himself. "I felt its limits very closely," he said. "When I became mayor, I wanted to bring a different kind of education to the city."

Last year, the city made a deal with St. John's College to bring the method in. Now 120 university students and about 770 younger students take part, plus 40 teachers in training.

Students read Aristotle, Shakespeare, and Korean writers, including the Nobel winner Han Kang. In one high school, they read Martin Luther King Jr.'s famous letter from a prison in Birmingham, and talk about which laws are fair and which are not. They even linked it to their own country: in 2024, people and their leaders stopped the president from putting the country under army control.

It is a small start. But the city's leader calls it "a small but big change."
