# Why a South Korean City Is Turning to a U.S. College to Outsmart AI

CHUNCHEON, South Korea — "Is the Frankenstein monster human?" a teacher asks pupils at the Toegye public elementary school in Chuncheon, about 50 miles east of Seoul, as they discuss Mary Shelley's novel Frankenstein.

"I think we can call it a human being with no heart," says student Ma Yeonwoo.

The school uses the "Great Books" method from St. John's College in Annapolis, Maryland. Then the talk turns to a newer question: is AI human?

"No," Ma Yeonwoo answers, because people made AI, and although it is clever, it "cannot create new things."

## Teachers Guide, They Don't Lecture

In this method, teachers do not lecture: they only guide discussions. There are no majors or special subjects. Students do not look for answers on the internet; they rely on their readings, their own thinking, and talk with their peers.

That suits Ma Yeonwoo, who enjoys talking things over with other students. "If only the teacher talks we get one idea, but if we all talk together, we can get many ideas," he says.

## Two Trends It Addresses

This young education movement deals with several worrying trends. One is that world tests such as PISA show South Korean reading skills fell after the virus years — after decades in which South Korean and other East Asian students did better than American ones.

Another is South Korea's fast use of AI in schools. 51% of South Korean students say they use AI chat tools to study at least once a week, OECD figures show. Students also face intense competition to pass university entrance exams, which piles pressure on them from a very young age and prompts many families to pay for cram schools, or to send children abroad to escape the system.

"We all feel that this system leaves students with no time to read and think," because they are too busy learning facts to pass tests, says Lee Yonghwa, a professor at Incheon National University, where he co-founded South Korea's first "Great Books" program in 2019.

But now, Lee says sadly, "Korean students are even losing their old skill of remembering facts, because they get facts and ideas mainly from AI." And if they can't think for themselves, he adds, they can't judge how good AI's answers are.

## A Leader's Own Experience

The limits of the system motivate many South Koreans to look for alternatives, from study overseas to experiments at home. "As someone who went through this system, I felt those limitations keenly," Chuncheon Mayor Yook Dong-han said in an interview. "When I became mayor, I wanted to introduce a distinct educational philosophy to the city."

Last year the city made a deal with St. John's College to bring the method in. Now 120 university students and about 770 younger students take part, plus 40 teachers in training, Lee Yonghwa says.

Although the program is still very new, Yook calls it "a small but big change — a breakthrough that challenges old ways," and says student interest was greater than he expected.

## What They Read

Students read Aristotle, Shakespeare, Euclid, and Korean authors including Nobel laureate Han Kang. In one city high school, they read Martin Luther King Jr.'s 1963 Letter from Birmingham Jail, and discuss which laws are just and which are unjust — and which citizens should obey or disobey.

Lee says he was impressed that students linked King's letter to their own country — for example 2024, when people and their leaders stopped former President Yoon Suk Yeol from putting the country under army rule.

That fits the aim of the method, in which "each seminar table becomes a miniature republic, where ideas clash but people cooperate," St. John's College President J. Walter Sterling wrote last year. Through civic participation and liberal education, he adds, "we quietly stitch the fabric of our democracy before it unravels."
