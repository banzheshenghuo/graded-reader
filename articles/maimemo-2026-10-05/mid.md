# The Purple App and the Small Visitor

## A small visitor

Three weeks after the strange night, our family grew by one small person. My daughter was born on a sunny morning after a night of snow. She is very small, very loud, and very much in charge. We were nervous first parents, but Grandma had packed spare everything — bottles, socks, even spare pants for the baby.

Grandma came to help for two weeks. She arrived with one big suitcase and Bobby the dog, who chased the pigeons outside our building within five minutes. Grandma waved at the taxi driver and then waved at the baby. "Hello, little secretary," she said. "You will run this family soon." My wife laughed until she cried. Bobby chased his own tail in the hall, happy to be somewhere new.

My brother, the nurse, appeared on video the same evening. "Write everything down," he said. "Feeding times, sleep times, medicine times. New parents forget things. That is normal." He held up his notebook. "I understand babies better than code, but your app understands lists better than my notebook." So that night, after everyone slept, I opened my laptop and thought about a new door in the little purple app: a mode for new parents.

## The night protocol

A baby has her own protocol: food every three hours, day or night. At first, the protocol ran us; slowly, we learned to run it. Grandma took the middle of the night. "I raised two boys," she said. "One night protocol more or less will not kill me." Moreover, she sometimes forgot her own pills while she cared for the baby. The app noticed too — at nine it said, in its warm voice, "Grandma, your prescription." She plugged her phone in, turned her head to the screen and smiled. "All right, all right," she said. The system logged it: prescription, done. That week the baby's sleep quality went up too — Grandma's tunes work better than any machine.

Diapers became a new subject in our house. The quantity shocked me: eight, sometimes ten a day. "Buy the good ones," Grandma said. "Cheap quality hurts the small skin." I learned to change a diaper on the floor, on the bed, and once, almost blind in the dark, on my own desk — the desk where code used to live, now a small white island of diapers and milk bottles. Constraint is the true word for this new life: my work time broke into short pieces of forty minutes. But constraints, I found, can also make you specific. With one hour of quiet, I did more than in three loud ones.

That week I wrote a short specification for the parent mode: three buttons — FOOD, SLEEP, GROW. Nothing more. The architecture stayed simple on purpose. No login for grandparents; their faces are the credentials they carry, and the rest is implicit trust. At night the app runs unsupervised, like a nurse we cannot hire. The next night I built the validation for the forms, made the numbers whole integers only, and decrypted the day's error reports to check every corner again. A mistake in a baby app is not a small thing, so I read the anomaly reports twice a day now. At two in the morning, while the baby drank her milk, I made the commit and pushed it to the repository. Three hundred tenants share that little server, and soon some of them will hold small phones in small hands.

My brother read the code the next day. "Robust," he said, "like a good hospital floor." Then he objected to one thing: "Your battery use is heavy. New parents charge nothing — remember that. And add one line to your specification: be specific with small things, like nurses are." He was right. The old version was hungry; the new one is more efficient. It drinks less power than a night light, and its battery now lasts three nights. That morning the hospital front desk had sent her first check message, locked; I decrypted it for Grandma — the small sample from her foot was fine, her weight was up.

## Supermarket mathematics

On Friday, Grandma and I took the baby to the supermarket. It was the first time outside for the small one, and she wore a red handbag-sized hat that made strangers smile. Grandma walked the floors like a general. "Inflation," she said, holding up a small bag of rice. "Last year this was one dollar less. Now everything asks for more money and gives less quantity." She paid with cash from her wallet — cards are still, in her theory, for rich people — and counted the change like a retired secretary.

The bus fare had grown too. "Inflation everywhere," Grandma said, and the driver laughed and agreed. On the way home we stopped at the medicine store for the baby's vitamin drops; the app already knew what was fresh in our refrigerator, and Bobby, who had waited outside, barked twice at his own reflection. Probably, my wife says, he thinks it is another dog. I think the other dog thinks the same.

In the evening the baby clapped her hands for the first time — three small claps, at nothing, at everything. We all clapped back, and Grandma recorded it on my phone. She hums one soft tune while she cooks, an old song from her town, and now the baby turns her head to that tune faster than to any toy. Music, it seems, travels in the family. In our living room stands the old box of my grandpa's instruments; the baby has not seen it yet, but her feet jump when Grandma hums. One day I will open that box and let the instruments meet their new listener.

## Small conclusions

On Sunday my brother came by train to meet his niece. He carried a small suitcase and one one-way ticket again — "I stay till Monday," he said. Grandma cooked her Sunday roast: chicken, and a small steak for him, because he is still too thin. He took the baby in his arms with the calm hands of a nurse. "Handle the head, always," he taught us. "Support it, like a small warm egg." She held his finger and did not let go. "She derives comfort from warm hands," he said. "Remember that when your servers are cold."

He also brought news: the app now has four hundred families. Moreover, a contributor had left a long message in the repository — "Our twins have their own buttons now; this efficient little app saved our nights" — and someone had found, and quietly fixed, one leak in the old log route before I even saw it. "Open work," my brother said, "is a family too."

After dinner, Grandma excused herself and came back with a spoon. "For the baby," she said, "when she is ready." She gave herself away, as always: it is an implicit rule of the family — a spoon means come back. The bell animation rang across the screen — time for the small one's food. She understands nothing of protocols and credentials, I thought, and yet she understands everything that matters. The little bell represents all of it: care, repeated, on time.

That night, snow again outside, and the wind soft. I fed the baby her milk at three, wrote the numbers into the app, and looked at myself in the dark kitchen window — a tired, happy man. The nightmare weeks were long gone. The app had automated another small piece of our life. Somewhere down the hall, Grandma slept in the cozy room, her cough gone, her prescription finished, tomorrow's roast already waiting in the refrigerator, and Bobby chased pigeons in his dreams. My conclusion was small and warm: the best products are not about screens. They are about hands — old hands and new hands — and the tunes that hold them together.
