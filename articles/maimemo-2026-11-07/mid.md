# The Saturday Market

Saturday market begins at seven, and by seven-fifteen the street where it stands has already become a different country: tarps, crates, three languages of prices, and the smell of hot oil doing something illegal and delicious. Grandma arrives with her trolley and her arrangements, which on market days are written in a different ink, the flexible kind: buy the fish first, then the greens, then whatever the boy wants. The secretary of this family has been writing market lists for thirty years and has never once lost one. The briefcase stayed home, its opinions on Saturdays noted and ignored. A foreign country, this street, at seven-fifteen, and foreign it will stay until the tarps come down at noon. That last line is new, and it was added the day Bobby learned to point.

He rode in the trolley like a small captain, one hand on the rail, one hand free for pointing, and the market performed for him the way it performs for any visiting dignitary, which is what a secretary's grandson is. Fish on ice, silver and formal. Bread stacked like bricks. Radishes in bundles tied with red string, a vegetable with a gift wrap. He commented on all of it, loudly, in his own language, and the vendors, who have heard everything, answered him in theirs. This is the great conspiracy of market people: they will always answer a child, in any language, foreign or not.

At the vegetable stall, Grandma conducted one of her tutorials in shopping, which is really a tutorial in refusing politely. The tomatoes, she said, looking at them with the softness of a woman who has known better tomatoes, are from the greenhouse and taste of water. The man smiled, agreed, and sold her the good ones from under the counter. This negotiation has happened every Saturday for eleven years. Neither of them has ever complained about it, and neither is ever frustrated by it; it is the consistency that makes it a friendship.

Then came the line in the arrangements book that changed the morning, the newest one, in the flexible ink. "You may choose one thing," said Grandma, and opened the trolley's front basket like a small stage. Bobby took the responsibility with terrible seriousness. He looked at the radishes. He rejected the radishes. He looked at a pineapple, and his eyes went wide in a way that suggested a history with pineapples we do not know about, and then he was distracted by a woman selling honey, and by her bees' story, and then he saw the flowers.

He chose a single stem of yellow flowers. One stem, out of everything in the market — the fish, the radishes, the pineapples, the honey, the man selling hot oil — and the woman who sold them wrapped it in newspaper with the care of a jeweler, and Grandma's evaluation was a slow nod, and her bias, which she has never hidden, was confirmed: the boy has taste. He carried it himself the rest of the morning, upright, both hands, the way a person carries an opinion.

We went home through the fish smell and the bread smell, and he held the flowers above his head like a torch. At the door he handed them to his grandmother, without being asked, which is the first gift he has ever given anyone on purpose. She held them the way she held his face at the haircut: both hands, all the way. Then she complained, briefly, that she had no vase, and went and found one, and the yellow flowers stood on the kitchen table for the rest of the day, in water, in the purple kitchen, being looked at, the encyclopedia propped open beside them at random, as if the book also wanted to see.

The market's own consistency is the other kind of lesson. Every Saturday the same stalls, the same voices, the same prices that rise by a small amount each year and are complained about in the same order. The constraint of a market is that it can only happen on its day; the virtue of a market is that it happens on its day. My own calendar — the one on the refrigerator, the one Grandma turns without asking — is full of things that could happen any time and therefore happen never, which frustrates me every January and never once in between. The briefcase is the same: it has no market day, so it is always almost open. Its consistency is the consistency of a promise made to nobody. The market has no such problem.

In the afternoon the tutorial of the week finally reached its last step, and its fourth page was one sentence, and I sent the whole tutorial to the team, and the response came back in eleven minutes: good, ship it. The tutorial took a month and the review took eleven minutes, which is the medicine this trade runs on. My evaluation of eleven minutes: suspicious. My evaluation of the tutorial: it took a month, and it is four pages, and the fourth page is one sentence. The sentence is: ask the machine nothing you would not ask a barber.

The kitchen guard did its Saturday rounds and found its one anomaly of the week, at 3:40, which turned out to be the balcony door again, which turned out to be me, checking whether the rain was coming back. The refrigerator held four degrees, the consistency of a thing with no Saturdays. The battery, replaced three weeks ago, reported itself healthy with an honesty I found touching, and the constraint of the cream stood in the door like a small guard of its own. The medicine shelf was full. The cream sat in its corner of the refrigerator, waiting for its hour, the constraint of the house, unchallenged since October.

At six the flowers were still standing, in the purple vase in the purple kitchen, and Bobby had eaten his fish, and the tiger had been introduced to the flowers nose-first, and had opinions. Grandma made her evening rounds the way a secretary closes an office: the arrangements checked off, the encyclopedia back on its shelf at K, the medicine shelf counted, the battery before the last round of the night. Her bias for order met his bias for flowers, and they shook hands.

He handed out the day's evaluation in two parts. Part one: no frustration anywhere, which is rare and worth recording. Part two: one anomaly and one battery flag, both mine, both closed, the day book otherwise free of any anomaly at all — the door at 3:40, and the sensor battery, which was reported low and then turned out to be a thought I had not had yet. The cream was eaten at its hour; the briefcase took the news from the hallway without comment. Grandma read the encyclopedia's K page — the kangaroos, which she described, accurately, as "a pocket that hops" — and Bobby listened, and clapped at the end, the way the market claps a good vendor, the bias of the family toward applause being older than any book, which is now how all stories end in this house: with applause, for the story, for the evening, for the small yellow flowers in the vase, which he chose himself, out of everything in the world that was offered.
