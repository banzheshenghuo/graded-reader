# The Eternal Life of Gilgamesh

The Epic of Gilgamesh is among the most loved works of literature in the world today. But for most of its history, it was lost.

## Lost — and Saved by Fire

An empire fell in 612 BCE, and enemy armies burned its cities, among them Nineveh, the great capital where King Ashurbanipal had collected copies of every story he could find. The stories were written on clay in the cuneiform script, so the fires did not destroy them — they baked and preserved them. Even so, the falling buildings buried Mesopotamia's literature for over 2,000 years, until it was found again in the mid-19th century.

Europe had sent teams hoping to find support for the Bible's stories. They found the opposite. The old writings showed that some of the most famous Bible stories — the Fall of Man, the Great Flood, Noah's Ark — were later versions of far older tales. The digs changed world history: people now understood the Bible was not the oldest book in the world, and that earlier peoples had invented many things later peoples took credit for.

Among those inventions was the heroic epic — long credited to Homer in Greece, but really born in Mesopotamia. The Epic of Gilgamesh came to the modern world in 1849, found on clay pieces in the ruins of Nineveh's great library. The first eleven pieces tell the standard story; one more piece tells an older tale that does not fit, so most versions leave it out.

## Origins of the Story

The epic began as songs in Sumer, written down centuries after the real king it describes. A Babylonian writer, Shin-Leqi-Unninni, shaped the songs into one great work around 1300-1000 BCE — often called the world's first author known by name, though that honor really belongs to the poet-priestess Enheduanna, who wrote over a thousand years earlier. The researcher Samuel Noah Kramer noted that Shin-Leqi-Unninni did far more than copy old songs: he created something wholly new — the heroic epic.

The real Gilgamesh was a king of Uruk. Letters and inscriptions of his time give no reason to doubt he existed, and in 2003 a team even claimed to have found his tomb. His story grew so great that people came to see him as half god — but still able to die.

## The Story

The proud king is sent a strange gift: the wild man Enkidu, to challenge his strength and teach him humility. The two fight, become best friends, and win great battles together. But the gods demand a life in payment, and Enkidu dies.

In that moment Gilgamesh understands that he, too, will die — and the thought fills him with fear. "How can I rest? Despair is in my heart. What my brother is now, that shall I be when I am dead," he cries. So he sets out to find Utnapishtim, the one man who survived the great flood and was granted eternal life.

After traveling across the Waters of Death, Gilgamesh hears Utnapishtim tell the flood story — a boat, animals, a family saved — an older cousin of Noah's story. He fails two final tests: he cannot stay awake six nights, and a snake eats the plant of youth. He returns home and writes down his story.

## The Ending That Wins

Gilgamesh never won a life without end for his body. But by writing his story, he won it anyway: every reading is another win over death. As the scholar Gwendolyn Leick notes, he made himself immortal through his city's greatest invention — writing.
