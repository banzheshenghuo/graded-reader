# The Eternal Life of Gilgamesh

The Epic of Gilgamesh is among the most popular stories in the world today and has moved numberless readers — but for the greater part of its history, it was lost.

The Assyrian Empire fell to a coalition of Babylonians and Medes in 612 BCE, who sacked and burned the Assyrian cities, among them Nineveh. Nineveh was the great capital where Ashurbanipal had built his library, which held copies of every written work he could find in Mesopotamia. As these works were pressed into cuneiform writing on clay, the fires that ate the library did nothing to the clay but bake it and keep it better. Even so, the falling buildings buried the written works of Mesopotamia under them for over 2,000 years until they were found again in the mid-19th century.

## A Dig Meant to Prove the Bible

At that time, European societies, museums and governments sent diggers to Mesopotamia hoping to find physical proof for the narratives in the Bible. This mattered especially because Charles Darwin's work, published in 1859, was spreading and people were questioning the Bible's historical authority. What they discovered was the exact opposite of what they were sent to find: the cuneiform texts they brought to light showed that some of the most famous stories in the Bible — the Fall of Man, the Great Flood, and Noah's Ark — were later versions of far older Sumerian myths.

The digs changed world history: it was now understood that the Bible was not the oldest book in the world, that peoples had grown strong for thousands of years before the Bible's date for the world's beginning, and that these earlier peoples had created many of the tools, beliefs and story forms later credited to others. Among those forms was the heroic epic — long credited to Homer in Greece but now understood as a Mesopotamian invention. The modern world found the Epic of Gilgamesh in 1849, in the ruins of the Library of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh, written in the Akkadian tongue. The first eleven tablets tell the standard version; a 12th tells an older Sumerian tale that does not agree with the rest, so most versions leave it out.

## Origins of the Story

The epic was first a series of Sumerian poems, later translated into Akkadian and written down some 700-1000 years after the reign of the real king of Uruk it describes. It was first known as Sha-naqba-imru ("He Who Saw The Deep"). The author, Shin-Leqi-Unninni — a Babylonian word-keeper writing around 1300-1000 BCE — is often called the first author in the world known by name, though that honor really belongs to the poet-priestess Enheduanna (circa 2300 BCE). The researcher Samuel Noah Kramer pointed out that Shin-Leqi-Unninni did far more than copy an earlier work; he created something wholly new from older sources — the heroic epic. As N. K. Sandars wrote, it is "the finest surviving epic poem from any period until the appearance of Homer's Iliad; and it is far, far older."

The hero was the half-legendary King of Uruk who, the poem says, cut down the great trees of the Cedar Forest with his friend Enkidu and journeyed far to find the secret of eternal life from the old wise man Utanapishtim. Letters and stone records of his deeds give no reason to doubt such a man existed — and in 2003, a team of diggers claimed to have found his tomb in the old bed of the Euphrates river. His deeds were so great that the man became half god in story: son of the priest-king Lugalbanda and the goddess Ninsun, strong beyond measure but still able to die.

## The Realization of Death

According to the epic, the proud king was sent a strange gift from the gods: the wild man Enkidu, who would test his strength and teach him to be humble. Enkidu is tamed, brought to Uruk, challenges Gilgamesh, loses — and the two swear eternal friendship, with Gilgamesh's mother taking Enkidu as her own.

After they beat the demon-monster Humbaba in the Cedar Forest, and the Bull of Heaven soon after, the gods call for the death of Enkidu: someone must pay for such deeds. Enkidu dies — and in that moment Gilgamesh understands that he, too, will die. The knowledge torments him. He cries out:

> How can I rest, how can I be at peace? Despair is in my heart. What my brother is now, that shall I be when I am dead. Because I am afraid of death I will go as best I can to find Utnapishtim whom they call the Faraway, for he has entered the assembly of the gods.

## The Quest for Eternal Life

After a journey across the Land of Night and the Waters of Death, Gilgamesh finds Utanapishtim, the only human to live through the Great Flood and then be given eternal life. Utanapishtim tells how he was warned of the coming flood, built an ark, filled it with animals, and saved his family and humanity itself — a far older cousin of Noah's story. Then comes the test: stay awake for six days. Gilgamesh fails; sleep takes him. He fails again with a magic plant of youth — a snake eats it, which is why snakes shed their skins and live on.

Having failed, Gilgamesh is brought home to Uruk, where he writes down his great adventure. The researcher D. Brendan Nagle calls the poem "a mirror of Mesopotamia's own heroic fight to hold off decay and leave a name for itself." And Sandars adds: "If Gilgamesh is not the first human hero, he is the first tragic hero of whom anything is known. He is at once the most sympathetic to us, and most typical of individual man in his search for life and understanding."

While the king failed in his quest — and is known to history only through lists and inscriptions — he lives on through the work of Shin-Leqi-Unninni and the nameless word-keepers who carried the tale from age to age. Gilgamesh, as Gwendolyn Leick notes, "became immortal by adding to the greatness of his city by using the city's greatest invention: writing."

Through the written word — his pride, his grief for his lost friend, his fear of death, his quest — the great king does conquer death, and wins his immortality each time his tale is read.
