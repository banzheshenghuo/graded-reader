# Who's Who in a Pirate Crew

It was all very well pocketing other people's gold and singing at drink parties, but life aboard such a ship involved a surprising amount of hard work. These men were sailors first, and in the great pirate years (1690–1730) a ship needed real skill and repairs all the time. They had to be excellent seamen to catch fleeing trade ships and shake off the navy ships sent after them. From captain to the youngest hand, each man played his part in the complex dance of tasks that kept an 18th-century sailing ship working.

## The Captain

The captain was very often the criminal brains of the whole operation. He might also have been the man who put up the money to buy the ship, or an officer who had once attacked enemy ships for a government.

Even among cut-throats, a captain was often elected — it was hard to lead by force men who put their liberty above all else. Many had turned to piracy exactly because of the harsh rules of the merchant and naval world, so they would not accept a leader who ruled by the stick alone. Still, the strongest captains could set real discipline: Bartholomew Roberts (1682–1722) wrote a rule into the ship's articles — no card games for money — which every man swore to follow. Nor was the position safe — men who had risen up once were not shy to do it again. Charles Vane (d. 1721) was removed for failing to attack a ship as his crew wished, and replaced by 'Calico Jack' Rackham (d. 1720).

Pirate captains often dressed the part. Blackbeard (Edward Teach, d. 1718) went for fear itself: a great black beard with slow-burning fire-cords under his hat. Roberts wore fine cloth and a diamond necklace. For his skill at leading men and his sheer charisma, the captain earned a double share of all they took. On the other hand, if the pirates were caught, the captain was almost sure to hang — and the most famous law-breakers might have their dead bodies hung in chains for years, like Captain Kidd in 1701.

## The Quartermaster

The second man — a job found only on such ships — was usually the deputy leader, and below him came the first and second mates. Like the captain, he could be voted in or out of office. He watched the man at the wheel, cared about the ship's course, recorded its speed, and usually led the boarding party onto a target ship himself. After a ship was taken, he decided what was worth taking and divided it fairly among the crew — his word was trusted. His share was double that of an ordinary seaman, the same as the captain's. When the crew voted a punishment for one of their own, it was the quartermaster who delivered it. And when a captain took a second ship, the quartermaster was the natural choice to lead it.

## The Navigator

A ship might carry a full-time chart man. Though captains often knew some of the art, a specialist was preferred: he knew the charts, coastlines, currents, winds, and local weather. Longitude could not yet be measured well, so long experience was a great advantage. With one, a captain could arrive exactly where rich trade ships were sure to pass, and find dangerous shallows and islands — perfect places to set an ambush. Since few men knew this mysterious art, he was often simply called the Artist.

## The Boatswain and the Mates

The deck master — 'bosun' — ran all the work of sailing, and answered for the stores, the ropes, and the sails. He received one and a half shares of any booty.

Crews had no formal ranks, but some men clearly stood above others in knowledge and experience, and they led the daily work. A foremast man directed a team of five or six. The coxswain held the wheel and led any small boats — useful for going ashore, exploring shallows, and boarding other vessels. Officers received one and a quarter shares.

## The Surgeon

Having a doctor on board was an advantage — but it depended on whether one could be forced to join. If not, a medicine chest was the best a captain could do against illness and injury. Without a doctor, the ship's repairman was called upon to perform such drastic operations as cutting off limbs.

## The Carpenter

Any sailing ship needed a good repairman: to keep the vessel sound, stay on top of water in the hold, and make the running repairs caused by wind, sea, and enemy fire. Aboard such a ship he also remade taken vessels — heavy top decks came off to cut weight, extra gun ports were opened in the hull, and inside walls removed to make room for more cannons. No wonder that when a trading ship was taken, its skilled repairman was very often pressed into the crew, and had to wave goodbye to a much longer, honest life. He received one and a half shares.

## The Cooper

A barrel-maker sailed only on larger vessels, but he was very useful: his barrels were the only large containers of the age, holding dry stores, water, drink, and powder for guns. He kept them tight against air and water, and could break up empty ones to make room.

## The Sailmaker

Sails were worn down by weather, ripped by storms, and sometimes torn to pieces by grapeshot. In the age of sail, a crew without a sailmaker could not sail far. And without a doctor, he was the one who stitched up wounds from battle and from the many accidents on board.

## The Gunner

The ship's gun teams were led by their master, who shouted the order "Fire!", helped by a second man. Each cannon needed four to six men. Experience was needed to measure the right amount of gunpowder to send different shots the right distance — with both ship and target moving. Fortunately, few captains wanted to sink a target, for fear of hurting its precious goods; but if a trading ship fought back after the Jolly Roger went up, or a navy ship came out to hunt them, good shooting was an obvious advantage. Cannon crews were not full-time: between battles they did other duties. The gunner received one and a half shares.

## The Cook

These men enjoyed food taken from captured ships and meals in the shore-side hangouts of their trade, but someone still had to cook at sea. The cook was often a sailor who had lost a limb — a fact Robert Louis Stevenson did not miss: in Treasure Island (1883), Long John Silver, we are told, once served as a cook before he turned pirate.

## The Musicians

Players of the fiddle and trumpet sailed with every crew. They set the rhythm for heavy work like pulling ropes, added to the fearful noise of an attack — and played, on request, for any man who felt like being entertained.

## Where Pirates Came From

Most crews were made up of men who had risen against their captains, or of men taken and forced to join to stay alive — the most common defence heard in court. The taken included ship's boys working as apprentices. Others came by choice: sailors worn out by hard work, harsh rules, and poor pay on merchant and navy ships; Newfoundland fishermen escaping brutal conditions; workers in hot, sickness-ridden wood factories, keen for fresh air and a fortune. Captains found men in pirate havens such as Tortuga, Providence Island, and Port Royal. Crews of the Caribbean included men of every nation — most were British or from the Americas, but Black Africans, whether taken and forced, escaped from plantations, or free volunteers, were a large part of many crews; some, the trial records show, learnt the trade and became equal members.

## Joining the Crew

To qualify as the newest hand, a man needed to manage sails (letting them out and rolling them up), to hold the wheel, and to measure how deep the water was; being good with a weapon was a clear plus, as was not being too particular about one's company. Youth mattered too: a hard life at sea was a young man's game, few pirates were over forty, and most were in their twenties.

## The Good and the Bad

The benefits: better food, better pay — perhaps years of wages in one capture — plenty of alcohol, freedom and adventure, and a real voice, since key decisions and the sharing of booty followed democratic lines. The disadvantages: a very real risk of prison, hanging, or serious injury. The average career of a pirate was around two years — and once turned pirate, a man would most likely never see his family again.
