# Who Does What on a Pirate Ship

We picture pirates pocketing gold and drinking rum. But life on such a ship involved a surprising amount of hard work. These men were sailors first, and in the great pirate years (1690–1730) a ship needed great skill to sail and repairs all the time just to keep going. From captain to the youngest hand, each man played his part.

## The Captain

The captain was often the criminal brains of the operation. In a surprise, the crew usually chose him — you cannot rule with a stick over men who put freedom above everything. Many had run away from navy ships and trade ships, where rules were hard and punishments cruel. Still, a successful captain who brought in big prizes could set rules: Bartholomew Roberts even wrote a no-card-games rule into the ship's articles, which every man swore to follow. And the job was never safe: Charles Vane was removed for not attacking a ship, and 'Calico Jack' Rackham took his place.

Captains also dressed to be noticed: Blackbeard went for fear; Roberts for beauty. For all this, the captain earned a double share of all they took. But if the pirates were caught, the captain was almost sure to hang — the most famous ones, like Captain Kidd in 1701, had their bodies hung in chains for years.

## The Quartermaster

The quartermaster was the second-in-command, chosen like the captain. He led the attack when a ship was taken, decided what was worth taking, and divided it fairly — the men believed him. He also carried out punishments when the crew voted for one. His share: double, same as the captain's.

## The Navigator

A good map-reader knew maps, coasts, and winds. In those days ships could not measure their position east and west, so long experience mattered more than instruments. With one, the captain could wait exactly where rich trade ships had to pass. Few men knew this secret art, so he was often simply called the Artist.

## The Boatswain and the Mates

The boatswain — 'bosun' — ran all the work of sails and ropes, and kept the ship's stores in order. No man had a fixed place, but experienced men led the others: one older hand led five or six, and the coxswain held the wheel and led the small boats. Officers got one and a quarter shares; the bosun, one and a half.

## The Doctor Problem

Having a doctor on board was a real advantage — but many doctors only "joined" because they were forced. Without one, the best a captain could do was carry a box of medicines. In emergencies, the ship's carpenter performed operations as serious as taking off a leg.

## The Carpenter and the Cooper

The ship's repairman never rested: the ship always let in water, he made running repairs all the time, and he changed taken ships — cutting extra gun ports and making them lighter and faster. Naturally, when such a ship was taken, its repairman was usually forced into the crew, and had to say goodbye to a longer, honest life. He earned one and a half shares.

The barrel-maker made and repaired barrels — the only large containers of that age — keeping them tight for water and gunpowder, and breaking up empty ones to free space.

## The Sailmaker, the Gunner, and the Cook

Sails wore out in weather and storms, so a sailmaker was a man no ship could sail without. When there was no doctor, he was the one who closed wounds after battle.

The cannon master led the gun teams — four to six men per gun — shouting "Fire!" Measuring gunpowder correctly, on a moving ship at a moving target, took real skill. Pirates rarely wanted to sink a target: the goods had to survive. The gunner also received one and a half shares.

And the cook? He was often a sailor who had lost an arm or a leg — which is how Robert Louis Stevenson gives Long John Silver a past as a ship's cook in his famous book.

## Who Became a Pirate?

Some were mutineers; many more were taken from captured ships and joined to stay alive — the most common defence in court. Others were simply worn down: poor pay and hard rules on navy and trade ships, brutal conditions for fishermen and wood workers far from home. Pirates were picked up at sea-towns like Tortuga and Port Royal, and crews of the Caribbean included men of all nations — including Black Africans, some taken by force, some free; a few learnt the trade and became equal members of the crew.

The entry test: work the sails, hold the wheel — and not ask too many questions about the men with you. Age mattered: most pirates were in their twenties; few lived this life past forty.

## The Good and the Bad

The good side: better food, maybe years of pay in one lucky day, plenty of alcohol, freedom, and a real vote in the ship's decisions. The price: prison, the rope, or a serious wound. An average pirate life lasted about two years — and most who turned pirate never saw their families again.
