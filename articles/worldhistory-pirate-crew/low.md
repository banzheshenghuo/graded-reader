# Who Does What on a Pirate Ship

We think of these sea robbers as men who only take and steal. But life on such a ship was full of hard work. The men were sailors first. Between 1690 and 1730, their best years, a ship needed real skill to sail and repairs all the time. Every man on board had a job.

## The Captain

The captain was often the clever criminal behind the whole operation. And here is a surprise: the men usually chose him — and could choose someone else later. Many had run away from navy ships and trade ships, where life was hard and punishment was cruel. They did not want another boss with a stick. But a captain who brought in big prizes could set rules. If he failed, the men voted him out: one captain, Charles Vane, lost his job because he did not attack a ship when the men wanted.

A successful captain got a double share of everything the ship took. But if the pirates were caught, the captain could be sure of the worst: almost all of them were hanged.

## The Man Who Divided the Gold

Next to the captain stood his second man. He led the attack when the ship took another ship, and then he divided the gold — and the men believed him to make it fair. He got a double share too, like the captain.

## No Doctor on Board

A doctor was very useful, but hard to get: many "joined" only because they were forced. Without a doctor, the man who repaired the ship had to do the cutting — even taking off a man's leg at sea.

That man himself had a full-time job: the ship always let in water, broke, or burned, and he made it well again. He got one and a half shares.

## The Cook

The cook often was a sailor who had lost a leg in battle. The writer Robert Louis Stevenson knew this: in his well-known book about pirates, the famous Long John Silver was a cook.

## A Young Man's Game

Who became one of them? Some had turned against their own captains. Some were taken from taken ships and joined to stay alive. Many were just worn out: worn out by hard work, low pay, and cruel captains on normal ships.

The rules were simple: be young (most pirates were in their twenties), know how to work the sails, and don't ask too many questions about the men with you.

The good sides: better food, better pay, strong drinks, and a free life — the men voted on big decisions. The bad side: prison, the rope, or a lost leg. This life lasted about two years. Most never saw their families again.
