# The Deep End

Lena had run the weekend shift at the community pool for twelve years, and she could tell what a swimmer needed before the swimmer reached the ladder. Twelve years had done that to her without asking, and she never wrote it on any form.

The pool belonged to the council, which meant paper. The council had a secretary named Marta who sent the rosters twice, once by email and once by post, and who had never once stood on the pool deck after dark. She complained about the printer first, then the rain, then her knees, always in that order, and she kept the safety log in a notebook the way a good secretary keeps a diary for a boss who has forgotten how. She had a theory that a pool was kept by its lifeguards and not its chlorine, and she had been testing that theory on Lena for nine years. When the captain arrived with a briefcase and a new rulebook, Marta looked at the briefcase and said, to Lena, that the last man with a briefcase had lasted three months, and that the secretary before him had lasted nine years.

At seven Lena did the round she had done since her first week. The lifeguard chairs, the first-aid room, then the small refrigerator in the break room, which held the water bottles and the after-sun cream and had to stay cold all day, because the cream melted at thirty degrees. A battery in the corner of the equipment shelf wrote down the temperature every hour, and she changed that battery on the first of every month, whether it complained or not. She kept the old ones in a drawer under the radio, which was not required and which she could not explain. A refrigerator that failed on a hot Saturday was the quiet nightmare of the job, and the locker nightmare was the loud one. and she had thought about it more than once with her hand on the door.

The morning crowd was always the same: the old men who swam lengths, the mothers with children, and the foreign tourists who read the rules twice and still did float in the deep end. That side of foreign never once left Lena frustrated, though the sign-poster did, because the words were too small, and a sign that no one could read could frustrate anybody, including the father who shouted at her in English. The rulebook could frustrate anyone on a hot afternoon. and the English too simple for the words they actually needed. She had learned more foreign from that pool than from any course. She kept a snack in her pocket for the child who always arrived without one, and she shared it without being asked, and she gave the mother a second snack for the ride home.

The captain came down at ten with his evaluation form, which he called an evaluation of the weekend safety standard. His bias was toward numbers: how many rescues, how many complaints, how often the first-aid kit was opened. The form had a bias built into it, and the bias favoured the pool that looked like the other six. Lena had a bias of her own, toward the swimmer who came back the following weekend, and no box on the form asked for that. Neither of them had numbers to settle it. They drank tea and agreed to disagree, and then he asked for further changes anyway. There was one further thing, which was the sign above the door, and further down the page there was a line for her signature, so she signed it.

The first change was a tutorial. Every new lifeguard would watch a short video about the chairs and then repeat the steps back to her, and the council would keep a record of it. Lena had trained eight lifeguards in twelve years without a video, and all eight had learned the chairs in a morning. She said so. The captain said the pools required consistency across six centres, and that consistency was the whole point of the tutorial, and the consistency of the tutorial mattered more to him than the pool did. There was a constraint he did not say out loud, which was money, and she heard it anyway and let it pass. The constraint was not cruel. It came from the shape of the council, and it reached as far as the snack shelf, and she had lived inside that constraint for nine years.

At twelve she took her break and sat on the highest chair with a book from the lost-and-found. The book was a water-damaged encyclopedia of first aid, which was older than the pool and wrong about two of its pages, and she kept that encyclopedia in the break-room box. and which she trusted anyway for the plain words. The encyclopedia had been wrong before and she still trusted it more than the captain's new rulebook, which was written by people who had never held a rescue tube. A purple float marked the deep-end line, a second purple was taped to the first-aid box, and a third purple was stuck to the locker where she kept the spare batteries. The purple meant nothing official. It was her own label, for the things she did not want to forget.

By three the crowd thinned out and the pool turned into a long length of blue light. She sat with the radio on her knee and watched the battery level, and she put the cream on her arms before she forgot. The radio had a prescription for a new part she had been meaning to fit for a month, and she kept the prescription in the same drawer as the spare batteries, because both were things she meant to do on the first fine day of spring, and the prescription was the only paper in that drawer she had never signed. because both were things she meant to do on the first fine day of spring. The medicine in the first-aid kit had changed its name twice since she started, and the pads had not, and the medicine was the only thing in that kit she had never had to sign for. The medicine stayed in the kit through every name change.

At five she made the end plans, and she wrote her own evaluation of the day in the log. She wrote them on paper, the same way Marta did, and she left the arrangements for the morning lifeguard on the notice board, and the arrangements were the same as yesterday's and she liked that. The refrigerator got its two lines in the log, the battery got a date, and the float got a clean. She put the day's forms and the tutorial notes into her briefcase, which was older than the captain's and held more, and whose handle she had never fixed.

On the walk to the car she thought about Marta's theory and the child who had learned to say "deep end" in three languages and the video she had not watched yet. A pool is a set of small arrangements that have to be made again every weekend. The consistency of it is not a personality. It is a decision she makes at seven o'clock whether or not the week has been kind. She would watch the video before she complained about it, and she would keep the purple float on the deep-end line either way. The nightmare of a forgotten locker would follow her into sleep, and she did not mind that, because she had checked the list twice.
