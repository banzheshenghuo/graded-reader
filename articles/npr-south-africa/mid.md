# South Africa 'feels like a prison' for women after a series of killings

Elizabeth Moselakgomo, a 38-year-old manager and mother, loved running in her neighborhood near the Johannesburg airport. Last week she went out for her usual afternoon run — and never returned.

Her 8-year-old daughter called relatives, and they called the police. Five days later, police found her body. Her face had been hit so hard that nobody could recognise her.

Police say she was one of nine women found killed in the same area of Johannesburg in a few weeks. The majority of the women were sexually assaulted.

The killings have shocked South Africans — although the country already has some of the highest rates of violence against women in the world. Police statistics say more than 100 rape cases are reported every day, and about 15 women are killed daily.

Police have not ruled out the possibility that one killer is responsible. They have warned women to be very careful and not to walk or run alone. Many women are angry at this advice. They say protecting women is the police's job — women should not have to change how they live.

President Cyril Ramaphosa has promised that the police will do everything to find the killer or killers. But many people say the government has not done enough. In 2025, the government declared violence against women a national disaster.

"Women in South Africa continue to be hunted and denied the most basic right to life and security," said Shenilla Mohamed of Amnesty International. "Women are tired of empty words. We want to know what will be done to make women and children feel safe."

Women marched in the streets in Kempton Park, the suburb where many of the killings happened, and also in Cape Town. "Enough is enough," they said.

Elizabeth loved running, so running clubs across the country are holding runs in her memory and in memory of all the victims. At one run in Johannesburg, people of all ages wore black and stood in silence for a minute before they started.

"You go for a run, you can be killed. You go to the post office, you can be killed. Even in your own home, you can be killed," said Shari Maluluke, 26. "It makes me angry that I cannot move freely. My whole country feels like a prison."

All the women NPR spoke to said they did not feel safe. Didi Tsitsi, 31, described the precautions women take: "You send your location, you share your Ubers."

Why is the killing of women such a big problem in South Africa? "The government and the police have failed," Tsitsi said. "There is also a deep problem in society about what men are brought up to be." And, she added, South Africa has never really dealt with the legacy of apartheid.
