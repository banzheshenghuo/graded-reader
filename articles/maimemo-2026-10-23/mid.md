# Debug Friday

The clap arrived on Friday morning, out of nowhere, both hands at once, a small round sound of applause for nothing in particular. Bobby clapped at his porridge, clapped at the tiger, clapped at the floor. Grandma, the secretary of this family, its historian and its filing system, worked out the source later, and it was the pool: the foreign family clapped for him on Thursday when he crossed the shallow end, and he did not clap back, being mid-crossing, but he filed it. Twenty hours later, the file opened. That is how he learns everything: the world performs, he takes notes, and the conclusion arrives on its own schedule, usually before breakfast, usually with both hands.

The briefcase went to the office carrying nothing but a bug report, and Friday was debug day, my favorite kind of day and nobody else's, a small weekly frustration for the whole floor. A bug had been living in the transformer project since Tuesday, eating one number in a hundred and making the downstream graphs politely wrong. The cost of it was small in money and large in trust; two teams, each with its bias toward blaming the other, had frustrated themselves for two days, which is how bugs survive. I read the logs the way Grandma reads a receipt: line by line, with care. By eleven I had it: a feature flag, flipped on Tuesday, turned off Wednesday, but never fully gone, like a guest who leaves his coat so he can come back.

The fix was a tutorial of three lines, written at last like a man keeping a promise. The commit was small. The tag on the version read v4, works, which is a family joke that escaped into my job. The constraint all along was honesty, not tricks. My evaluation of the whole adventure: the bug was honest, it left a trail, and it cost us a day and a half of pointing. The transformer service itself is fine, has no intelligence, learns nothing, and tells the truth about electricity, which is more than I can say for most software and some people.

Grandma called at noon with her own report. The encyclopedia has reached G: giraffes, the ones Bobby never met at the zoo because he was asleep on my shoulder. She read him the giraffe page in the morning, and he patted the picture, and she has concluded, speaking as the secretary of common sense, that the giraffe is "a horse built by a committee." She also complained, briefly and with love, about the cost of the good pears this week, straight from the refrigerator into her bag, a brand she switched to in June and now defends like family, which is the softest kind of bias. Her prescription for Friday is consistency itself: walk, water, and one small scoop of cream after dinner, the same medicine in the same order since June. The constraint of the house stands, and the house stands on the constraint.

At five we walked to the park, the whole family and the plush elephant, who has been promoted from toy to passenger. Bobby walked most of it himself, past a foreign couple and their pram, clapping at dogs as they passed, and one small dog, complaining in its way about being applauded at by a stranger, clapped back, tail going like a metronome. The sky at the edges had gone purple. At the swing he pointed, I pushed, and at the top of the arc he clapped mid-air, which is new, briefly bad for my heart, and completely his style.

He found the instruments when we got home and played them with his feet for the first time, the drum under one dirty sneaker, the bell somehow balanced on the other, a one-boy band with an applause habit. His battery ran out exactly at bath time, which is the machine in him: he goes from full concert to full stop with no warning light.

The kitchen guard's evening report was its usual small poem, its weekly evaluation one word, boring: the refrigerator holding four degrees in the purple kitchen with machine consistency, no intelligence anywhere in the design, the doors counted, the medicine shelf checked, the night uploaded, the battery, still flagged, still holding, and one anomaly of the week crossed off the list, because the machine that watches the house has no idea that the boy in the house has learned to clap. Some things you do not need a sensor for. You need a mother, or a grandmother, or a very small audience at the edge of a swimming pool.

Tomorrow is Saturday, and the arrangements are already forming in someone's head, the way arrangements do in this family, in ink, in advance, and out loud; the briefcase is by the door, light and off duty; the encyclopedia stands at G with the whole alphabet to go; the giraffe page will be read again, twice, once for facts and once for memory; the cream is budgeted. The bug is dead, the tutorial is filed, all sentences at last, the tag is on the wall, and the boy, who cannot say a word yet, has learned to say well done.
