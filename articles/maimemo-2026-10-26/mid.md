# The Long Weekend

Some weekends are two days long, and some, by the quiet agreement of everyone in the house, are three. Ours was three. It began on Saturday the way all our Saturdays begin, with arrangements made by a secretary who retired from an office but not from command: breakfast at eight, market at nine, park at four, and the whole day, between those two ends, left open, no constraint at all, cream budgeted, the briefcase left by the door, for whatever the day itself wanted.

What the day wanted, it turned out, was the aquarium. Not the plan, but the suggestion of a small wet hand pointing at a poster of a fish from a foreign sea, and we have learned, as a family, to lose these arguments without frustration; a tutorial in pointing, taught by a fourteen-month-old with excellent taste. The encyclopedia's fish page could wait; the fish themselves could not. The aquarium is one long purple-blue hallway of glass, and Bobby walked it the way a bishop walks a church, slow, nodding, clapping at the fish he liked best. The jellyfish got the loudest clap of the morning, and confirmed our bias. The penguins, swimming underwater like small flying men, got two, and his battery was still full of applause at the exit, which he complained about, briefly, by falling asleep against it.

His evaluation of the day, delivered by falling asleep against my shoulder before the exit sign: worth it. Grandma's evaluation was written in the arrangements book, a real book with a real pen, kept by the secretary of this family: "Saturday, aquarium, spontaneous, recommend." A foreign family asked her to take their photo in front of the shark tank, and she framed them like a professional and complained, gently, about their camera, her bias against machines with minds of their own being standing policy, which is her way of blessing people.

Sunday was for repairing the damage of Saturday: the purple towels washed, quiet hours, the refrigerator cleaned and loaded, and the quiet complaint of a house that has been happy and needs to be cleaned. I changed the battery in the balcony sensor at last, a small promise kept on a slow day, and the kitchen guard, rebooted, came back online with its boring consistency, counting the doors of a family that mostly stayed home. Not one anomaly all Sunday, unless you count a battery lasting as long as it did, which the machine, modestly, did not. The encyclopedia reached H: horses, the ones Bobby knows from the park carousel. "The horse," Grandma read, twice, "was the first animal to carry a person." Bobby patted his plush elephant, out of sympathy, I think, for a colleague's history.

Monday, the third day of the weekend, the one nobody plans, was for the medicine cabinet, the shopping list, and my briefcase, cream on the list twice, which had been standing by the door since Friday like a dog that knows its walk is coming. Returning slowly is its own medicine. Back at work, the week greeted me with a tutorial nobody had written and a bug nobody had filed, frustrating nobody but themselves, and I fixed both before noon, each fix a tutorial of one line, out of pure holiday strength, the consistency of rest. The evaluation of my machine learning colleague, who has opinions the way a fountain has water: "You are annoyingly relaxed." The constraint of a good holiday is exactly that, I find: you come back with more patience than the job can use.

At home in the evening, the report arrived, right on time: the refrigerator holding four degrees in the purple kitchen, the milk cold, the battery new and quiet, the cream waiting for its one small scoop under the standing constraint, and no anomaly anywhere, the consistency of machines in a house of people, unless you count a family that spent three days together and liked it, which the machine does not have a column for, and which I would not file under anomaly anyway. I would file it under medicine.

The briefcase sleeps by the door tonight, walked at last. Bobby went to bed early and unwillingly, the frustration of a boy undone by his own long weekend, asking for an encore, the one foreign word we all understand, with the tiger, the rabbit, and the elephant in their row, and the house settled around them. Tomorrow's arrangements are already spoken for, and the encyclopedia stands at H with the rest of the alphabet coming. Tomorrow is Tuesday, and the long weekend is over, and the calendar, turned forward at last by the secretary's own hand, agrees. The bias in me says long weekends are lost time, cream and all. The boy's new clap says otherwise. The refrigerator says four degrees. Everyone I love is accounting for themselves, and the week can start whenever it's ready.
